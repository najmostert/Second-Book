# The shape of the story

A working map of the book's underlying journey. This is a **guide, not a plan to obey** — nothing here should ever be announced to the reader. If a reader can see the shape, we've done it wrong.

Everything below is drawn from what is already written. Nothing is invented. Where something is uncertain it is written as a question.

---

## The two questions

**On the surface:** *Where are you from?* — asked outright in the preface.

**Underneath:** *Who am I?* — never asked directly, but it is the one the book is actually about.

**Confirmed 2026-07-26:** the second question does **not** get a clean answer, and we are not going to invent one. Ireland is not the destination. The ending is a woman who has stopped needing the answer to be tidy.

---

## Why the question exists at all

The question is not a whim — the book shows exactly where it comes from, in the first two chapters.

Uganda is a whole world with a place in it. Then it is taken away. And when the family arrives in London, the MP tells Mum, *"You people make me sick!"* and she writes: *"I think I have just met apartheid in reverse."* Cape Town then greets them with **"Go home British pigs!"** in pink paint.

So: thrown out of the place she lived, refused by the place she was supposedly from. That is the wound the whole book grows out of. Thirteen schools and a dozen accents are what she builds on top of it.

**This is already on the page. It does not need adding — only not burying.**

## The stages, in your life, in plain words

| Stage | Where it is | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **The world she's given** | Uganda 1959–70 (ch. 2) | Not her choice, and told largely in her mother's voice. The book opens with someone else's words — which is right, because at this point she doesn't have her own. |
| **The door shuts** | Amin; Entebbe; Woolwich (ch. 2) | Not a call answered. A world removed. |
| **Refused twice** | London, then Cape Town (ch. 2–3) | The pink paint. The strangling attack. Another move. The question is born here. |
| **Learning to adapt** | Schools, Windhoek (ch. 3–4) | Accents as survival. The skill that runs the entire book. |
| **Becoming competent** | Groote Schuur (ch. 5) | Nursing, teaching anatomy in Afrikaans, Barnard's unit. She becomes someone with real authority. Easy to undersell — don't. |
| **Life turns** | Rhodesia; Colin; Tim (ch. 6) | Out of violence — John paralysed, Gerry shot — comes a marriage and a son. |
| **The long road** | 1992–2013, roughly ch. 7–35 | Kenya to Madagascar to Ethiopia to Brazil. Comic ordeal after comic ordeal. |
| **The dark stretch** | 2014–2017 (ch. 36, 38, 42) | Mum dies. Divorce. John moves in. Shadow drowns. *"annus horribilis."* Then in 2017, John dies too. |
| **Travelling as herself** | 2015 onwards (ch. 38–53) | Everything changes character. See below. |
| **The return** | Ireland 2019 and 2025 (ch. 46, 54) | Not an arrival. A partial answer, taken anyway. |

## The hinge of the whole book

**The middle years are somebody else's quest.**

Through the long road, she is largely travelling inside Col's obsessions — his birds, his butterflies, his jaguars, his cameras. She is the one holding the torch, hobbling on a septic toe, sleeping on a camera bag. Tim is officially *"the supplement."*

That is not a criticism of the writing — it is **the engine of the comedy**. The humour comes from a clever, capable woman being dragged up a ninety-degree muddy slope in the dark for a lemur she didn't ask to see.

**Then it stops.** After the divorce, the trips change completely:

- No more Land Cruiser. Hostels, capsule hotels, a sleeping pod in Doha.
- No more quarry. She goes for cities, food, K-dramas, her sisters, Tim.
- She starts choosing. Istanbul for a significant birthday. *"I felt Istanbul was far enough away."*
- She starts asking for what she wants — the Bali hotel-room moves, worrying about being "a difficult customer."
- She travels alone and is fine.

**This is the real turn in the book, and at the moment it is never noticed out loud.** Not by announcing it — but a reader should be able to feel that the second half is a different woman travelling.

## The ending you have already written

Three things at the end are doing the work, and none needs improving:

1. **The rock at the Giant's Causeway.** She looks for the name she carved aged eleven and finds *"what could be part of my name,"* photographing it anyway because it is *"roughly the right height for an eleven year old."* A partial answer. Possibly not even hers. Taken anyway.

2. **The David Whyte poem** Tim plays her — *"Just beyond yourself. It's where you need to be."* The answer isn't a place. It's the going.

3. **"And on the road you meet those who come before you."** — which is also the epigraph to Bali 2024. It already echoes across two chapters.

**Open question for her:** the book's actual last sentence is currently about the weather in Cape Town. The three things above happen before it. Is the weather line the ending you want, or should the book close nearer the causeway and the poem?

## Where the shape is currently thin

Honest structural notes. None require rewriting — mostly they need a sentence or two placed well.

1. **The question is asked in the preface and then vanishes for forty chapters.** It survives only as a running gag about mangled names — Mosteots, Mestart, Janeth, Jenna, Dora D'Oraborous. That gag is perfect and should stay. But the question itself never surfaces as a thought until Ireland.

2. **She has already noticed this herself.** In Bali 2023 she writes: *"Being partially British, (hmm will this statement direct me later?)"* — she is asking her own book the question mid-flight, and it goes unanswered. That aside is a gift. It should land somewhere.

3. **Mum opens the book and then disappears.** Glenys and the pink Mac are the reason the book exists, and after chapter 2 she is almost absent until the toast in Bali 2023: *"'one for Mum'... 'She'll have cheesecake with cinnamon ice cream.' Because she would have."* That is one of the best lines in the book, and nothing sets it up.

4. **Uganda is 18,000 words; Barcelona is 335.** Some unevenness is honest — big years are bigger. But Jakarta/Bali 2021 runs to nearly 20,000 words while several substantial chapters run under a thousand.

5. **The three deaths that matter most are handled briefly.** Mum's death is a clause in the Italy chapter. John's death is a paragraph tucked into the end of Venice. Both are told at speed, in passing. That may well be deliberate — it is how she copes, and understatement is her whole voice. Worth a conversation rather than an assumption.

## Rules for using this note

- **Never signpost.** No chapter is called "The Ordeal." No sentence says "little did I know."
- **A shape is felt as pacing**, not stated. Slow down where it matters; don't explain why.
- **The comedy is not decoration** — it is how she survives, and it should never be sacrificed to make the shape clearer.
- **Nothing here overrides truth.** If life didn't do it, the book doesn't do it.
- This note is for us. It never appears in the book.
