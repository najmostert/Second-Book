# Voice guide

How she sounds when she sounds like herself — with real examples from her own pages.

Use this whenever we polish, restore, or write. When she says *"more like my May version,"* this is what she means.

---

## The short version

**She is funny about frightening things, precise about small things, and never tells you how to feel.**

If a passage explains the joke, labels the emotion, or reaches for a grand adjective, it isn't her.

---

## What she actually does

### 1. Short sentences. The joke lands last.

> *"She wakes up only once during the flight to eat breakfast and close the porthole cover, instantly shutting off the magnificently hued sunrise from view. I amuse myself envisioning how I'll poke her in the eyes when she wakes up."*

> *"At last the curtains opened and out she came - costume intact. All that is, except the hat with the fruit, which she ate during the long wait."*

The last sentence of her paragraphs is very often the punchline. **Never move the funny bit to the middle. Never add a sentence after it.**

### 2. She undercuts drama with something ordinary or undignified

The bigger the moment, the smaller the thing she puts next to it.

> Storm at sea: *"This was, I knew for certain the ships swan song, a symphony to shipwreck. I prayed for morning, calm, and my mother."* — and then Mr Taylor says: *"Oh good oh….bangers."*

> Gravel in Col's mouth: *"To say he was displeased is to make light of the moment."*

> The indri, a rare and wondrous animal, *"presents its bottom to us"* — and she keeps *"gazing in rapt attention."*

> Hotel loo paper: *"So hard you can cut your nether regions with an energetic wipe."*

### 3. She talks to the reader

> *"Let me start at the beginning and you can make up your own mind…"*

> *"I'm not good with abusive authority as you'll know by now."*

Asides in brackets, as though leaning across the table.

### 4. She is hard on herself, but never sorry for herself

> *"gosh, forgotten already, but I know it has several syllables and sounds foreign."*

> *"I'm only on year two in Duolingo."*

> *"I somewhat uncharitably imagine him choking on a mouldy croissant and feel better."*

Self-pity is entirely absent from this book. Keep it that way.

### 5. She gives you the exact thing, not the atmosphere

> *"cling-wrapped cocoons of rucksacks"*

> *"400 euros and receive 3,600,000 Malagasy francs"*

> *"a bush the colour of a pale blueberry that screams 'poisonous'"*

> Waterbuck with *"wet paint toilet seat"* bottoms

One odd, exact, real detail beats three adjectives every time.

### 6. Dialogue is kept exactly as it was said

She doesn't tidy people's English. The mistakes *are* the scene.

> Col: *'We would like some wine.'*
> Waiter: *'You would like it, no?'*
> Col: *'Yes, now.'*
> Col: *'Non, yes, now.'*
> Waiter: *'I bring it now, non?'*

> *"Smell and taste zis tweeg"*

> The MP: *"You people make me sick!"*

**Never paraphrase dialogue. Never correct someone's grammar inside quotation marks.**

### 7. She names things to make them funny

Clive the chicken. Freda Flintstone. Happy Harry. Hotel 'Grubby'. Racoute the mosquito. *"You simply cannot eat something you've named"* — which is a joke she then lives out.

---

## Her habits on the page

- **Single quote marks** for speech: `'Bonjour'`
- **Brackets** for the aside she can't resist
- **Dashes** for the afterthought
- **Short paragraphs** — usually one to four sentences. In an action scene, shorter still.
- One long breathless sentence, then a very short one. That contrast is the rhythm.

---

## Her own rules, in her own words

Found written at the end of her May pages — she gave herself this advice:

- *"Don't begin with there is/was or it was/is"*
- *"Remove 'had' and 'that'"*
- *"Instead of Really, Very or So… 'he sprinted desperate to get home'"*
- Avoid *"itinerary in prose"* — the day-by-day slog

## What the rewrite did to her (and what to strip out)

The pattern, measured across matched passages: the rewrite **added roughly 10–40% more words to every paragraph**, and up to **60% more** to anything explanatory. It rarely changed the facts. It just would not shut up.

### The padding words it loved

devastatingly · intensely · thoroughly · remarkably · spectacular · pristine · primeval · magnificent · iconic · literal / literally · completely · absolutely · officially · significantly · beautifully · expertly · frantically · undeterred · profusely

### The guidebook voice it added

*"the bustling capital"* · *"nestled"* · *"serves as"* · *"the primary drivers of deforestation"* · *"navigating the bureaucracy"* · *"cultural rules and rituals surrounding death"*

Plus whole encyclopaedia paragraphs she never wrote, and Latin names in brackets — *(Indri indri)*, *(Uroplatus)* — which she only ever used if a guide actually said them out loud.

### It softened her jokes

| Hers | The rewrite |
|---|---|
| *"presents its bottom to us"* | *"unhelpfully presented its bottom to our cameras"* |
| *"can't eat something you've named"* | *"You simply cannot eat a creature that you have formally named"* |
| *"I amuse myself envisioning how I'll poke her in the eyes"* | *"envisioning exactly poking her in the eyes with my fork"* |

### It explained emotions she had already shown

She writes a dead man on the road, no pulse, and they walk on. The rewrite adds *"Our dilemma was what to do regarding this somewhat grisly find"* and *"cast a pall over the evening."* She had already shown us. Twice over is once too many.

### Side by side — the square eggs

**Hers (125 words):**

> *"Col asks the waitress for boiled eggs but she doesn't understand him so he takes out a piece of paper and draws what he thinks would be identified as a boiled egg inside a pot of boiling water. I'm not convinced and when she returns with two fried eggs and apologises that they aren't 'square enough' I have to laugh."*

**The rewrite (174 words)** added: *"our young waitress"*, *"completely failed to understand"*, *"Undeterred"*, *"extracted a piece of scrap paper"*, *"universally identified"*, *"I wasn't at all convinced by his artistry"*, *"apologizing profusely"*, *"I had to laugh out loud"*, *"this specific Western request"* — and a paragraph about a vanilla plantation.

Same joke. Fifty more words. Much less funny.

## The fifteen questions

Ask these of any paragraph to tell her from the rewrite.

1. Would this sound right said aloud over a cup of tea?
2. Does the paragraph **end** on the joke or the sharpest detail?
3. Have I named an emotion the scene already showed?
4. Is there a Latin name in brackets that nobody said out loud?
5. Any guidebook words — *bustling, nestled, serves as, features*?
6. Three adjectives where she'd have used one plain one?
7. Has *we* been quietly changed to *I*? She says *we* far more than you'd think.
8. Is there a set-piece missing its bodily detail or its sod's-law moment?
9. Is dialogue word-for-word, or has it been tidied?
10. Any paragraph over five sentences without a new beat? Split it.
11. Count *literally, actually, perfectly, completely, remarkably*. More than one a page — cut.
12. Has a joke been explained?
13. Does a sentence start with *There was* or *It was*? Her own rule says no.
14. Would she recognise this as herself talking to a friend — or does it read like a travel magazine?
15. If I delete this sentence, does any fact or joke break? If not, it's padding.

## Standing defaults

Past tense for chapter narrative. British English throughout — *colour, organised, travelling, towards, centre, favourite*. Light grammar when we're in a passage anyway. Obvious typos fixed without asking.

**One permanent exception:** Dan's email typed on a French keyboard in Madagascar 2003. Never touched, never tidied, never spell-checked. The mess is the joke.
