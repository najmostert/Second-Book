# Chapter 48: Indonesia 2021

## Jakarta

Travelling during a pandemic was always going to be different and leaving for Indonesia in the middle of one was no exception.
The paperwork for travel was double the usual amount as it included PCR tests (taken at just the right time for the number of hours before and after arrival in Jakarta, otherwise I would not have been able to either leave or arrive as it were), special travel visas (as motivation for travel), twenty email and WhatsApp messages to and from ‘my’ Bali travel guru. My hotel booking in Jakarta was no less difficult as it had to be one that ‘catered’ for isolation.
I got up early and rushed for my phone. My PCR test results from the pathology lab had to arrive that day. I saw there was an email from them and opened it with one eye, hardly daring to read it. The other eye flew open, Phew! It was negative. I’d been a little worried as I was slightly sniffly with a scratchy throat. I tried to send the results to my special Bali ‘Travel passport’ called the PeduliLindungi app but it wasn’t uploading. This wasn’t good — I needed it for the airport on arrival. In desperation I phoned a Pathcare travel doctor.
He was short and rude (I mean, he may be six feet tall, but he spoke gruffly in single word sentences). He said he’d upload the results for a few hundred blue notes.
It was pitch dark when I reluctantly peeled back the bedclothes as we were in the middle of yet another power outage. These occurred (at that time) in South Africa throughout the week on a daily basis. I dressed by feel and hoped I wasn’t wearing anything inside-out then staggered down the stairs by the light of my phone with my suitcase.

After a cup of tea, (without which I’m going nowhere) using the last of my gas, I fed Fish n Chips and tried to give them a last hug but they wriggled out of my clutches and scarpered. Having donned my Mexican ‘day of the dead’ face mask, I stood at the gate and waited for my uber. Peter, the British driver I’d pre-ordered arrived at 5:20. He was a chatty fellow and he told me of his previous life working 9–5 in the UK. He told me he worked for the film industry and usually ferried film crew and famous actors around. We arrived at the airport and I pressed some blue cracklies into his palm and entered the airport which was quiet due to the early hour and the fact that very few people were travelling during covid. Those who were, all wore masks of varying degrees of effectiveness (most lay just under the nose or chin — they may as well have attached a damp serviette to their chin for all the protection they were getting).
In Joburg airport, I had to collect my suitcase which was about 5 km away but easy to identify once I’d walked the length of the huge airport.
Check-in took ages as they had to ask me about my flights, destination, reason for travelling, visas, Covid test results, my stay in Jakarta, my stay in Bali and my hotel information. I entrusted my huge suitcase to the airline and walked with a much lighter step to the other side of the airport again to where my flight would take off. I’m sure I walked the requisite 10,000 steps in the airport alone.

My seat was the last seat at the furthest point in the huge B777 but at least no one sat next to me, thanks to covid restrictions. As we were taxiing down the runway I heard the guy in the next aisle chatting loudly on his phone. I glared at him several times but he either ignored me or didn’t notice, then another man in the seat in front started a loud conversation. I grabbed a passing stewardess and told her to ask them to get off their phones. She raised a finely pencilled brow but made an announcement on the PA for all passengers to get off their goddamn phones, or words to that effect. They knew it was me who told teacher so I got glared at by both. They were both wearing their masks on their chins which annoyed me even more, (I hadn’t enjoyed my eight hours of sleep, remember,) so I pointed to my mask and glared again. So having won ‘Miss popularity’ in the space of a few minutes I was thinking my food would be spat on (I probably deserved it for being a Karen, [sorry Karen]).

The plane itself was really nice. It looked new and was fairly spacious, even in cattle class. Of course we were not allowed to sit next to each other as an extra covid ‘protection’ which was great news in my opinion. Ah the joys of travelling in a pandemic; we had to wear our masks the entire time (bar eating). I lay down on the remaining empty seats next to me and tried to sleep but the eye mask kept slipping and I felt claustrophobic with the other mask over my mouth and nose, so eventually I gave up and sat up again.
I found one of the sloping metal seats that abound in airports and are designed to make you wish you’d never thought of travelling then gazed around the mostly empty lounge. Hazmat suits seemed to be all the rage in Doha as I saw no less than five people wearing them. It was quite warm, (28°C) even at midnight and it seemed to take hours before it was time to board the next plane to Jakarta. I was beyond tired and shuffled onto the Airbus to the back seat. This time I had two companions unfortunately and it was really cramped which kind of negated all the space I had earlier. I only had tea for ‘supper’ or whatever a meal at 3am constitutes. After a few hours of cramped misery, the kind steward came to me and asked if I’d like to move up to the front where there was more leg room. I was so grateful and hoped my eyes crinkled sufficiently to let him know I was smiling at him.
This seat was much better and my nearest neighbour was two seats away. I was at least able to doze for short periods of time, though I kept waking up with pins and needles in my hands and feet. And so the night passed with intermittent dozing, waking and wee-ing. The overhead lights snapped on suddenly at 7:15, South African time (midday local time) and we were given our trays of breakfast. My stomach was rebelling against eating again so I had tea, coffee and water. The steward said he liked my mask and I got an extra packet of biscuits.

We disembarked at Soekarno-Hatta airport in Jakarta and I immediately regretted it. The lounge was hot and airless as we shuffled towards the baggage area to collect our suitcases. Halfway there we were stopped by an officious uniformed man who barked orders at us in Indonesian as though we were a group of prisoners instead of paying customers. Only a few of us understood what he was saying and we shuffled around uncertainly. He pointed to rows of plastic chairs and said what must have been, ‘Sit!’ All passengers sat down on a plastic chair and looked around in bewilderment. No one knew what was happening or why. I just figured it had something to do with a Covid protocol. We were requested to send several selfie photos to both the hotel and the visa dude. I snapped quickly and sent each a disgruntled expression — well the eyebrows were definitely not gruntled!
And so we sat and waited in sleep-deprived stupor waiting to be told what to do next. An hour and a half passed before I shoved several papers at some uniform after which he led me to a makeshift cubicle where I sat and waited again. I learnt twenty minutes later that I was to suffer yet another covid test. Several tubes were shoved up my nostrils and down to my stomach (Ok it just felt that far), till I obliged with a gag reflex. I got up with watery, red eyes and followed his finger to yet another seat where I guessed I was to await the results of my test. I messaged my hotel sponsor to let them know I had arrived but was stuck at the airport. An hour and a half later I was met by a hotel representative. He took my passport and went in search of my results. At least he spoke the language I’m familiar with. I found a stall selling coffee and took my mug back to my seat. Much later, (I was a total zombie by now) the hotel rep returned with my passport and test results which were negative (they were negative before boarding so why would this tradition not continue?)

He led me to the luggage area as I was incapable of independent action by then. I located my giant overweight bag and I was led to yet another seat and told to wait. I raised my bloodshot eyes slowly in disbelief, I’d done nothing but wait for the better part of two days, what’s another day or two. He disappeared and I sat slumped in my plastic chair for another forty minutes. It had been three hours since we arrived in Jakarta and I already wanted to leave on the next plane, except that would have meant another few days without sleep and I’d already forgotten who I was.
Eventually my visa chap pitched up, and I rose hopefully but he just wanted another photo of me. By then they could have papered the walls with photos of a slowly deteriorating red-eyed female. He left and I moved my sore pink-rimmed eyeballs to my left where two girls were sitting. We had a muffled chat through our masks and I found out they were also going to Bali after Jakarta. They were from the Netherlands. We would share a taxi to the hotel, so, when the time eventually came, we left the confines of the airport to the grey evening light outside. They were going to a different hotel so the taxi deposited me first outside the Novotel hotel and chucked my giant case onto the pavement and zoomed off. I was left breathing in hot, humid air through my mask and grabbed my suitcase preparing to enter the hotel doors. Before I got any further, a uniformed security guard stopped me and asked me to spread my legs.
Before I could ask why, I was sprayed from top to bottom with some sort of insecticide. I received a mouthful of spray through my soggy mask before being ushered briskly around the side of the hotel like some leprous beggar. Well I use the word brisk but in my case I moved like someone that has woken up from a bad dream to find out the reality is worse. I was pushed into a lift to the 9th floor and deposited into a small hallway between hotel rooms and wheeled my case uncertainly towards a small uniformed woman wearing two masks. She was seated at a wooden desk. She gestured towards an empty chair in front of her. I sat swaying with exhaustion while she looked through my file of documents then shuffled them into a neat pile and reached for a three-page questionnaire. She asked me a hundred muffled questions regarding my travel plans, interspersed with the proffering of corresponding documentation then stood abruptly and left me to gaze through sore eyeballs for the next hour of probing.
I felt confident now, should I at any stage in the future find myself at the opposite end of a Russian interrogation, that I’ll do okay after this two-day nightmare.
After half an hour she returned and asked me to come with her. I was certain I was being led to a cell for some misdemeanour or other but she stopped outside a numbered door which she opened with a flourish leading me into my hotel room.

‘Don’t leave room for any reason,’ she barked and trotted off down the corridor.

The room was standard hotel; two single beds, a desk and chair and en-suite bathroom. I glanced at the mirror and saw someone who looked like she needed urgent medical attention. There was a TV and the shower has a big clear window, presumably for those odd couples who enjoy watching their spouse on the toilet. It was already 11pm local time but I decided to have a cup of tea. I had a lovely hot shower and phoned down for some milk for my tea. No ways was I using the powdered milk they’d provided. A small jug came up on a tray by a masked man who didn’t even hand me the tray but set it down on a small wooden table outside my room. I think I unpacked a few things but must have fallen asleep on top of the bed fully clothed.

Something had woken me. It was dark so after stumbling to the bathroom I switched on the light then hastily switched it off again because water was gushing out of the light fixture and I’m pretty sure electricity and water don’t mix well.

I got the bedside phone and looked up the number for reception and told the woman who answered that water was coming out of my bathroom light and they must hurry. I glanced at my watch. It was 1:30am and only two hours’ sleep in three days. I must not have emphasised the ‘hurry up’ bit because just as I was dozing off an hour later, someone knocked on my door. I was met with two men dressed from head to toe in white. They were wearing masks over their already covered heads and faces. News of my leprosy must have travelled. The hazmats moved towards the bathroom and I crawled into bed. They were there till 2:45 banging and chatting. They said something to the effect that the light was fixed and closed the door behind them. I was beyond tired by then so read my book until 4:30 am. After all I wasn’t going anywhere for three days.

I woke up to the sound of the phone ringing shrilly next to me on the bedside table. I leant over and mumbled, ‘Hullo?’. It was room service and they told me that it was now 10:30 and I had not eaten my breakfast. The woman told me my food was outside my door and I was only to collect it and not leave the room. She asked me what time I’d like lunch. Given that I was about to eat breakfast at 11:30, I asked for 2pm. I mumbled my thanks though in reality I was annoyed. Throwing on a long, floaty top, I opened the door and peered out cautiously. To my left was the small metal table with a tray. I grabbed it and closed the door quickly lest I was being watched for any transgression. I lifted the cover and peered at a semi-congealed omelette, some rubbery toast and an unidentifiable soup. I took a few bites and re-covered the food and decided I needed a strong cup of tea more than I needed indigestible food. After a good cuppa using my own tea bags, I spent the rest of the day packing and repacking my huge suitcase in the hope I could find space for eight bars of chocolate I’d brought for a friend of Tim’s who was craving Turkish delight. I squeezed two bars in but had to leave the others for my already bulging backpack. The hours passed slowly as I answered email and WhatsApp messages, watched a programme or two on the telly about growing tomatoes and twenty ways to cook chicken. I was desperate to look outside, but my room had no balcony and the windows had been sealed. I plonked a moisturising face mask on my face in the hope it could erase some of the results of my sleepless nights and washed some underclothes in the bathroom then shoved them over the curtain rail to dry, then read my book and did some puzzles on my iPad. Lunch arrived at 2 and I brought in a more appetising plate of food.
I’d asked for some laundry to be done and at 3pm a soft knock at the door announced my clean, neatly folded clothes. The young masked girl motioned for me to stand clutching my clean laundry while she took a photograph. I was wondering whether to smile or look suitably serious as this ‘proof of delivery’ took place. I opted for a sort of grimace in the end, thanked her and closed the door. Read some more, watched a wildlife programme on telly, and later a Korean series on my iPad until 9pm and supper had come and gone. I was feeling tired so climbed into bed and prepared for a better night’s sleep.
The second day went pretty much the same as the first except again I was startled into wakefulness by a loud knocking on my door. I grabbed a gown and stumbled to the door. I was greeted by two hazmat men, a nurse heavily masked and for some reason an armed guard. I was seriously beginning to feel like a dangerous prisoner by then.
I glanced at my watch, it was 1am! The nurse told me in halting English that she was going to test me for covid again and I stood unceremoniously in the doorway overlooked by three people as the nurse assaulted my nostrils with a long tube until I gagged. They left as quickly as they arrived and I closed the door. It’s hard to remain sleepy when my nostrils have just had a pipe shoved into my oesophagus so I left the light on, reached for my laptop and booked my flight to Bali. I watched another two episodes of my Korean series and fell asleep around 4am.
I awoke at 10:30 and fetched my breakfast tray, knowing it had been there since 7am. I lifted the lid and gazed at a plate full of carbs. There was white bread, a pancake and two small croissants. I ate the croissants with my tea then plonked the tray back outside and messaged Tim. We discussed my upcoming flight and noticed a discrepancy with times. Seems I was leaving at 4pm not 6pm which immediately increased my anxiety. I phoned reception and asked if my PCR results had come through yet. She said they had but they were waiting for, ‘the government’ to prepare the necessary documentation to that effect.

I was panicking as it was already 11:45 and I had to leave for the airport by 2pm at the latest. I began shoving items around my case and repacking stuff mostly to pass the time. At last another knock on the door announced that I was covid free and it was time to leave the room I’d been in for the best part of three days (I was lucky; a few weeks earlier, the minimum time spent in isolation was a week to ten days.) I staggered with my case, rucksack and extra bag holding things that could not be squeezed in anywhere without changing their composition.
In the same foyer as before, I was told to wait (no use asking why). I met another European girl who was also leaving for Bali but on a later plane. The receptionist presented me with my passport and an official letter typed in Indonesian that informed anyone who was interested, (and so far there had been many,) that I was free of the dreaded Covid virus. She asked me to stand and hold the passport and official document in front of me while she took a photo. There must have been at least eight photos of me holding various objects floating around Jakarta by then. I was really anxious to get going now they’d photographed me doing everything except leaving the hotel. They summoned a taxi and I hopped in,

‘Airport, quickly please.’ and off we went. As the taxi driver tried to enter the busy main road, he flung his hand out of the window and waved a hundred Rupiah note. A man appeared from the side of the road and he marched into the busy traffic with his hand up. I was surprised to see cars actually slow down sufficiently enough for the taxi to join the swarm of traffic. This dude ought to get danger pay though. I wasn’t looking forward to my return to the airport.

We arrived at 2pm and I leapt out of the taxi. He retrieved my huge case from the boot and asked me for 500,000 Indonesian Rupiah. I looked at him in disbelief.

‘Hotel pay.’ I told him. He shook his head, ‘No, No pay.’ I was in a quandary. I knew that I’d paid for an all-inclusive ‘Isolation package’ but he was now holding onto my suitcase.

‘I don’t have Indonesian money,’ I told him truthfully.

He said, ‘ATM inside.’ and pointed to the airport doors.
I tried to argue with him but he wasn’t budging and it was getting later. I still had to send my luggage on. In the end I rushed inside and tried to find an ATM. I had no idea of the actual value of all the notes that I withdrew but the numbers had a lot of zeros at the end. I panted back outside and thrust a note at him. He looked at it disdainfully and gestured at the rest of my pile of notes that I was clutching. I peeled off another IR100,000. He pointed to a note with the legend IR 50,000 so I thrust it at him and grabbed my case out of his hand and rushed inside. The airport was uncomfortably full and I felt anxious. I pushed ahead murmuring apologies and muttering that I was going to miss my plane.

I discovered my suitcase was now underweight by 2 kilos and I was angry with myself because now I was lugging around an extra heavy bag as hand luggage. For some reason I was given a different ticket and boarding pass to the one I already had.

I wasn’t sure if I was in the right place for boarding, so after an hour of indecision, I rushed over to another waiting room and asked if it was the correct one. The officials just stared at me rudely or shook their heads. I decided to just wait it out and hope for the best. By the time the call to board my plane came, we were two hours past take-off and I was hot and grumpy. As a result I walked past my allocated seat to almost the end of the plane, then had to try and find my way back to the seat like a salmon swimming upstream. I found it was a tiny seat next to a window (I’d asked for an aisle seat). I was joined by a petite woman sporting the same colour hair as me. I was annoyed to see her hair was long and sleek with nary a frizz in sight. Bet hers isn’t an expensive Brazilian either.
We all sat in the small stuffy plane with our masks on waiting for take-off which only occurred forty minutes later. I had absolutely no idea what the time was as I’d crossed three time zones already and was about to cross another. I was rather hoping the food would arrive soon as I hadn’t eaten since my two small croissants early that morning but nothing was forthcoming. I mentally added hypoglycaemia to my list of woes. The slim woman in the next seat opened a packet of nuts and my stomach gurgled. I was saved from passing out by unintentionally knocking them out of her hand and grabbing a few from the floor on my way up.
She had nevertheless seen my slobbering intention and turned away in her seat to slide all the crispy, salted nuts directly down her slim gullet.

‘Cow!’ I muttered uncharitably and gazed out of the small window as we approached the runway at Denpasar airport, Bali.

I was feeling excited now. I was soon to see my Tim after a year and a half of lockdown. After finding my luggage without trouble I walked through after Customs to find my precious boy waiting for me. A long hug then Tim took my case and wheeled it outside for a taxi. The heat was intense and I was already peeling off layers. Tim haggled a good price for a taxi, I slid in and smiled at him, having lowered my mask.

‘Thought I’d never get here.’ I told him giving him another hug. I pulled back and gazed at him again,

‘Hmm, You’ve grown a moustache.’

I knew he’d grown one but this comment was meant to convey my dislike for it. Tim ignored the comment. He said he came on his scooter, so spoke to the taxi driver in Indonesian, waved and left. I was really impressed. Granted he had spent two years of lockdown here in Bali, but to be able to give instructions in Indonesian was impressive. Tim gave the driver directions to my hotel in Uluwatu, some forty km away. I was too tired to formulate any type of conversation so the drive was quick and quiet. Tim had already paid him so after he left my suitcase next to me and departed, I stood for a minute. I didn’t see an actual sign but the driver said the small bamboo decorated entrance was the entrance to my hotel. I descended a flight of white painted steps, the end of which is a large sparkling turquoise infinity pool. A small reception area lies to the left and a young Indonesian man greeted me. We exchanged the necessary paperwork and he led me to my room which is off the pool area down a short path. It is an A-shaped thatch-roofed room and looks quite lovely. Inside was a huge bed in the centre of the round room, surrounded by mosquito netting. There was a large stone walk-in shower and toilet off the room. It was delightful! Tim arrived shortly afterwards on his bike. He had brought a takeaway meal for us which we ate sitting upstairs on the wide stone balcony overlooking the pool. It was very humid but there was a fan blowing over us. The meal was a local dish which was hot and spicy. Tim and I caught up till about 10:30pm. He left me to unpack, shower and fall gratefully into bed.

## Uluwatu

I jumped out of bed when I saw it was already 8:30am. I had slept 7 and a half hours. The air conditioner was deliciously cool but when I took my cup of tea outside, the intense heat hit me like a wet towel. Tim messaged me to say he was waiting at the top so I grabbed my bag and raced as fast as I could up the somewhat slippery white stone steps to the entrance. Tim told me to get on the back of his bike. I had a few tries till I was sufficiently stable. It must be difficult for him to keep the bike steady while I clambered on and shuffled around, flattening the back tyre substantially in the process. I put my arms round his waist and he set off. We were going for coffee. He told me to have my face mask ready but that they were less strict about protocol here in Uluwatu, which is situated south-east of Bali in Pecatu. I looked around at the lush forest as we motored past on a narrow road.

It helped to cool me down as the wind flowed over my hot face. We arrived at a place called BGS. I climbed off while Tim steadied the bike. I told him he rode well despite having me on the back.
Tim and I caught up on life on the island since lockdown. He said he was lucky to have made some really good friends, as they were some of the very few westerners to stay behind after lockdown. They called themselves “The Bali Survivors”. I hoped to meet some of them while I was there. A couple of young, tanned girls at the next table leant over and told me they liked my mask. Instead of the mouth-to-nose paper mask that most people wear, this one hangs below my chin and two loops hook over my ears. The painted face, à la ‘Mexican Day of the Dead’, matches my features perfectly, so it looks as if I’m wearing a full mask. It’s hardly efficient as a virus deterrent though.
After coffee, I climbed on the back of Tim’s bike and we headed off to a place called Mana. It is built overlooking the sea from a cliff and is one of his favourite places. En route, we stopped at a local temple; Uluwatu Temple is a respected Balinese Hindu Sea Temple and is regarded as one of the Sad Kahyangan (not Sad as in unhappy), six sanctuaries of the world. It’s a holy place of worship and according to Balinese beliefs, it’s one of six pivotal points on the island which provide spiritual balance to Bali. We walked along the cliff edge. There are many red blossom trees and macaque monkeys scuttling around the trees. Two local Indonesian women were sitting near the temple weaving colourful bracelets from yarn. Tim stopped and chatted a little to them in Indonesian. He asked how much for the bracelets;

‘Selamat pagi. Berapa harga gelang-gelang ini?’

They speak Balinese here in Bali, although Indonesian is also spoken and understood by most. I bought four bracelets and was given two for free. A huge statue depicting some deity rises from the top of the forest like a scary character from a children’s storybook. Tim was wearing a small purple cloth around his waist in deference to the locals whilst in a temple. We walked back to the scooter and rode to Mana; a huge open-plan restaurant, open on three sides with a long counter against one wall. We found a seat at the edge so we got an offshore breeze. Tim told me I must try the coconuts and ordered,

‘Tolong dua buah kelapa,’ and two shaved coconuts arrived with the tops lopped off with a straw.
Tim said he was taking me to his favourite spa for a massage. As I alighted from his bike, I found a small square of raffia on the step and bent down to pick it up. Tim stopped me.

‘That is an offering,’ he told me, ‘You shouldn’t pick it up.’
Every morning, Balinese women collect colourful flower heads and place these on the small square of raffia together with some rice, bits of fruit, incense, sweets and sometimes a cigarette. These offerings are called ‘canang sari’ and form a sacred act of gratitude to the Gods. The religion in Bali is mostly Hindu, although there are some people from other islands, such as Jakarta, who are Muslim. I thought it was delightful and so colourful. There are a lot of stray Bali dogs lying around or sniffing at the offerings which often provide a morsel of sustenance. I watched anxiously as motorbikes deftly swerved past them. The dogs are mostly the ‘Kintamani’ breed; medium in size, friendly, affectionate and very independent. Some dogs just lie in the middle of the road, and I wondered how more aren’t killed, as hundreds of motorbikes and scooters whizz by on the busy roads every day.
At the spa, we were greeted in Balinese,
‘Om Suwastiastu,’ with hands held prayer-like in front of the face by a pretty, petite woman wearing the traditional Balinese dress; a tight-fitting, jacket-style top (usually of a white, lacy material) over a long skirt which is wrapped around the body. A cloth belt is tied around their middle. They often wear flowers in their long hair which is tied back into a small bun.
Tim and I removed our shoes, placed them in a shoe box against one wall and were led over to some raised wooden box seats. A woman arrived with a basin of warm, petal-strewn water. She gestured gracefully to place our feet in the basin while she gently washed them and I giggled at the ticklish bits.
Tim arrived on his scooter and we headed straight to breakfast. One is spoilt for choice in Bali; the food is fresh and delicious everywhere you go. Later I took a wander around. It was a bit hairy walking around here as there are absolutely no pavements until you get to the shopping area, so you’re constantly within a whisker of passing cars, lorries and bikes. I looked at the many small shrines along the way, all with the prerequisite offerings. Tim picked me up and we raced down to the lookout point overlooking the beach, clambered over a grassy area to the top of the huge white cliffs and waited for sunset. Tim had brought us each a can of Bintang beer, so we sat watching the most spectacular sunset I’d ever seen. Every shade of red, orange and yellow washed the sky in vibrant colour that slowly deepened and darkened. I took a hundred photos. A ‘warung’ is a small, family-owned eatery that serves affordable, traditional Indonesian or Balinese food, often in a casual and simple setting.
Our small wooden table faced a small, cracked basin and tap attached to a crumbling wall. Seven containers of food were kept warm at the counter and these were scooped onto a big plate. We sat outside in the still humid evening and ate fried chicken and vegetables. In bed, I heard what was becoming a familiar sound that came from the roof.
Tim was already waiting for me, so I flung myself on the back of his scooter making the bike wobble. ‘Sorry,’ I mumbled before zooming off. Drifters is a hippie surfer shop with a large retail section selling clothes and surfing requirements in the front and a restaurant at the back. There are two tables indoors and quite a few outside. It has very cosy décor inside with big easy chairs, red woven cushions, plants, books and candles. We ordered a smoothie bowl, and twenty minutes later the most delicious bowl of goodness arrived. It had fresh berries, dragon fruit, mango, coconut cream, chia seeds, banana and honey. It was as tasty as it was colourful. I ventured off to find the loo, which is outside and chatted to a family from Boston en route. The man wearing a stetson and a wide smile started the conversation by asking if I was married. He said it was his first visit to Bali to see his son (with a nod in his direction) and I told him I was also visiting my son. I gathered the young woman with long dark hair who was scrolling endlessly on her phone was his son’s girlfriend. The toilet door opened and I left the conversation with a short, ‘nicetomeetyou’. After finishing the delicious smoothie bowl, I wandered around the shop and bought a few things to take home as presents.
From here, we set off towards the Garuda Wisnu Kencana Cultural Park or GWK. It’s about forty minutes away so I clung on to Tim and looked round at the passing forest. At the entrance we donned our masks again. Tim brought another helmet for me. It had an annoying visor that kept slipping over my eyes but I appreciated the extra safety. At the desk, we showed our health status cards (mine was on my PeduliLindungi app). The tickets were much cheaper then with Covid — another dubious ‘plus’ with the pandemic, though obviously not for the Balinese. We hopped on an open-top bus and rode to the main entrance, then walked towards the huge statue of Vishnu that you can see from as far away as Canggu on a clear day. Sandstone statues line the inside wall which comprises a long room with exhibits on either side. A series of rather gruesome-looking models depict Batara Kala, the god of death and also creator of light and the earth. Being the god of time, he also ‘devours’ people which is one way of looking at it I guess. The creatures look fierce with terrifying expressions and blood-red eyes. Snakes feature prominently.
After a short wait, the same open bus returned to where we had left the bike. I hopped on and we set off, my visor banging down every few minutes and obstructing my view as it is dusty and scratched. I met Tim’s German friend, Mel and her mum. Mel has long blonde hair and a wide smile; she is gorgeous. Her mum is small and blonde with a pleasant open face. She spoke very little English, so there were a lot of smiles between us but not much in the way of conversation. Tim’s other long-time friend, Elena, arrived. She had pale green hair with a pink fringe (that is to say, at the moment, later on as I got to know her, she would sport red, blue, purple and pink hair). Canggu evolved into a hotspot for digital nomads, surfers, and expats who are drawn to its laid-back atmosphere, trendy cafes, and picturesque beaches. As a result, traffic congestion has become a significant issue in the area, especially during peak hours. The ‘Shortcut’ is a two-kilometre stretch of road that was developed to alleviate some of the traffic congestion from the main road and to provide a more efficient route between Berawa and Batu Bolong. However, the Canggu Shortcut is now more of a hazard due to the combination of the narrow road, potholes galore, inexperienced scooter drivers and the high volume of traffic. This results in numerous accidents, some minor but also several fatalities over the years. My sisters and I inadvertently ended up on this very road a few years later, nearly ending our lives in the process.
There’s another well-known feature; ‘The Canggu Pole’, a traffic pole situated near the middle of the Canggu Shortcut. It stands in the middle of the road, splitting the already narrow road into even narrower lanes. ## Nusa Lembongan

A huge electrical storm zapped the darkened sky last night and my room lit up like a Christmas tree after each clap of thunder. Then the geckos started with their evening, ‘Gecko’ greeting which was really loud. That morning I made tea and sat outside on the verandah where the temperature was a balmy 30°C. Raindrops dripped from the bamboo trees and bushes that surround my room.

We were going to stay on an island for a few days — Lembongan to be specific. I packed my rucksack and a small bag and joined Elena in the back seat of the Grab car. The harbour was very busy so we had to sit on a low crumbling stone wall till our ferry was ready for us to board. The tide was out so they anchored further into the sea which meant we had to roll up our trousers (those who weren’t wearing shorts) and wade out with all our shoes in a large raffia basket carried by the ferry staff. I watched the boat bump over the waves. The sea became clear and a paler blue as we approached the island and I could see fish darting through the clear blue water. We arrived at Nusa Lembongan after forty-five minutes. The island is surrounded by coral reefs with white sand beaches and low limestone cliffs. It looks tropical with clear blue water and white sand beach.

Nusa Lembongan lies just southeast of Bali, and is part of a group of three islands; Nusa Penida, Nusa Lembongan, and Nusa Ceningan, collectively known as the Nusa islands, with Nusa meaning ‘island’. Lembongan is separated from the larger island of Nusa Penida and the smaller Nusa Ceningan by narrow channels.
We disembarked and waded through the warm water and onto the hot sand to claim our shoes from the raffia basket that was waiting on the beach. Tim and Elena moved quickly to the bike rental place. There are no cars on the island and hiring bikes is the only way you can get around. Tim found a scooter that was similar to the one he has on the mainland, he negotiated a good price and we roared off in tandem in search of our accommodation. ‘Krisna Huts’ are situated further down a very narrow dirt path. Apparently I had an upgrade although it looked the same as the others to me, except it had a small fridge and a bedside light. It was a large room with wooden shutters and a whitewashed wooden floor. Otherwise it was pretty basic which is to be expected for a very small island of only 8 square kilometres. At around 11am we walked down to the beach, stumbling over the hot, white sand towards a rustic looking restaurant called, ‘Ombak, zero waste café’ in Jungubatu. I was intrigued by the zero waste and watched keenly to see if this statement was true. The ‘floor’ is just white sand and a large, white wooden table with chairs around it.
I joined Tim, Elena and Tyler to ride to the other side of the island to look at some huge waves that crash out on a small projection near Panorama sunset. The area is called ‘Devil’s Tears’ accessible by walking over muddy areas and old cement ruins. We clambered over to the edge of the high cliff point and watched as gigantic waves broke a hundred metres into the air. It was quite exhilarating and some of the wave misted over our hot faces and cooled us off, albeit with a spray of salt water.

Another storm tore into the night last night sending zaps of light across the sky and again some noisy geckos. Tim collected me and I climbed on his bike saying, ‘Sorry’ as I shifted up the seat. ‘Tim! I’m sinking!’ I yelled frantically.

The water inside the Blue Lagoon is a light blue colour. ## Uluwatu

Tim took me to the other side of the island, to the big yellow bridge to look for the seaweed collectors. Seaweed farming is a major income for many people on the island. There’s a patchwork of wooden ‘plots’ in the bay at Jungut Batu, the mangrove and in the channel between Lembongan and Ceningan. Every morning, when the tide is low, they harvest the seaweed and put them in huge piles to dry. Traditional ‘Jukung’ boats have sails or are motorised and are used by farmers to access their plots and for transportation to the mainland.
