# Chapter 48: Indonesia 2021

## Jakarta

Travelling during a pandemic was always going to be different and leaving for Indonesia in the middle of one was no exception. The paperwork for travel was double the usual amount as it included PCR tests (taken at just the right time for the number of hours before and after arrival in Jakarta, otherwise I would not have been able to either leave or arrive as it were), special travel visas (as motivation for travel), twenty email and WhatsApp messages to and from ‘my’ Bali travel guru. My hotel booking in Jakarta was no less difficult as it had to be one that ‘catered’ for isolation. I rose early and rushed for my phone. My PCR test results from the pathology lab had to arrive that day. I saw an email from the lab and opened it with one eye, hardly daring to read it. The other eye flew open, Phew! It was negative. I’d been a little worried as I was slightly sniffly with a scratchy throat. I tried to send the results to the Bali ‘Travel passport’ or PeduliLindungi app but it wasn’t uploading. This wasn’t good — I needed it for the airport on arrival. In desperation I phoned a Pathcare travel doctor. He was short and rude (I mean, he might have been be six feet tall, but he spoke gruffly in monosyllables). He agreed to upload the results for a few hundred blue notes. 

It was very dark when I reluctantly peeled back the bedclothes as we were in the middle of yet another power outage. These occurred (at that time) in South Africa throughout the week on a daily basis. I dressed by feel and hoped I wasn’t wearing anything inside-out then staggered down the stairs by the light of my phone with my suitcase.

After a cup of tea, (without which I was going nowhere) using the last of my gas, I fed Fish n Chips and tried to give them a hug but they wriggled out of my clutches and scarpered. Having donned my Mexican ‘day of the dead’ face mask, I stood at the gate and waited for my uber. 

Peter, the British driver I’d pre-ordered arrived at 5:20. He was a chatty fellow and told me of his previous life working 9–5 in the UK. He said he'd worked for the film industry and ferried film crew and famous actors. I pressed some blue cracklies into his palm and entered the airport which was quiet due to the early hour and the fact that very few people were travelling during covid. Those who were, all wore masks of varying degrees of effectiveness (most lay just under the nose or chin — they may as well have attached a damp serviette for all the protection they were getting). In Joburg airport, I had to collect my suitcase which was about 5 km away but easy to identify once I’d walked the length of the huge airport. Check-in took ages as they queried  flights, destination, reason for travelling, visas, Covid test results, my stay in Jakarta, my stay in Bali and my hotel information. I entrusted my huge suitcase to the airline and walked with a much lighter step to the other side of the airport again to where my flight would take off. I was pretty sure I walked the requisite 10,000 steps in the airport alone.

My seat was the last one  at the furthest point in the huge B777 but at least no one sat next to me, thanks to covid restrictions. As we were taxiing down the runway I heard the guy in the next aisle chatting loudly on his phone. I glared at him several times but he couldnt hear over the sound of his own voice, then another man in the seat in front started a loud conversation. I grabbed a passing stewardess  to ask them to get off their phones. Travelling during a pandemic had made me nervous. She raised a finely pencilled brow but made an announcement on the PA for all passengers to get off their goddamn phones, or words to that effect. They knew it was me who told teacher so I was glared at by both. They were both wearing their masks on their chins which annoyed me even more, (I hadn’t enjoyed my usual eight hours of sleep, remember,) so I pointed to my mask and glared back. So having won ‘Miss popularity’ in the space of a few minutes I was thinking my food would be spat on (I probably deserved it for being a Karen, [sorry Karen]).

The plane looked new and was fairly spacious, even in cattle class. Of course we were not allowed to sit next to each other as an extra covid ‘protection’ which was great news in my opinion. Ah the joys of travelling in a pandemic; we had to wear our masks the entire flight (bar eating). I lay down on the remaining empty seats and tried to sleep but the eye mask kept slipping and I felt claustrophobic with the other mask over my mouth and nose, so eventually I gave up and sat up again. 

Later I found one of the sloping metal seats that abound in airports designed to make you wish you’d never thought of travelling and gazed around the mostly empty lounge. Hazmat suits seemed to be all the rage in Doha as I saw no less than five people wearing them. It was quite warm, (28°C) even at midnight and  seemed to take hours before it was time to board the next plane to Jakarta. I was beyond tired and shuffled onto the Airbus to the back seat. This time I had two companions unfortunately and it was really cramped which kind of negated all the precautions taken earlier. I had tea for ‘supper’ or whatever a meal at 3am constitutes. After a few hours of cramped misery, a kind steward came and asked if I’d like to move up to the front where there was more leg room. I was so grateful and hoped my eyes crinkled sufficiently to let him know I was smiling at him. This seat was much better and my nearest neighbour was two seats away. And so the night passed with intermittent dozing, waking and wee-ing. The overhead lights snapped on suddenly at 7:15, South African time (midday local time) and we were given our trays of breakfast. My stomach was rebelling against eating again so I had tea, coffee and water. The steward said he liked my mask andd handed me an extra packet of biscuits.

We disembarked at Soekarno-Hatta airport in Jakarta and I immediately regretted it. The lounge was hot and airless as we shuffled towards the baggage area to collect our suitcases. Halfway there we were stopped by an officious uniformed man who barked orders at us in Indonesian as though we were a group of prisoners instead of paying customers. Only a few of us understood what he was saying and we shuffled around uncertainly. He pointed to rows of plastic chairs and said what must have been, ‘Sit!’ We all sat and looked around in bewilderment. No one knew what was happening or why. I just figured it had something to do with Covid protocol. I received text messages with the request to send several selfie photos to both the hotel and the visa dude. I snapped quickly and sent each a disgruntled expression — well my eyebrows were definitely not gruntled! 

And so we sat and waited in sleep-deprived stupor waiting to be told what to do next. An hour and a half passed before I shoved several papers at some uniform after which he led me to a makeshift cubicle to wait some more. I learnt twenty minutes later that I was to suffer yet another covid test. Several tubes were shoved up my nostrils and down to my stomach (Ok it just felt that far), till I obliged with a gag reflex. I got up with watery eyes and followed his finger to yet another seat where I guessed I was to await the results of my test. I messaged my hotel sponsor to let them know I had arrived but was stuck at the airport. An hour and a half later I was met by a hotel representative. He took my passport and went in search of my results. At least he spoke the language I was familiar with. I found a stall selling coffee and took my mug back to my seat. Much later,  the hotel rep returned with my passport and test results which were negative (I was unsurprised; they were negative before boarding so why would this tradition not continue?)

He led me to the luggage area as I was incapable of independent action by then. After locating my giant overweight bag, I was led to yet another seat and told to wait. Raising bloodshot eyes slowly in disbelief, I muttered I’d done nothing but wait for the better part of two days, what’s another day or two. He disappeared and I sat slumped in my plastic chair for another forty minutes. It had been three hours since we arrived in Jakarta and I already wanted to leave on the next plane, except that would have meant another few days without sleep and I’d already forgotten who I was. Eventually my visa chap pitched up and I rose hopefully but he just wanted another photo of me. By then they could have papered the walls with photos of a slowly deteriorating red-eyed female. He left and I moved my sore, pink-rimmed eyeballs to my left where two girls were sitting. We had a muffled chat through our masks and I found out they were also going to Bali after Jakarta. They were from the Netherlands. We would share a taxi to the hotel so when the time eventually came, we left the confines of the airport into the grey evening light. They were going to a different hotel so the taxi deposited me and  my giant red case onto the pavement before zooming off. I was left breathing in hot, humid air through my mask and grabbed my suitcase preparing to enter the hotel. Before I got any further, a uniformed security guard stopped me and asked me to spread my legs. Before I could ask why, I was sprayed from top to bottom with some sort of insecticide. I received a mouthful of spray through my soggy mask before being ushered briskly around the side of the hotel like some leprous beggar. Well I use the word brisk but in my case I moved like someone that has woken up from a bad dream to find out the reality is worse. I was pushed into a lift to the 9th floor and deposited into a small hallway between hotel rooms where I moved uncertainly towards a uniformed woman wearing two masks and a headscarf. She was seated at a wooden desk and gestured towards the empty chair in front of her where I sat swaying with exhaustion while she looked through my file of documents before shuffling them into a neat pile. She reached for a three-page questionnaire and asked me a hundred muffled questions regarding my travel plans, interspersed with the proffering of corresponding documentation then stood abruptly, leaving me to gaze through painfull eyeballs for the next hour of probing. I felt confident now, should I at any stage in the future find myself at the opposite end of a Russian interrogation, that I’d do okay after this two-day nightmare. She returned thirty minutes later and motioned me to follow her. I was certain I was being led to a cell for some misdemeanour or other but she stopped outside a numbered door and swiped the keycard with a flourish.

‘Don’t leave room for any reason,’ she barked and trotted off down the corridor.

The room was standard hotel; two single beds, a desk and chair and en-suite bathroom. I glanced at the mirror and saw someone who looked like she needed urgent medical attention. The bathroom was situated behind a big clear window, presumably for those odd couples who enjoy watching their spouse on the toilet. It was already 11pm local time but I decided to have a cup of tea. I had a lovely hot shower and phoned down for some milk. A small jug came up on a tray with a masked man who didn’t even hand me the tray but set it down on a small wooden table outside my room. I think I unpacked a few things but must have fallen asleep on top of the bed fully clothed.

Something had woken me. It was dark so after stumbling to the bathroom I switched on the light then hastily switched it off again because water was gushing out of the light fixture and I was pretty sure electricity and water didn’t mix well.

I dialled reception and said water was coming out of my bathroom light then glanced at my watch. It was 1:30am and only two hours’ sleep in three days. I must not have emphasised the ‘hurry up’ bit because just as I was dozing off an hour later, someone knocked on my door. I was met with two men dressed from head to toe in white. They were wearing masks over their already covered heads and faces. News of my leprosy must have travelled. The hazmats moved towards the bathroom and I crawled into bed. They were there till 3am banging and chatting. They said something to the effect that the light was fixed and closed the door behind them. I was beyond tired by then so read my book until 4:30 am. After all I wasn’t going anywhere for three days.

I woke to the sound of the phone ringing shrilly next to me on the bedside table and leant over mumbling, ‘Hullo?’. It was room service and they told me that it was now 10:30 and I had not eaten my breakfast. The woman said my food was outside the door and I was only to collect it and not leave the room. She asked me what time I’d like lunch. Given that I was about to eat breakfast at 11:30, I asked for 2pm. I mumbled my thanks though in reality I was annoyed. Throwing on a long, floaty top, I opened the door and peered out cautiously. To my left was the small metal table with a tray. I grabbed it and closed the door quickly lest I was being watched for any transgression. I lifted the cover and peered at a semi-congealed omelette, some rubbery toast and an unidentifiable soup. After a few bites I re-covered the food and decided I needed a strong cup of tea more than I needed indigestible food. After a good cuppa using my own tea bags, I spent the rest of the day packing and repacking my huge suitcase in the hope I could find space for eight bars of chocolate I’d brought for a friend of Tim’s who was craving Turkish delight. I squeezed two bars in but had to leave the others for my already bulging backpack. The hours passed slowly as I answered email and WhatsApp messages, watched a programme or two on the telly about growing tomatoes and twenty ways to cook chicken. I was desperate to look outside, but my room had no balcony and the windows had been sealed so I plonked a moisturising  mask on my face in the hope it could erase some of the results of my sleepless nights. Lunch arrived at 2 and I brought in a more appetising plate of food. I’d asked for some laundry to be done and at 3pm a soft knock at the door announced my clean, neatly folded clothes. The young masked girl motioned for me to stand clutching my clean laundry while she took a photograph. I was wondering whether to smile or look suitably serious as this ‘proof of delivery’ took place. I opted for a sort of grimace in the end, thanked her and closed the door. Read some more, watched a wildlife programme on telly until 9pm and supper had come and gone. I was feeling tired so climbed into bed and prepared for a better night’s sleep. The second day went pretty much the same as the first except again I was startled into wakefulness by a loud knock. I grabbed a gown and stumbled to the door to be greeted by two hazmat men, a nurse heavily masked and for some reason an armed guard. I was seriously beginning to feel like a dangerous prisoner by then. I glanced at my watch, it was 1am! The nurse told me in halting English that she was going to test me for covid again and I stood unceremoniously in the doorway overlooked by three people as the nurse assaulted my nostrils with a long tube until I gagged. They left as quickly as they arrived and I closed the door. It’s hard to remain sleepy when my nostrils had recently had a pipe shoved into my oesophagus so I left the light on, reached for my laptop and booked my flight to Bali. I watched another two episodes of my Korean series and fell asleep around 4am. 
At 10:30 I fetched my breakfast tray, knowing it had been there since 7am. I lifted the lid and gazed at a plate full of carbs. White bread, a pancake and two small croissants. I ate the croissants with my tea then plonked the tray back outside and messaged Tim. We discussed my upcoming flight and noticed a discrepancy with times. Seems I was leaving at 4pm not 6pm which immediately increased my anxiety. I phoned reception and asked if my PCR results had come through yet. She said they had but they were waiting for, ‘the government’ to prepare the necessary documentation to that effect.

I was panicking as it was already midday and I had to leave for the airport by 2pm at the latest. I began shoving items around my case and repacking stuff mostly to pass the time. At last another knock on the door announced that I was covid free and it was time to leave the room I’d been in for three days (I was lucky; a few weeks earlier, the minimum time spent in isolation was a week to ten days.) I left with my case, rucksack and an extra bag holding things that could not be squeezed in anywhere without changing their composition. In the same foyer as before, I was told to wait (no use asking why).  The receptionist presented me with my passport and an official letter typed in Indonesian that informed anyone who was interested, (and so far there had been many,) that I was free of the dreaded Covid virus. She asked me to stand and hold the passport and official document in front of me while she took a photo. There must have been at least eight images of me holding various objects floating around Jakarta by then. I was really anxious to get going now they’d photographed me doing everything except leaving the hotel. They summoned a taxi and I hopped in,

‘Airport, quickly please.’ and off we went. As the taxi driver tried to enter the busy main road, he flung his hand out of the window waving a hundred Rupiah note. A man appeared from the side of the road and marched into the busy traffic with his hand up. I was surprised to see cars actually slowing down sufficiently for the taxi to join the swarm of traffic. This dude ought to get danger pay though. I wasn’t looking forward to my return to the airport.

We arrived at 2pm and I leapt out of the taxi. He retrieved my huge case from the boot and asked me for 500,000 Indonesian Rupiah. I looked at him in disbelief.

‘Hotel pay.’ I told him. He shook his head, ‘No, No pay.’ I was in a quandary. I knew that I’d paid for an all-inclusive ‘Isolation package’ but he was now holding my suitcase hostage.

‘I don’t have Indonesian money,’ I told him truthfully.

He replied, ‘ATM inside.’ and pointed to the airport doors. I tried to argue with him but he wasn’t budging and it was getting later.  In the end I rushed inside and tried to find an ATM. I had no idea of the actual value of all the notes that I withdrew but the numbers were followed by a lot of zeros. I panted back outside and thrust a note at him. He looked at it disdainfully and gestured at the remaining notes I was clutching. I peeled off another IR100,000. He pointed to a note with the legend IR 50,000 so I thrust it at him,  grabbed my case out of his hand and rushed inside. The airport was uncomfortably full and I felt anxious. I pushed ahead murmuring apologies and muttering that I was going to miss my plane.

I discovered my suitcase was now underweight by 2 kilos and I was angry with myself because now I was lugging around an extra heavy bag as hand luggage. For some reason I was given a different ticket and boarding pass to the one I already had.

I wasn’t sure if I was in the right place for boarding, so after some indecision, I rushed over to another waiting room and asked if it was the correct one. The officials just stared at me rudely or shook their heads. I decided to just wait it out and hope for the best. By the time the call to board came, we were two hours past take-off and I was hot and grumpy. As a result I walked past my allocated seat to almost the end of the plane then had to try and find my way back to the seat like a salmon swimming upstream. I found a tiny seat next to a window (I’d asked for an aisle seat) and was joined by a petite woman sporting the same colour hair as me. I was annoyed to see her hair was long and sleek with nary a frizz in sight. Bet hers wasn’t an expensive Brazilian either. We all sat in the small stuffy plane with our masks on waiting for take-off which only occurred forty minutes later. I had absolutely no idea what the time was as I’d crossed three time zones already and was about to cross another. I was rather hoping food would arrive soon as I hadn’t eaten since my two small croissants early that morning but nothing was forthcoming then mentally added hypoglycaemia to my list of woes. The slim woman in the next seat opened a packet of nuts and my stomach gurgled. I was saved from passing out by unintentionally knocking them out of her hand and grabbing a few from the floor on my way up. She had seen me gulping down three of her nuts and turned away in her seat to slide  the rest of her crispy, salted nuts down her slim gullet.
I gazed out of the small window as we approached the runway at Denpasar airport, Bali.

I was feeling excited now, soon to see my Tim after a year and a half of lockdown. After finding my luggage without trouble I walked through after Customs to find my precious boy waiting for me. A long hug then Tim took my case and wheeled it outside for a taxi. The heat was intense and I was already peeling off layers. Tim haggled a good price for a taxi, I slid in and smiled at him, having lowered my mask.

‘Thought I’d never get here.’ I told him giving him another hug. I pulled back and gazed at him again,

‘Hmm, You’ve grown a moustache.’

I knew he’d grown one but this comment was meant to convey my dislike for it. Tim ignored the comment. He said he'd come on his scooter, so spoke to the taxi driver in Indonesian, waved and left. I was impressed. Granted he had spent two years of lockdown here in Bali, but to be able to give instructions in Indonesian was impressive. My hotel in Uluwatu was some forty km away. I was too tired to formulate any type of conversation so the drive was quick and quiet. Tim had already paid him so after he  departed, I stood for a minute. I didn’t see an actual sign but the driver said the small bamboo decorated entrance was the entrance to my hotel. I descended a flight of white painted steps, the end of which lay a large sparkling turquoise infinity pool. A young Indonesian man greeted me from a small reception area to the left. We exchanged the necessary paperwork and he led me to my room; an A-shaped thatch-roofed room. Inside was a huge bed in the centre of the round room, surrounded by mosquito netting. A large stone walk-in shower and toilet led off the bedroom. It looked delightful. Tim arrived shortly afterwards on his bike. He'd brought a takeaway meal for us which we ate sitting upstairs on the wide stone balcony overlooking the pool. The meal was a local dish; hot and spicy. Tim and I caught up till about 10:30pm. He left me to unpack, shower and fall gratefully into bed.

## Uluwatu

I jumped out of bed when I saw it was already 8:30am. I had slept 7 and a half hours. The air conditioner was deliciously cool but when I took my tea outside, the intense heat hit me like a wet towel. Tim messaged to say he was waiting at the top so I grabbed my bag and raced as fast as I could up the somewhat slippery white stone steps to the entrance. Tim asked me to climb on the back of his bike. I tried a few times till I was sufficiently stable. It must've been difficult for him to keep the bike steady while I clambered on and shuffled around, flattening the back tyre substantially in the process. We were going for coffee and he told me to have my face mask ready but that they were less strict about protocol there in Uluwatu. I looked around at the lush forest as we motored past on a narrow road.

It helped to cool me down as the wind flowed over my hot face. We arrived at a place called BGS. I climbed off while Tim steadied the bike. 
We caught up on life on the island since lockdown. He said he was lucky to have made some really good friends, as some of the very few westerners to stay behind after lockdown. They called themselves “The Bali Survivors”. I hoped to meet some of them while I was there. A couple of young, tanned girls at the next table leant over and told me they liked my mask. Instead of the mouth-to-nose paper mask that most people wore, this one hung below my chin with loops hooked over my ears. The painted face, à la ‘Mexican Day of the Dead’, matched my features perfectly, so it looked as though I was wearing a full mask.
After coffee, I climbed on the back of Tim’s bike and we headed off to a place called Mana. It was built overlooking the sea from a cliff. En route, we stopped at a local temple; Uluwatu Temple was a respected Balinese Hindu Sea Temple, regarded as one of the Sad Kahyangan (not Sad as in unhappy), six sanctuaries of the world. It was a holy place of worship and according to Balinese beliefs, one of six pivotal points on the island which provided spiritual balance to Bali. We walked along the cliff edge. There were many red blossom trees and macaque monkeys scuttling around the trees. Two local Indonesian women were sitting near the temple weaving colourful bracelets from yarn. Tim stopped and chatted a little to them in Indonesian. He asked how much for the bracelets;

‘Selamat pagi. Berapa harga gelang-gelang ini?’

They spoke Balinese there in Bali, although Indonesian was also spoken and understood by most. I bought four bracelets and was given two for free. A huge statue depicting some deity rose from the top of the forest like a scary character from a children’s storybook. Tim was wearing a small purple cloth around his waist in deference to the locals whilst in a temple. We walked back to the scooter and rode to Mana; a huge open-plan restaurant, open on three sides with a long counter against one wall. We found a seat at the edge so we got an offshore breeze. Tim told me I must try the coconuts and ordered,

‘Tolong dua buah kelapa,’ and two shaved coconuts arrived with the tops lopped off with a straw.
Tim said he was taking me to his favourite spa for a massage. As I alighted from his bike, I found a small square of raffia on the step and bent down to pick it up. Tim stopped me.

‘That is an offering,’ he told me, ‘You shouldn’t pick it up.’
Every morning, Balinese women collected colourful flower heads and placed these on the small square of raffia together with some rice, bits of fruit, incense, sweets and sometimes a cigarette. These offerings were called ‘canang sari’ and formed a sacred act of gratitude to the Gods. The religion in Bali was mostly Hindu, although there were some people from other islands, such as Jakarta, who were Muslim. I thought it was delightful and so colourful. There were a lot of stray Bali dogs lying around or sniffing at the offerings which often provided a morsel of sustenance. I watched anxiously as motorbikes deftly swerved past them. The dogs were mostly the ‘Kintamani’ breed; medium in size, friendly, affectionate and very independent. Some dogs just lay in the middle of the road, and I wondered how more weren’t killed, as hundreds of motorbikes and scooters whizzed by on the busy roads every day.
At the spa, we were greeted in Balinese,
‘Om Suwastiastu,’ with hands held prayer-like in front of the face by a pretty, petite woman wearing the traditional Balinese dress; a tight-fitting, jacket-style top (usually of a white, lacy material) over a long skirt which was wrapped around the body. A cloth belt was tied around their middle. They often wore flowers in their long hair which was tied back into a small bun.
Tim and I removed our shoes, placed them in a shoe box against one wall and were led over to some raised wooden box seats. A woman arrived with a basin of warm, petal-strewn water. She gestured gracefully to place our feet in the basin while she gently washed them and I giggled at the ticklish bits.
Tim arrived on his scooter and we headed straight to breakfast. One was spoilt for choice in Bali; the food was fresh and delicious everywhere you went. Later I took a wander around. It was a bit hairy walking around there as there were absolutely no pavements until you got to the shopping area, so you were constantly within a whisker of passing cars, lorries and bikes. I looked at the many small shrines along the way, all with the prerequisite offerings. Tim picked me up and we raced down to the lookout point overlooking the beach, clambered over a grassy area to the top of the huge white cliffs and waited for sunset. Tim had brought us each a can of Bintang beer, so we sat watching the most spectacular sunset I’d ever seen. Every shade of red, orange and yellow washed the sky in vibrant colour that slowly deepened and darkened. I took a hundred photos. A ‘warung’ was a small, family-owned eatery that served affordable, traditional Indonesian or Balinese food, often in a casual and simple setting.
Our small wooden table faced a small, cracked basin and tap attached to a crumbling wall. Seven containers of food were kept warm at the counter and these were scooped onto a big plate. We sat outside in the still humid evening and ate fried chicken and vegetables. In bed, I heard what was becoming a familiar sound that came from the roof.
Tim was already waiting for me, so I flung myself on the back of his scooter making the bike wobble. ‘Sorry,’ I mumbled before zooming off. Drifters was a hippie surfer shop with a large retail section selling clothes and surfing requirements in the front and a restaurant at the back. There were two tables indoors and quite a few outside. It had very cosy décor inside with big easy chairs, red woven cushions, plants, books and candles. We ordered a smoothie bowl, and twenty minutes later the most delicious bowl of goodness arrived. It had fresh berries, dragon fruit, mango, coconut cream, chia seeds, banana and honey. It was as tasty as it was colourful. I ventured off to find the loo, which was outside, and chatted to a family from Boston en route. The man wearing a stetson and a wide smile started the conversation by asking if I was married. He said it was his first visit to Bali to see his son (with a nod in his direction) and I told him I was also visiting my son. I gathered the young woman with long dark hair who was scrolling endlessly on her phone was his son’s girlfriend. The toilet door opened and I left the conversation with a short, ‘nicetomeetyou’. After finishing the delicious smoothie bowl, I wandered around the shop and bought a few things to take home as presents.
From here, we set off towards the Garuda Wisnu Kencana Cultural Park or GWK. It was about forty minutes away so I clung on to Tim and looked round at the passing forest. At the entrance we donned our masks again. Tim brought another helmet for me. It had an annoying visor that kept slipping over my eyes but I appreciated the extra safety. At the desk, we showed our health status cards (mine was on my PeduliLindungi app). The tickets were much cheaper then with Covid — another dubious ‘plus’ with the pandemic, though obviously not for the Balinese. We hopped on an open-top bus and rode to the main entrance, then walked towards the huge statue of Vishnu that you could see from as far away as Canggu on a clear day. Sandstone statues lined the inside wall which comprised a long room with exhibits on either side. A series of rather gruesome-looking models depicted Batara Kala, the god of death and also creator of light and the earth. Being the god of time, he also ‘devoured’ people which was one way of looking at it I guessed. The creatures looked fierce with terrifying expressions and blood-red eyes. Snakes featured prominently.
After a short wait, the same open bus returned to where we had left the bike. I hopped on and we set off, my visor banging down every few minutes and obstructing my view as it was dusty and scratched. I met Tim’s German friend, Mel and her mum. Mel had long blonde hair and a wide smile; she was gorgeous. Her mum was small and blonde with a pleasant open face. She spoke very little English, so there were a lot of smiles between us but not much in the way of conversation. Tim’s other long-time friend, Elena, arrived. She had pale green hair with a pink fringe (that is to say, at the moment, later on as I got to know her, she would sport red, blue, purple and pink hair). She was from Helsinki and worked as a translator and knew Tim from their travels around Europe a few years ago. I told her that I had brought a pile of chocolate for her as per her request and she was delighted. It was a very pleasant evening with lots of laughter; I really liked Tim’s friends. I fell asleep after 11:30 p.m. with the sound of ‘Geck-Ko!’ in my ears.
Gojek was the local mode of transport there and on the app, you could order a bike, a car or food. It was a cheap way of getting around if you didn’t have a motorcycle or scooter. I asked the receptionist about the loud noise I heard in my room every night.

‘It sounds like “gecko”,’ I told him. He laughed,

‘That’s because it IS a gecko!’
Apparently, the Tokay geckos in Bali were big and noisy, announcing themselves to potential partners with the sound that gave them their name. My coffee arrived on a small green scooter with a friendly driver. I handed him a few extra Indonesian Rupiah and sat at the top verandah overlooking the valley sipping delicious coffee. I walked down to meet Tim at Drifters. It took half an hour to walk there, which was just long enough without dying in the humidity. Since the beginning of lockdown, working from laptops had become a common sight for those who could and the room was full of bent heads and flying fingers.

Time was fast running out for my stay in Bali. I ordered a Grab rather than have some poor little Balinese guy chugging up the steep hills with me and my heavy rucksack on my back. Back at Le Bamboo, I continued repacking and shoving things around. Tim and I spent a few hours in the beautiful infinity pool. Suddenly, without warning, it poured down so we waited it out.
An English guy wandered up to reception and asked the rather simple night receptionist;

‘Hello. Can I order breakfast here?’
The receptionist replied somewhat enigmatically,
‘My friend.’ so the Englishman said,

‘You will give me your friend for breakfast?’ I smiled into my mug but felt the Englishman would fare worse if he tried speaking Indonesian.

I had a supper and chat with Tim about his plans (vague), his state of mind, (fuzzy), and his friends (many), then he took me to the Isitara Spa Resort. It was situated on the top of a cliffside overlooking the sea. I was feeling a little apprehensive. This spa offered steam rooms and ice baths. I was to try both he said. As we entered the opening to the spa, we were immediately surrounded by ‘The tanned, the bold and the beautiful’. Lithe bodies strolled by in various stages of undress and I clutched my towel closer to my pale stomach. After showering Tim showed me a steam room which was extremely hot, dark and yes, steamy! My hair immediately shrivelled up and bushed out unbecomingly. I stood in the doorway feeling very claustrophobic and tried to breathe. Every time I took a breath, the hot steam burned my nostril hairs. It was very uncomfortable and I couldn’t believe the calm state of the others (those that I could see). I kept wanting to run outside but persevered for ten to fifteen minutes then stumbled outside coughing. I showered then followed Tim to a round structure that is set in the ground. There are small slippery steps down one side down where one descends into the ice water. Literally water and ice cubes! To make sure this scenario was as uncomfortable for me as it could possibly be, there were three young, tanned, attractive men already sitting in the small tub. They glanced over with mild interest as I removed my towel and gently lowered myself onto the first step. I smiled nervously at them (may have been a grimace as my feet made contact with the first ice floes). Tim was encouraging me from behind. I took another cautious step, then another. Eventually my nether regions were submerged and I let out a gasp as all feeling left my body. Tim climbed in and seated himself up to his neck. I lowered myself some more but found breathing difficult. I semi crouched, teeth chattering wildly but just couldn’t bring myself to totally submerge. I gasped and wheezed and chattered for no more than four minutes before standing up in defeat. I left Tim to turn into an ice cube and clambered out with difficulty. It was surprisingly hard to do when you couldn’t feel your feet. By now my brain had numbed in sympathy to the rest of me so another five minutes in the steam room and I was done. I dried off and dressed and joined Tim on the vast lawn where quite a few people were also lying around. There were a number of what looked like gravestones dotted around but these were to rest your back against apparently, rather than the remains of frozen people. A huge fireplace sat in the centre of the lawn but a sudden gust of wind sent smoke everywhere. I lay down and gazed up at the sky which was slowly turning from blue to violet to bright orange as the sun set over the cliff tops.

I woke early feeling good after a good sleep; I was sure the steam and ice helped. I made tea and joined Tim upstairs before setting off for the local hospital for my covid test. A prerequisite before flying. The process for Covid testing was fairly straightforward. I registered at the front desk while Tim had an Antigen test, then walked to the second floor for the insertion of the long swab down my nose and throat. We waited a short time at reception for Tim’s results. Mine would come later.

I decided to have a last massage and we were lucky enough to find a good spa for half price. Tim opted for acupressure and massage and I asked for full Bali massage with a body scrub. The masseuse applied the scrub liberally over my entire body.
I was left rolled up like a veggie wrap for fifteen minutes before showering and this was followed by a warm bath littered with some local leaves (she gave me a name but I couldn’t remember what she said), twigs, roots and herbs completing the contents. I self-flagellated for a few minutes as suggested, using the long twigs that were left on a chair then she brought me a lovely hot rosehip tea. Thus well oiled and pummelled, I met up with an equally relaxed looking Tim and we set off back to Le Bamboo.

I was feeling sad because it was my last evening in Bali and the sunset was spectacular. After watching the last of the sun’s rays melt into a velvet sky we went for a last swim. The rest of Uluwatu twinkled in the evening light, the pool was still warm and the moon threw a wobbly image onto the rippling water.

I locked my bulging suitcase and dragged it and a bag of dirty laundry of Tim’s up to reception where I tried again to pay with my credit card. Tim arrived at 6 just as his taxi came into view. They were off to Gili island that day. I gave him a big hug and with a lump in my throat waved them off. I ordered two almond lattes to go from The Bakery and kept trying to pay for my accommodation and almost gave up on it and my coffee as the Gojek arrived minutes before my taxi. I grabbed my lukewarm coffee to slurp in the back of the taxi and said goodbye to Le Bamboo. My driver was happy to hear my results, that had just come through, were negative for Covid.
The process for boarding Air Asia for Jakarta was uneventful except — somewhat annoyingly, my suitcase only weighed 15kg! I could have put in an extra five.
The next miracle occurred mid-flight when I found I was able to buy airtime. More miraculous is that I was then able to pay for my hotel whilst 33,000 feet above earth! Technology never ceases to amaze.
