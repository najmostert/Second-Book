# Chapter 48: INDONESIA 2021 — Jakarta

Traveling during a pandemic was always going to be different and leaving for Indonesia in the middle of one is no exception.
The paperwork for travel is double the usual amount as it includes PCR tests (taken at just the right time for the number of hours before and after arrival in Jakarta, otherwise I would not be able to either leave or arrive as it were), special travel visas (as motivation for travel), twenty email and Whatsapp messages to and from ‘my’ Bali travel guru. My hotel booking in Jakarta is no less difficult as it has to be one that ‘caters’ for isolation. 
This morning I get up early and rush for my phone. My PCR test results from the pathology lab have to arrive today. I see there’s an email from them and open it with one eye, hardly daring to read it. The other eye flies open, Phew! It’s negative. I’ve been a little worried as I’m slightly sniffly with a scratchy throat. I try to send the results to my special Bali ‘Travel passport’ called the PeduliLindungi app but it’s not uploading. This isn’t good- I need it for the airport on arrival. In desperation I phone a Pathcare travel doctor. 
He is short and rude (I mean, he may be six feet tall, but he speaks gruffly in single word sentences). He says he’ll upload the results for a few hundred blue notes. 
It is pitch dark when I reluctantly peel back the bedclothes as we are in the middle of yet another power outage. These occur ( at this time) in South Africa throughout the week on a daily basis. I dress by feel and hope I’m not wearing anything inside-out then stagger down the stairs by the light of my phone with my suitcase.

After a cup of tea, (without which I’m going nowhere) using the last of my gas, I feed Fish n Chips and try to give them a last hug but they wriggle out of my clutches and scarper. Having donned my Mexican ‘day of the dead’ face mask, I stand at the gate and wait for my uber. Peter, the British driver I’d pre-ordered arrives at 5:20. He’s a chatty fellow and he tells me of his previous life working 9-5 in UK. He tells me he works for the Film industry and usually ferries film crew and famous actors around. We arrive at the airport and I press some blue cracklies into his palm and enter the airport which is quiet due to the early hour and the fact that very few people are travelling during covid. Those who are, all wear masks of varying degrees of effectiveness (most lie just under the nose or chin - they may as well attach a damp serviette to their chin for all the protection they’re getting). 
In Joburg airport, I have to collect my suitcase which is about 5 km away but easy to identify once I’ve walked the length of the huge airport.
Check in takes ages as they have to ask me about my flights, destination, reason for travelling, visas, Covid test results, my stay in Jakarta, my stay in Bali and my hotel information. I entrust my huge suitcase to the airline and walk with a much lighter step to the other side of the airport again to where my flight will take off. I’m sure I’ve walked the requisite 10,000 steps in the airport alone.

My seat is the last seat at the furtherest point in the huge B777 but at least no one sits next to me, thanks to covid restrictions. As we’re taxying down the runway I hear the guy in the next aisle chatting loudly on his phone. I glare at him several times but he either ignores me or doesn’t notice, then another man in the seat in front starts a loud conversation. I grab a passing stewardess and tell her to ask them to get off their phones. She raises a finely pencilled brow but makes an announcement on the PA for all passengers to get off their goddamn phones, or words to that effect. They know it’s me who told teacher so I get glared at by both. They’re both wearing their masks on their chins which annoys me even more, (I haven’t enjoyed my eight hours of sleep, remember,) so I point to my mask and glare again. So having won ‘Miss popularity’ in the space of a few minutes I’m thinking my food will be spat on (I probably deserve it for being a Karen, [sorry Karen]). 

The plane itself is really nice. It looks new and is fairly spacious, even in cattle class. Of course we are not allowed to sit next to each other as an extra covid ‘protection’ which is great news in my opinion.  Ah the joys of travelling in a pandemic; we have to wear our masks the entire time ( bar eating). I lie down on the remaining empty seats next to me and try to sleep but the eye mask keeps slipping and I feel claustrophobic with the other mask over my mouth and nose, so eventually I give up and sit up again. 
I find one of the sloping metal seats that abound in airports and are designed to make you wish you’d never thought of travelling then gaze around the mostly empty lounge. Seems Hazmat suits are all the rage in Doha as I see no less than five people wearing them. It’s quite warm, (28° C) even at midnight and it seems to take hours before its time to board the next plane to Jakarta. I’m beyond tired and shuffle onto the airbus to the back seat. This time I have two companions unfortunately and it's really cramped which kind of negates all the space I had earlier. I only have tea for ‘supper’ or whatever a meal at 3am constitutes. After a few hours of cramped misery, the kind Steward comes to me and asks if I’d like to move up to the front where there’s more leg room. I am so grateful and hope my eyes crinkle sufficiently to let him know I’m smiling at him.
This seat is much better and my nearest neighbour is two seats away. I’m at least able to doze for short periods of time, though I keep waking up with pins and needles in my hands and feet. And so the night passes with intermittent  dozing, waking and wee-ing. The overhead lights snap on suddenly at 7:15, South African time (midday local time) and we’re given our trays of breakfast. My stomach is rebelling against eating again so I have tea, coffee and water. The steward says he likes my mask and I get an extra packet of biscuits.

We disembark at Soekarno Hasta airport in Jakarta and I immediately regret it. The lounge is hot and airless as we shuffle towards the baggage area to collect our suitcases. Halfway there we’re stopped by an officious uniformed man who barks orders at us in Indonesian as though we’re a group of prisoners instead of paying customers. Only a few of us understand what he’s saying and we shuffle around uncertainly. He points to rows of plastic chairs and says what must be,’Sit!’ All passengers sit down on a plastic chair and look around in bewilderment. No one knows what’s happening or why. I just figure it has something to do with a Covid protocol. We’re requested to send several selfie photos to both the hotel and the visa dude. I snap quickly and send each a disgruntled expression- well the eyebrows are definitely not gruntled!
And so we sit and wait in sleep deprived stupor waiting to be told what to do next. An hour and a half passes before I shove several papers at some uniform after which he leads me to a makeshift cubicle where I sit and wait again. I learn twenty minutes later that I am to suffer yet another covid test. Several tubes are shoved up my nostrils and down to my stomach (Ok it just feels that far), till I oblige with a gag reflex. I get up with watery, red eyes and follow his finger to yet another seat where I guess I am to await the results of my test. I message my hotel sponsor to let them know I have arrived but stuck at the airport. An hour and a half later I’m met by a hotel representative. He takes my passport and goes in search of my results. At least he speaks the language I’m familiar with. I find a stall selling coffee and take my mug back to my seat. Much later, ( I am a total zombie by now) the hotel rep returns with my passport and test results which are negative (they were negative before boarding so why would this tradition not continue?) 

He leads me to the luggage area as I am incapable of independent action by now. I locate my giant overweight bag and I’m led to yet another seat and told to wait. I raise my bloodshot eyes slowly in disbelief, I’ve done nothing but wait for the better part of two days, what’s another day or two. He disappears and I sit slumped in my plastic chair for another forty minutes. It has been three hours since we arrived in Jakarta and I already want to leave on the next plane, except that would mean another few days without sleep and I’ve already forgotten who I am. 
Eventually my visa chap pitches up, and I rise hopefully but he just wants another photo of me. By now they can paper the walls with photos of a slowly deteriorating red-eyed female.  He leaves and I move my sore pink-rimmed eyeballs to my left where two girls are sitting. We have a muffled chat through our masks and I find out they are also going to Bali after Jakarta. They’re from the Netherlands. We will share a taxi to the hotel, so, when the time eventually comes, we leave the confines of the airport to the grey evening light outside. They are going to a different hotel so the taxi deposits me first outside the Novotel hotel and chucks my giant case onto the pavement and zooms off. I’m left breathing in hot, humid air through my mask and grab my suitcase preparing to enter the hotel doors. Before I get any further, a uniformed security guard stops me and asks me to spread my legs. 
Before I can ask why, I’m sprayed from top to bottom with some sort of insecticide. I receive a mouthful of spray through my soggy mask before being ushered briskly around the side of the hotel like some leperous beggar. Well I use the word brisk but in my case I move like someone that has woken up from a bad dream to find out the reality is worse. I’m pushed into a lift to the 9th floor and deposited into a small hallway between hotel rooms and wheel my case uncertainly towards a small uniformed woman wearing two masks. She is seated at a wooden desk. She gestures towards an empty chair in front of her. I sit swaying with exhaustion while she looks through my file of documents then shuffles them into a neat pile and reaches for a three page questionnaire. She asks me a hundred muffled questions regarding my travel plans, interspersed with the proffering of corresponding documentation then stands abruptly and leaves me to gaze through sore eyeballs for the next hour of probing. 
I feel confident now, should I at any stage in the future find myself at the opposite end of a Russian interrogation, that I’ll do okay after this two day nightmare.
After half an hour she returns and asks me to come with her. I’m certain I’m being led to a cell for some misdemeanour or other but she stops outside a numbered door which she opens with a flourish leading me into my hotel room. 

‘Don’t leave room for any reason’, she barks and trots off down the corridor.
 
The room is standard hotel; two single beds, a desk and chair and en suite bathroom. I glance at the mirror and see someone who looks like she needs urgent medical attention. There’s a TV and the shower has a big clear window, presumably for those odd couples who enjoy watching their spouse on the toilet. Its already 11pm local time but I decide to have a cup of tea. I have a lovely hot shower and phone down for some milk for my tea. No ways am I using the powdered milk they’ve provided. A small jug comes up on a tray by a masked man who doesn’t even hand me the tray but sets it down on a small wooden table outside my room. I think I unpack a few things but must have fallen asleep on top of the bed fully clothed. 

Something has woken me. It’s dark so after stumbling to the bathroom I switch on the light then hastily switch it off again because water is gushing out of the light fixture and I’m pretty sure electricity and water don’t mix well.

I get the bedside phone and look up the number for reception and tell the woman who answers that water is coming out of my bathroom light and they must hurry. I glance at my watch. It is 1:30am and only two hours sleep in three days. I must not have emphasised the ‘hurry up’ bit because just as I’m dozing off an hour later, someone knocks on my door. I’m met with two men dressed from head to toe in white. They are wearing masks over their already covered heads and faces. News of my leprosy must have travelled. The hazmats move towards the bathroom and I crawl into bed. They are here till 2:45 banging and chatting. They say something to the effect that the light is fixed and close the door behind them. I’m beyond tired by now so read my book until 4:30 am. After all I’m not going anywhere for three days!

I wake up to the sound of the phone ringing shrilly next to me on the bedside table. I lean over and mumble, ‘Hullo?’. It’s room service and they tell me that it is now 10:30 and I have not eaten my breakfast. The woman tells me my food is outside my door and I’m only to collect it and not leave the room. She asks me what time I’d like lunch. Given that I’m about to eat breakfast at 11:30, I ask for 2pm. I mumble my thanks though in reality I’m annoyed. Throwing on a long, floaty top, I open the door and peer out cautiously. To my left is the small metal table with a tray. I grab it and close the door quickly lest I’m being watched for any transgression. I lift the cover and peer at a semi congealed omelette, some rubbery toast and an unidentifiable soup. I take a few bites and re-cover the food and decide I need a strong cup of tea more than I need indigestible food. After a good cuppa using my own tea bags, I spend the rest of the day packing and repacking my huge suitcase in the hope I can find space for eight bars of chocolate I’ve brought for a friend of Tim’s who is craving Turkish delight. I squeeze two bars in but have to leave the others for my already bulging backpack. The hours pass slowly as I answer email and Whatsapp messages, watch a programme or two on the telly about growing tomatoes and twenty ways to cook chicken. I’m desperate to look outside, but my room has no balcony and the windows have been sealed. I plonk a moisturising face mask on my face in the hope it can erase some of the results of my sleepless nights and wash some under clothes in the bathroom then shove them over the curtain rail to dry, then read my book and do some puzzles on my iPad. Lunch arrives at 2 and I bring in a more appetising plate of food. 
I’d asked for some laundry to be done and at 3pm a soft knock at the door announces my clean, neatly folded clothes. The young masked girl motions for me to stand clutching my clean laundry while she takes a photograph. I’m wondering whether to smile or look suitably serious as this ‘proof of delivery’ takes place. I opt for a sort of grimace in the end, thank her and close the door. Read some more, watch a wild life programme on telly, and later a Korean series on my iPad until 9pm and supper has come and gone. I am feeling tired so climb into bed and prepare for a better night’s sleep. 
The second day goes pretty much the same as the first except again I’m startled into wakefulness by a loud knocking on my door. I grab a gown and stumble to the door. I’m greeted by two hazmat men, a nurse heavily masked and for some reason an armed guard. I am seriously beginning to feel like a dangerous prisoner by now.
I glance at my watch, it’s 1am! The nurse tells me in halting English that she is going to test me for covid again and I stand unceremoniously in the doorway overlooked by three people as the nurse assaults my nostrils with a long tube until I gag. They leave as quickly as they arrived and I close the door. It’s hard to remain sleepy when my nostrils have just had a pipe shoved into my oesophagus so I leave the light on, reach for my laptop and book my flight to Bali. I watch another two episodes of my Korean series and fall asleep around 4am.
I awaken at 10:30 and fetch my breakfast tray, knowing it has been there since 7am. I lift the lid and gaze at a plate full of carbs. There’s white bread, a pancake and two small croissants. I eat the croissants with my tea then plonk the tray back outside and message Tim. We discuss my upcoming flight and notice a discrepancy with times. Seems I’m leaving at 4pm not 6pm which immediately increases my anxiety. I phone reception and ask if my PCR results have come through yet. She says they have but they’re waiting for, ‘the government’ to prepare the necessary documentation to that effect.

I’m panicking as it’s already 11:45 and I have to leave for the airport by 2pm at the latest. I begin shoving items around my case and repacking stuff mostly to pass the time. At last another knock on the door announces that I’m covid free and it’s time to leave the room I’ve been in for the best part of three days (I’m lucky; a few weeks earlier, the minimum time spent in isolation was a week to ten days.) I stagger with my case, rucksack and extra bag holding things that could not be squeezed in anywhere without changing their composition. 
In the same foyer as before, I’m told to wait (no use asking why). I meet another European girl who is also leaving for Bali but on a later plane. The receptionist presents me with my passport and an official letter typed in Indonesian that informs anyone who is interested, (and so far there have been many,) that I’m free of the dreaded Covid virus. She asks me to stand and hold the passport and official document in front of me while she takes a photo. There must be at least eight photos of me holding various objects floating around Jakarta by now. I’m really anxious to get going now they’ve photographed me doing everything except leaving the hotel. They summons a taxi and I hop in, 

‘Airport, quickly please.’ and off we go. As the taxi driver tries to enter the busy main road, he flings his hand out of the window and waves a hundred Rupiah note. A man appears from the side of the road and he marches into the busy traffic with his hand up. I’m surprised to see cars actually slow down sufficiently enough for the taxi to join the swarm of traffic. This dude aught to get danger pay though. I’m not looking forward to my return to the airport. 

We arrive at 2pm and I leap out of the taxi. He retrieves my huge case from the boot and asks me for 500,000 Indonesian Rupiah. I look at him in disbelief. 

‘Hotel pay.’ I tell him. He shakes his head, ‘No, No pay.’ I’m in a quandary. I know that I’ve paid for an all inclusive ‘Isolation package’ but he’s now holding onto my suitcase.

‘ I don’t have Indonesian money’, I tell him truthfully. 

He says, ‘ATM inside.’ and points to the airport doors. 
I try and argue with him but he isn’t budging and it’s getting later. I still have to send my luggage on. In the end I rush inside and try to find an ATM. I have no idea of the actual value of all the notes that I withdraw are but the numbers have a lot of zeros at the end. I pant back outside and thrust a note at him. He looks at it disdainfully and gestures at the rest of my pile of notes that I’m clutching. I peel off another IR100,000. He points to a note with the legend IR 50,000 so I thrust it at him and grab my case out of his hand and rush inside. The airport is uncomfortably full and I feel anxious. I push ahead murmuring apologies and muttering that I am going to miss my plane.

I discover my suitcase is now underweight by 2 kilos and I’m angry with myself because now I’m lugging around an extra heavy bag as hand luggage. For some reason I’m given a different ticket and boarding pass to the one I already have. 

I’m not sure if I’m in the right place for boarding, so after an hour of indecision, I rush over to another waiting room and ask if it is the correct one. The officials  and just stare at me rudely or shake their head. I decide to just wait it out and hope for the best. By the time the call to board my plane comes, we are two hours past take off and I’m hot and grumpy. As a result I walk past my allocated seat to almost the end of the plane, then have to try and find my way back to the seat like a salmon swimming upstream. I find it’s a tiny seat next to a window (I’d asked for an aisle seat). I am joined by a petite woman sporting the same colour hair as me. I’m annoyed to see her hair is long and sleek with nary a frizz in sight. Bet hers isn’t an expensive Brazilian either.
We all sit in the small stuffy plane with our masks on waiting for take off which only occurs forty minutes later. I have absolutely no idea what the time is as I’ve crossed three time zones already and about to cross another. I’m rather hoping the food arrives soon as I haven’t eaten since my two small croissants early this morning but nothing is forthcoming. I mentally add hypoglycaemia to my list of woes. The slim woman in the next seat opens a packet of nuts and my stomach gurgles. I’m saved from passing out by un-intentionally knocking them out of her hand and grabbing a few from the floor on my way up. 
She has nevertheless seen my slobbering intention and turns away in her seat to slide all the crispy, salted nuts directly down her slim gullet. 
‘Cow!’ I mutter uncharitably and gaze out of the small window as we approach the runway at Denpasar airport, Bali. 

I’m feeling excited now. I’m soon to see my Tim after a year and a half of lockdown. After finding my luggage without trouble I walk through after Customs to find my precious boy waiting for me. A long hug then Tim takes my case and wheels it outside for a taxi. The heat is intense and I’m already peeling off layers. Tim haggles a good price for a taxi, I slide in and smile at him, having lowered my mask. 

‘ Thought I’d never get here.’ I tell him giving him another hug. I pull back and gaze at him again,

‘ Hmm, You’ve grown a moustache.’

I know he’s grown one but this comment is meant to convey my dislike for it. Tim ignores the comment. He says he came on his scooter, so speaks to the taxi driver in Indonesian, waves and leaves. I’m really impressed. Granted he has spent two years of lockdown here in Bali, but to be able to give instructions in Indonesian is impressive. Tim gave the driver directions to my hotel in Uluwatu, some forty km away. I’m too tired to formulate any type of conversation so the drive is quick and quiet. Tim has already paid him so after he leaves my suitcase next to me and departs, I stand for a minute. I don’t see an actual sign but the driver says the small bamboo decorated entrance is the entrance to my hotel. I descend a flight of white painted steps, the end of which is a large sparkling turquoise infinity pool. A small reception area lies to the left and a young Indonesian man greets me. We exchange the necessary paperwork and he leads me to my room which is off the pool area down a short path. It is an A-shaped thatch roofed room and looks quite lovely. Inside is a huge bed in the centre of the round room, surrounded by mosquito netting. Theres a large stone walk-in shower and toilet off the room. It is delightful! Tim arrives shortly afterwards on his bike. He has brought a take away meal for us which we eat sitting upstairs on the wide stone balcony overlooking the pool. It is very humid but there’s a fan blowing over us. The meal is a local dish which is hot and spicy. Tim and I catch up till about 10:30pm. He leaves me to unpack, shower and fall gratefully into bed.

ULUWATU

I jump out of bed when I see it is already 8:30am. I have slept 7 and a half hours. The air conditioner is deliciously cool  but when I take my cup of tea outside, the intense heat hits me like a wet towel. Tim messages me to say he is waiting at the top so I grab my bag and race as fast as I can up the somewhat slippery white stone steps to the entrance. Tim tells me to get on the back of his bike. I have a few tries till I’m sufficiently stable. It must be difficult for him to keep the bike steady while I clamber on and shuffle around, flattening the back tire substantially in the process. I put my arms round his waist and he sets off. We are going for coffee. He tells me to have my face mask ready but that they are less strict about protocol here in Uluwatu, which is situated South East of Bali in Pecatu. I look around at the lush forest as we motor past on a narrow road. 

It helps to cool me down as the wind flows over my hot face. We arrive at a place called BGS. I climb off while Tim steadies the bike. I tell him he rode well despite having me on the back. 
Tim and I catch up on life on the island since lockdown. He says he is lucky to have made some really good friends, as they are some of the very few westerners to stay behind after lockdown. They call themselves “The Bali Survivors”. I hope to meet some of them while I’m here. A couple of young, tanned girls at the next table lean over and tell me they like my mask. Instead of the mouth-to-nose paper mask that most people wear, this one hangs below my chin and two loops hook over my ears. The painted face, à la ‘Mexican Day of the Dead’, matches my features perfectly, so it looks as if I’m wearing a full mask. It’s hardly efficient as a virus deterrent though.
After coffee, I climb on the back of Tim’s bike and we head off to a place called Mana. It is built overlooking the sea from a cliff and is one of his favourite places. En route, we stop at a local temple; Uluwatu Temple is a respected Balinese Hindu Sea Temple and is regarded as one of the Sad Kahyangan (not Sad as in unhappy), six sanctuaries of the world. It's a holy place of worship and according to Balinese beliefs, it's one of six pivotal points on the island which provide spiritual balance to Bali. We walk along the cliff edge. There are many red blossom trees and macaque monkeys scuttling around the trees. Two local Indonesian women are sitting near the temple weaving colourful bracelets from yarn. Tim stops and chats a little to them in Indonesian. He asks how much for the bracelets;
‘Selamat pagi. Berapa harga gelang-gelang ini?’

They speak Balinese here in Bali, although Indonesian is also spoken and understood by most. I buy four bracelets and am given two for free. A huge statue depicting some deity rises from the top of the forest like a scary character from a children’s storybook. Tim is wearing a small purple cloth around his waist in deference to the locals whilst in a temple. We walk back to the scooter and ride to Mana; a huge open-plan restaurant, open on three sides with a long counter against one wall. We find a seat at the edge so we get an off shore breeze. Tim tells me I must try the coconuts and orders, 

‘Tolong dua buah kelapa,’  and two shaved coconuts arrive with the tops lopped off with a straw. 
Tim says he’s taking me to his favourite spa for a massage. As I alight from his bike, I find a small square of raffia on the step and bend down to pick it up. Tim stops me.

‘That is an offering,’ he tells me, ‘You shouldn’t pick it up.’ 
Every morning, Balinese women collect colourful flower heads and place these on the small square of raffia together with some rice, bits of fruit, incense, sweets and sometimes a cigarette. These offerings are called ‘canang sari’ and form a sacred act of gratitude to the Gods. The religion in Bali is mostly Hindu, although there are some people from other islands, such as Jakarta, who are Muslim. I think it’s delightful and so colourful. There are a lot of stray Bali dogs lying around or sniffing at the offerings which often provide a morsel of sustenance. I watch anxiously as motorbikes deftly swerve past them. The dogs are mostly the ‘Kintamani’ breed; medium in size, friendly, affectionate and very independent. Some dogs just lie in the middle of the road, and I wonder how more aren’t killed, as hundreds of motorbikes and scooters whizz by on the busy roads every day.
At the spa, we’re greeted in Balinese, 
‘Om Suwastiastu,’ with hands help prayer-like in front of the face by a pretty, petite woman wearing the traditional Balinese dress; a tight-fitting, jacket-style top (usually of a white, lacy material) over a long skirt which is wrapped around the body. A cloth belt is tied around their middle. They often wear flowers in their long hair which is tied back into a small bun. 
Tim and I remove our shoes, place them in a shoe box against one wall and are led over to some raised wooden box seats. A woman arrives with a basin of warm, petal-strewn water. She gestures gracefully to place our feet in the basin while she gently washes them and I giggle at the ticklish bits.
Tim arrives on his scooter and we head straight to breakfast. One is spoilt for choice in Bali; the food is fresh and delicious everywhere you go. Later I take a wander around. It is a bit hairy walking around here as there are absolutely no pavements until you get to the shopping area, so you’re constantly within a whisker of passing cars, lorries and bikes. I look at the many small shrines along the way, all with the prerequisite offerings. Tim picks me up and we race down to the lookout point overlooking the beach, clamber over a grassy area to the top of the huge white cliffs and wait for sunset. Tim has brought us each a can of Bintang beer, so we sit watching the most spectacular sunset I’ve ever seen. Every shade of red, orange and yellow washes the sky in vibrant colour that slowly deepens and darkens. I take a hundred photos. A ‘warung’  is a small, family-owned eatery that serves affordable, traditional Indonesian or Balinese food, often in a casual and simple setting.
Our small wooden table faces a small, cracked basin and tap attached to a crumbling wall. Seven containers of food are kept warm at the counter and these are scooped onto a big plate. We sit outside in the still humid evening and eat fried chicken and vegetables. In bed, I hear what is becoming a familiar sound that comes from the roof. 
Tim’s already waiting for me, so I fling myself on the back of his scooter making the bike wobble. ‘Sorry,’ I mumble before zooming off. Drifters is a hippie surfer shop with a large retail section selling clothes and surfing requirements in the front and a restaurant at the back. There are two tables indoors and quite a few outside. It has very cosy decor inside with big easy chairs, red woven cushions, plants, books and candles. We order a smoothie bowl, and twenty minutes later the most delicious bowl of goodness arrives. It has fresh berries, dragon fruit, mango, coconut cream, chia seeds, banana and honey. It is as tasty as it’s colourful. I venture off to find the loo, which is outside and chat to a family from Boston en route. The man wearing a stetson and a wide smile starts the conversation by asking if I’m married. He says it’s his first visit to Bali to see his son (with a nod in his direction) and I tell him I’m also visiting my son. I gather the young woman with long dark hair who’s scrolling endlessly on her phone is his son’s girlfriend. The toilet door opens and I leave the conversation with a short , ‘nicetomeetyou’. After finishing the delicious smoothie bowl, I wander around the shop and buy a few things to take home as presents.
From here, we set off towards the Garuda Wisnu Kencana Cultural Park or GWK. It’s about forty minutes away so I cling on to Tim and look round at the passing forest. At the entrance we don our masks again. Tim brings another helmet for me. It has an annoying visor that keeps slipping over my eyes but I appreciate the extra safety. At the desk, we show our health status cards (mine is on my PeduliLindungi app). The tickets are much cheaper now with Covid - another dubious ‘plus’ with the pandemic, though obviously not for the Balinese. We hop on an open-top bus and ride to the main entrance, then walk towards the huge statue of Vishnu that you can see from as far away as Canggu on a clear day. Sandstone statues line the inside wall which comprises a long room with exhibits on either side. A series of rather gruesome-looking models depict, Batara kala, the god of death and also creator of light and the earth. Being the god of time, he also ‘devours' people which is one way of looking at it I guess. The creatures look fierce with terrifying expressions and blood-red eyes. Snakes feature prominently.
After a short wait, the same open bus returns to where we have left the bike. I hop on t and we set off, my visor banging down every few minutes and obstructing my view as it is dusty and scratched. There are some local dishes as well the usual Christmas fare such as Jackfruit stew, dragonfruit slices and chilli sauces. Maggie the dog sits under the table and catches any stray morsel that has missed my mouth. A surprising amount of food seems to find its way into Maggie or onto my clothes. Much hilarity and laughter ensues as the night wears on and more wine is consumed. I stick to tea which means more fun watching others. Elina and Max entertain us with stories from their childhood from an American and Finnish perspective. Elina explains her ‘half a queen of hearts’ tattoo on her upper thigh. She climbs onto the table and lifts her shimmery dress to expose a leg so we can all see. The story goes, she was in Bulgaria and was invited to a party where each person was given half a playing card which you take with you as you wander round the room talking to people. The idea being, you’d eventually find the other half of your card. According to Elina, she spoke to every guy in the room but no one had the missing half of her card so she ended up going home with half of the Queen of hearts. She says she still has one half a card and decided to tattoo it onto her thigh ( the image, not the actual card).
I’m awake long before anyone else and pad over to the kitchen and make tea and sit at the pool. 
After a lazy afternoon chatting, and frolicking in the pool we leave on our respective motorbikes for Balangan beach in Jimbaran.  The sunset is amazingly colourful this evening. I watch a small family from Jakarta at the waves. (I’m guessing Jakarta as the women are all wearing traditional Islamic gear) The dad is asked to take hundreds of photos of his wife and daughters who pose endlessly after which each photo is posted onto Instagram. Just as it’s getting dark we all head back to the villa to watch a movie on Netflix. We watch ‘Don’t Look Up’  about a comet hitting earth so an excellent choice for a new year.

Time is fast running out for my stay in Bali. I order a Grab rather than have some poor little Balinese guy chugging up the steep hills with me and my heavy rucksack on my back.Back at Le Bamboo, I continue repacking and shoving things around. Tim and I spend a few hours in the beautiful infinity pool. Suddenly, without warning, it pours down so we wait it out.
An English guy wanders up to reception and asks the rather simple night receptionist;

'Hello. Can I order breakfast here?’ 
The receptionist replies somewhat enigmatically,
‘My friend.’ so the Englishman says, 
‘You will give me your friend for breakfast?' I smile into my mug but feel the Englishman would fare worse if he tried speaking Indonesian. 

I have a supper and chat with Tim about his plans (vague), his state of mind, (fuzzy), and his friends (many), then he takes me to the Istana Spa Resort. It’s situated on the top of a cliffside overlooking the sea. I’m feeling a little apprehensive. This spa offers steam rooms and ice baths. I am to try both he says. As we enter the opening to the spa, we’re immediately we’re surrounded by ‘The tanned, the bold and the beautiful’. Lithe bodies stroll by in various stages of undress and I clutch my towel closer to my pale stomach. After showering Tim shows me a steam room which is extremely hot, dark and yes, steamy! My hair immediately shrivels up and bushes out unbecomingly. I stand in the doorway feeling very claustrophobic and try to breathe. Every time I take a breath, the hot steam burns my nostril hairs. It is very uncomfortable and I can’t believe the calm state of the others (those that I can see). I keep wanting to run outside but persevere for ten to fifteen minutes then stumble outside coughing. I shower then follow Tim to a round structure that is set in the ground. There are small slippery steps down one side down where one descends into the ice water. Literally water and ice cubes! To make sure this scenario is as uncomfortable for me as it can possibly be, there are three young, tanned, attractive men already sitting in the small tub. They glance over with mild interest as I remove my towel and gently lower myself onto the first step. I smile nervously at them ( may have been a grimace as my feet make contact with the first ice floes). Tim is encouraging me from behind. I take another cautious step, then another. Eventually my nether regions are submerged and I let out a gasp as all feeling leaves my body. Tim climbs in and seats himself up to his neck. I lower myself some more but find breathing difficult. I semi crouch, teeth chattering wildly but just can’t bring myself to totally submerge. I gasp and wheeze and chatter for no more than four minutes before standing up in defeat. I leave Tim to turn into an ice cube and clamber out with difficulty. It’s surprisingly hard to do when you can’t feel your feet. By now my brain has numbed in sympathy to the rest of me so another five minutes in the steam room and I’m done. I dry off and dress and join Tim on the vast lawn where quite a few people are also lying around. There are a number of what look like grave stones dotted around but these are to rest your back against apparently, rather than the remains of frozen people. A huge fireplace sits in the centre of the lawn but a sudden gust of wind sends smoke everywhere. I lie down and gaze up at the sky which is slowly turning from blue to violet to bright orange as the sun sets over the cliff tops.

I wake early feeling good after a good sleep; I’m sure the steam and ice helped. I make tea  and join Tim upstairs before setting off for the local hospital for my covid test. A prerequisite before flying. The process for Covid testing is fairly straight forward. I register at the front desk and while Tim has an Antigen test, then walk to the second floor for the insertion of the long swab down my nose and throat. We wait a short time at reception for Tim’s results. Mine will come later.

I decide to have a last massage  and we’re lucky enough to find a good spa for half price. Tim opts for acupressure and massage and I ask for full Bali massage with a body scrub. The masseuse applies the scrub liberally over my entire body.
I’m left rolled up like a veggie wrap for fifteen minutes before showering and this is followed by a warm bath littered with some local leaves ( she gives me a name but I can’t remember what she said), twigs, roots and herbs complete the contents.  I self flagellate for a few minutes as suggested, using the long twigs that are left on a chair then she brings me a lovely hot rosehip tea. Thus well oiled and pummelled, I meet up with an equally relaxed looking Tim and we set off back to Le Bamboo.

I’m feeling sad because it’s my last evening in Bali and the sunset is spectacular. After watching the last of the suns rays melt into a velvet sky we go for a last swim. The rest of Uluwatu twinkles in the evening light, the pool is still warm and the moon throws a wobbly image onto the rippling water. 

I lock my bulging suitcase and drag it and a bag of dirty laundry of Tim’s up to reception where I try again to pay with my credit card. Tim arrives at 6 just as his taxi comes into view. They are off to Gili island today. I give him a big hug and with a lump in my throat wave them off. I order two almond lattes to go from The Bakery and keep trying to pay for my accommodation and almost give up on it and my coffee as the Gojek arrives minutes before my taxi.  I grab my luke warm coffee to slurp in the back of the taxi and say goodbye to Le Bamboo.  My driver is happy to hear my results, that have just come through, is negative for Covid. 
The process for boarding Air Asia for Jakarta is uneventful except- somewhat annoyingly, my suitcase only weighs 15kg! I could have put in an extra five.
The next miracle occurs mid flight when I find I am able to buy airtime. More miraculous is that I am then able to pay for my hotel whilst 33,000 feet above earth! Technology never ceases to amaze.
