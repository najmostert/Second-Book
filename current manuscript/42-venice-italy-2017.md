# Chapter 39: Venice, Italy 2017

After dragging our luggage to a ‘human transporter’, we were ferried to the edge of the water, where we climbed aboard a water taxi—a plentiful mode of transport in a city that is quite literally built on water. The taxi dropped us at a canal exit, and we walked to a rather larney (smart and elegant) restaurant for an incredibly expensive cappuccino. I suppose everything in Venice will be pricey, given the weakness of our Rand! We continued walking through the busy streets until the Hotel Banalia came into view, beckoning us with its pretty, flowered courtyard. We entered, only to be greeted by a sullen receptionist who told us to come back when our room was ready. ‘And when will that be?’ I ventured carefully. ‘One o'clock,’ he replied, without making eye contact. ‘Um, can we leave our cases here?’ ‘Leave there,’ he grunted, pointing vaguely at the opposite wall. 

Venice: A Floating City 
Our experiences with receptionists and waitrons recently haven't been great, although it is high season, so perhaps they’re all just fed up with the endless trail of tourists and their baggage, wants, and needs. (I make a point of never including ourselves in this thought process and sleep better for it!) The narrow streets of Venice are filled with tourists, scooters, and shops selling Venetian glassware and touristy trinkets. As expected, there are many canals with indolent gondoliers sitting in the sun or traversing the narrow waters, ancient buildings, crumbling churches, and cobbled side streets. It’s fun seeing the Venice of books and film brought to life. I took in a deep, appreciative breath before coughing loudly—I guess the air could be cleaner! I have succumbed to the hype and joined the thousands with smartwatches, trying to walk the required ten thousand steps per day. I’ve heard this number originated from a Japanese marketing campaign for a pedometer called the manpo-kei (10,000 steps meter) in the 1960s; it’s an arbitrary number, but it certainly promotes activity. We continued our walk over small bridges, watching gondoliers in striped T-shirts and black jeans clutching cellphones as they steered their beautiful black-and-gold gondolas through the inky depths. We stopped for coffee at Sestiere Cannaregio, where I took photos of boats and old architecture before finding the beautiful Salizada Du Spechieri church, the Ponte San Antonio, and the Teatro Italia. We continued over to the Grand Canal—Ponte Scalzi over Santa Croce and Rio Marin. Next was a look into the Basilica dei Frari in Colle San Nicole, which has a beautiful white marble altar and thirty wooden chairs facing each other as if in greeting. 

The Walking Tour 

I told Tim that breakfast was available outside, but ‘Hilda the Miserable’ came over to say they had stopped serving. I begged for some coffee, and she reluctantly returned with a small basket of bread and a jug of brew. Tim decided to book us a walking tour. The coffee was too strong for me—I only drank half—and Tim declared it tasteless, so we walked down the street to the little shop we’d found the day before. Again, we were confused by the prices: €1.60 or €2.60 depending on whether you stood at the bar or sat down! It wasn't a great day for a walking tour, as it started to rain, but we decided to go anyway. Eighteen other multi-national tourists were waiting at Campo Dei Santissimi. Our guide was a tall, blonde, and very pregnant Lithuanian woman named Simona. She was a lovely mix of humour and information, taking us through small churches, outlining the significance of important buildings, and showing us birthplaces and buildings supported by special wooden poles that petrify in salt water. We came to the ‘first ghetto’ in the Cannaregio district, established in 1516, which consists of tall buildings and five historic synagogues. Simona explained that each ‘Campo’ had its own water reservoir—one for locals, and one for cats! Cats were brought in to assist with rat control, some even arriving from Croatia. As she told us this, I thought of my two useless tuxedo kittens, Fish and Chips, who’d let a rat wander into the house without stirring! We reached the Sotoportego del Milion, associated with Marco Polo (nicknamed ‘Il Milione’), and then the ‘most beautiful bookshop in the world’, where books lie inside wooden gondolas to protect them from floods. There were eleven cats in the shop, and I found one curled up in a gondola who looked just like Fishie. Simona gave us tips on navigating the streets—keep to the right and hold umbrellas high to avoid poking others in the eye—and recommended a local cicchetti spot (Bacarondo in Carte Dell’Orso). It was cosy, busy, and full of local dishes. I tried my first classic Italian Aperol spritz; it was okay, but I probably won’t have another. 

Islands of the Lagoon 

The rain abated, so we took a water boat to the Fondamenti dei Mendicanti and walked through to the hospital, where priests of yore healed the sick; the grounds were full of hundreds of accepted, well-fed cats. The next day, we took a water taxi to Burano, a vibrant island famous for brightly coloured houses, intricate lace, and a leaning bell tower. The distinct hues were originally meant to help fishermen spot home in the fog. Next, we hopped over to Torcello, a peaceful, rural island historically significant as the first settlement in the region. It is presently known for Byzantine treasures like the Cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta and the ancient stone ‘Attila’s Throne’. We learned that people initially left Torcello due to malaria-carrying mosquitoes, and their return was largely due to the Sile River diversion. Back on the water taxi, we passed a garden filled with rose bushes and life-sized resin sculptures by Carole Feuerman; they were so real—complete with water droplets—that I loved them! Finally, we reached Murano, famous for glass-blowing. As we walked around photographing the houses, we noticed a large number of rabbits jumping around—they are popular subjects for the unique glass sculptures sold there. 

St Mark’s Square 

Back at the hostel, we relaxed before heading into town toward the Grand Canal. We boarded a large water taxi and sailed past opulent old buildings—many with extravagant entrances that looked less so once you got inside. In St Mark’s Square, we spotted a newly married couple having photographs taken among the pigeons—a very photogenic scene. Inside St Mark’s Basilica, I was scolded for my backpack by a stern woman in an embroidered jacket; I briefly contemplated wearing it as fancy pants but decided against it and locked it away. We spent time in an exhibition by Enzo Fione, featuring lifelike Madonna and ‘Girl with a Pearl Earring’ statues, alongside resin-captured insects. Later, we visited a fascinating Damien Hirst exhibition with an undersea theme: barnacle-clad statues, fake underwater scenes, and skulls of cyclops. I was amused to spot a gold bust of Yolandi, the singer from the South African band Die Antwoord! After packing, Tim and I wandered into the garden terrace for breakfast. Hilda presented an additional bill for four breakfasts; I insisted I’d only had coffee, so she reluctantly charged us for three. Our euros were fast haemorrhaging into the watery depths of Venice! For our last trip on a water taxi, we were weighed down with heavy suitcases, and we hopped off before the last stop, feeling vaguely justified in not paying again—even if the logic didn't quite hold up. We hopped off the ferry before the last stop, just in case a ticket inspector was lurking, and dragged our gear to the bus station. We waited, and waited, and waited. Several buses came and went, but none were headed to Croatia. Tim paced up and down, calling out, "Pula? Pula?" to no avail. Our scheduled departure had been 1 p.m., and it was now approaching 3 p.m. We had no Wi-Fi, no cash, no airtime, and no ideas. Eventually, we sprinted to the train station and booked a last-minute ticket to Trieste, right near the border. After a five-hour trip, Tim scrambled to book a bus to cross the border. The ticket master told us the bus was leaving at that very second, so Tim ran, jumped on, and shouted for them to wait for me. We grabbed the last two seats on the Crnja Tour bus. It wasn't comfortable and lacked Wi Fi, but we were finally moving. 

Pula, Croatia 

We arrived at our hostel, The Crazy Horse. It was bright, clean, and colourful, and the receptionist was so welcoming that we felt our stresses slipping away. After chatting with other backpackers in the communal lounge, we collapsed into bed. I awoke early to the sound of chirping birds—unfortunately, it was just my iPad alarm. After a cup of tea, I explored the hostel; the walls were a vibrant mix of cyclamen pink and lime green. The owner told me he had worked in construction before buying the hostel eighteen months prior. We took a walk past the famous Amphitheatre (built in 900 A.D.). As we passed the massive, remarkably complete Roman ruins, I tried to imagine the violent spectacles of the past: gladiator fights, wild animal hunts, and public executions—the classic ‘bread and circuses’ of the emperors. As evening fell, we walked back past hedges of lavender and tulip-shaped lights. The next day, we explored the old town, including Scampicchio House with its cobbled streets and stone walls. We visited the Temple of Augustus, dedicated to the first Roman emperor, and then stumbled upon the recently unearthed Rimski mosaic—a complete floor in the middle of the city. It was fantastic to stare at something that had been covered for so long, imagining the sandals that had last walked across that smooth surface. Following the hostel owner's recommendation, we ate a tasty meat-and-vegetable dish at The Terrace. The bus to Split had Wi-Fi but no toilet—a fact that immediately filled my bladder to capacity. When we finally stopped, the toilet attendant demanded kuna; I told her I didn’t have any and marched past her anyway. 

Split, Croatia 

After dragging our luggage up a hill, we stopped at the Palace of Diocletian for a coffee by the harbour. We were led to an old, yellow, double-storey building on Marjanski put. An elderly woman who spoke no English showed us to reception, where we waited for a young man to arrive. He offered us a liqueur—a bit strong for that hour of the morning! Eventually, a young, dark-haired man named Ivan (pronounced ‘Eevahn’) appeared. He looked distinctly hungover, but he was incredibly kind. After showing us our room, he treated us to coffee at a restaurant overlooking the harbour and even gave Tim a SIM card worth €35. We spent the day exploring markets, music stalls, and watching a film crew shoot a cooking segment. Later, we walked through the old city, where stone walls were covered in wildflowers, and the narrow cobbled streets were lit by ornate, ancient lampposts. It was a beautiful, atmospheric end to our first day in Split I finally found ‘Froggy Land’; a place I had read about featuring a collection of taxidermied frogs from a hundred years ago. The exhibits were brilliant, showcasing beautifully stuffed frogs in various states of dress and activity—some were playing golf with miniature balls, others sitting at tiny tables eating a meal, or even swimming. They were well-preserved, and their tiny costumes were delightful. I was thrilled we found it. We continued wandering around Split, exploring shops, statues, and narrow lanes. Back at the hostel, we found Ivan in the BBQ area. As soon as he saw us, he switched on the lights, and we sat on a bench chatting until he presented us with his mother's homemade cherry liqueur in orange juice. It was a lovely evening with him and his brothers. The next morning, we walked up the mountain behind the hostel toward Marjan Hill. We found the ‘314 steps’ leading to the Telegrin peak, which offered panoramic views of the city and the shimmering blue Adriatic. Tim caught a butterfly for his dad—a ‘rare swallowtail’—which he tucked away in his backpack. After a brief look at a rupestrian (rock-cut) church, we headed to Plaza Kašjuni. We found a row of recliners with private curtains, but a waiter soon sprinted down to inform us they cost R500 each to hire. I sprang up immediately, assuring him we’d be perfectly happy with the cheaper deckchairs on the pebbly beach! We later took an Uber to Klis to see the fortress—famous for the sword fight scene in Game of Thrones. It was a sunny day, and the acoustics in an old church were so fantastic that I started chanting like a monk of yore. Tim joined in with a baritone, and we were quite chuffed until a blonde Australian girl appeared at the door, having heard what she thought was a choir! 

Bosnia & Dubrovnik 

After bidding a fond farewell to Ivan and his family, we caught a bus to Dubrovnik. The journey was scenic, passing towns with names like Šugi, Omiš, and Neum. At one toilet stop in Ploče, a strange middle-aged woman in a black dress grabbed my shoulder, insisting I follow her to photograph the men's toilets. I shook her off with a firm "Go away!" We eventually crossed into Bosnia Slivno for passport control, where the houses were all uniform with new red-tiled roofs. We arrived in Dubrovnik later than scheduled. After avoiding a pushy taxi driver, we took an Uber to the L'Orangerie. 

Turkey: Istanbul Again 

Tim grabbed a final croissant at Bobis before our Uber driver roared into view. He parked across two spaces, chucked our bags in the boot, and drove with such terrifying velocity that I spent the ride trying to distract him by asking about his Prius. He screeched to a halt at the bus terminus, threw our luggage onto the pavement, and sped off. We made it to the airport, flew to Turkey, and received a warm welcome back at the Sultania Hotel, complete with Turkish delight. Our final itinerary included the Basilica Cistern, the Column of Constantine, the Blue Mosque, the Sinan Pasha, and the Great Indoor Market—the largest in the Northern Hemisphere. The air was thick with exotic spices, incense, and sweets. The stall owners were relentless: ‘Come and buy something you don’t need!’ When I told one owner I was just looking, he countered, ‘Well, I’m just selling!’ I couldn't argue with that, so I ended up buying kilos of rose-flavoured Turkish delight, roasted pistachios, and some pretty glass lamps. As we strolled through the market, I remembered that it was Ramadan, and many of the stall owners had not eaten since pre-dawn. It was a humbling thought as we navigated the vibrant, bustling heart of Istanbul before our flight back to Cape Town the next day. I’m feeling tired, so I can only imagine how they must be feeling. It’s also very quiet on the ‘Western front,’ so to speak—many of the stall owners seem lethargic and resigned. We walk up ‘Lingerie Street,’ selling everything from granny pants to lacy thongs. I remark to Tim that Saudi Arabia was similar—not the lacy underwear, but the way entire streets are dedicated to selling the exact same thing: electronics, shoes, or toys. Back at the Sultania, we make a booking for The Olive Restaurant for later that evening. We repack our straining cases, rest for a short while, and then head out. We’re met by the same waiter as last time—the one with the quivering, theatrical presentation of food—who welcomes us like long-lost friends. Since it’s well after sunset, the place is full of families and friends observing Iftar; they’re breaking their fast with dates and water, their faces lit with the relief and joy of post-Maghrib dining. Thankfully, there’s time the next morning to walk to the Archaeological Museum. It’s a lovely, balmy day, and the walk is pleasant. The museum is fascinating, with exhibits from Mesopotamia, Ancient Greece, Rome, and Egypt. Cats and kittens are hiding among the exhibits, curled up and fast asleep. We have a coffee sitting in the ‘archaeological garden’ amidst several life-size statues and other pieces of antiquity. It’s a lot to see, and it takes us a good two hours to complete the tour. After the museum, we walk over to the nearby Hagia Sophia Mosque, which was the largest domed building in the world for over 800 years. Originally built by Christians and later taken over by Muslims, it displays a fascinating mix of the two traditions. There’s even a Viking runic inscription on one of the walls, likely carved by a member of a Viking bodyguard unit for the Byzantine emperors. The inscription, discovered in 1964, reads ‘Halvdan’— or possibly, ‘Halvdan was here.’ A second inscription has been interpreted as ‘Árni made’ (Árni cut these runes). Well done, Árni! 

The Journey Home 

After passing through passport control and security, I suggest we stop over in the priority lounge. I hand over my credit card, imagining sitting in comfort, eating free food, and using Wi-Fi until boarding. My dream fades fast: the snooty woman at the desk sighs, hands me back my card with her fingertips as if it were contaminated, and says it has been declined. I know I have the funds and had pre-arranged lounge access, but she is adamant. I can hardly call my bank at this hour, so we admit defeat and walk next door to pay for expensive food and Wi-Fi instead. So, no Cuba—but a really lovely Adriatic Coast holiday covering five countries. Not too shoddy in my opinion! The next thing I do is request Facebook to change me back from ‘Dora D’Ourabora’ to my actual name, but they say I have to wait another two weeks. My friends are calling me Dora now, and I suppose I’ll just have to live with it for a while! My dear brother died in unfortunate circumstances a month ago. He was a long-term quadriplegic, and a physiotherapist ‘forgot’ to check his charts before hitting him so forcefully on his back that he fell forward and broke both femurs. The resulting fractures, internal bleeding, and shock ultimately led to his death. Eight years later, I am still waiting for an apology from the hospital. His death has left me uncharacteristically depressed, so when my sister-in-law arranged a visit to Zimbabwe for a ‘wildlife count’ in Hwange National Park, I was happy to go. I was the last off the plane in Harare, and my blood pressure shot up when I realised I couldn't find my small black bag containing my passport, US dollars, Rands, and boarding pass. After a frantic, wheezing rush across the tarmac and a stint at a useless "Lost and Found," I finally found my bag at the security office—they were busy counting my money. After a chaotic experience filling out entry forms—blaming the two glasses of foul plane wine for my temporary amnesia—I finally cleared customs and met my sister-inlaw, Cecilia. We met her friends, T (who was suffering from a tummy bug) and D (her beau), and began the eight-hour drive to Hwange. Along the way, I learned that termites orient their mounds to face the morning sun to regulate temperature. In the park, we were rewarded with sightings of guinea fowl, baboons, wildebeest, zebra, ostriches, and a pride of lions—a male, two females, and cubs. We also discussed the tragic fate of Cecil, the park's famous lion who was lured and killed by a trophy hunter in 2015. The ‘Game Count’ is Africa's longest-running census. As volunteers, we were stationed at a waterhole for 24 hours to track every mammal that appeared. It was extremely hot, and the pump at our pan wasn't working, which meant fewer animals than hoped for. We worked in shifts: four hours watching, two hours sleeping on a mattress in the back of the Land Cruiser. The highlight was a massive bull elephant that wandered right up to our vehicles, putting out an inquisitive trunk. We also saw Sable antelope and at 3:30 a.m., a wild dog. Returning to main camp, we handed in our data. It was a lovely, healing interlude, though it felt strange not to be bringing back Tanganda tea leaves for my brother.
