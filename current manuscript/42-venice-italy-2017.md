# Chapter 39: Venice, Italy 2017

After dragging our luggage over to a kind of locomotion called a ‘human transporter’ which took us to the edge of the sea to climb aboard a water taxi. These modes of transport were plentiful in Venice, seeing as how it was quite watery. The taxi took us to the canal exit where we hopped off and walked to a rather larney (South African; meaning smart and elegant) restaurant for a very expensive cappuccino. I guessed everything would be expensive with our weak Rand. We continued walking along the busy streets until the Hotel Banalia came into view and beckoned us to enter with its pretty flowered courtyard. Actually we entered because we’d booked. A sullen receptionist told us to come back when the room was ready.

‘And when will that be?’ I ventured carefully.
‘One o’clock.’ He replied without eye contact.
‘Um, can we leave our cases here?’
‘Leave there.’ He grunted, pointing vaguely at the opposite wall.

Our experiences with receptionists and waitrons recently hadn’t been great, although it was high season so maybe they were all just fed up with the endless trail of tourists and their extra baggage and wants and needs. (I make a point of never including ourselves in this thought process and sleep better for it).
The narrow streets of Venice were filled with the aforementioned tourists, scooters, busy shops selling Venetian glassware and other touristy trinkets. As to be expected, many canals held indolent gondoliers sitting in the sun or traversing the narrow waters of the canal, ancient buildings rose haughtily on either side, crumbling churches opened their doors and cobbled side streets held the promise of hidden antique shops and cafés.
It was fun seeing the Venice of books and film brought to life. I took in a deep appreciative breath before coughing loudly. I guessed the air could have been cleaner.

I had succumbed to the hype having joined thousands of others with smart watches and was trying to walk the required ten thousand steps per day. I heard this number originated from a Japanese marketing campaign for a pedometer called the ‘manpo-kei’ (meaning ‘10,000 steps meter’) in the 1960s, around the time of the Tokyo Olympics. While some research suggested potential benefits, it was an arbitrary number chosen for its cultural resonance and ability to successfully promote a higher level of physical activity. I kept this in mind when my steps became weary.

We continued our walk over small bridges, watching gondoliers wearing striped T-shirts and black jeans and clutching cellphones as they gently rowed their human cargo through the inky depths of the canal. We had another reviving coffee at Sestiere Cannaregio where I took a lot of photographs of boats and old buildings before finding firstly the beautiful Salizada Du Spechieri church down a side street, the Ponte San Antonio and the Teatro Italia on the Campiello de l’Ancaneta. Another large church at Giochina was made from marble. So much beautiful ancient architecture. We continued over to the Grand Canal — Ponte Scalzi over Santa Croce and Rio Marin with more gondoliers steering their beautiful black and gold painted gondolas.
Next was a look into the Basilica Del Friar in Colle San Nicole which had a beautiful white marble altar and about thirty wooden chairs facing each other as if in greeting.

 Tim and I sat outside for breakfast. ‘Hilda the Miserable’ came over and said they’d stopped serving. I begged for some coffee and she reluctantly returned with a small basket of bread and a jug of coffee. Tim decided to book us in for a walking tour. The coffee was too strong for me so I only drank half of it and Tim declared it tasteless so we walked down the street to the little coffee shop we’d found the day before. Again we were confused at the prices which seemed to vary between €1.60 or €2.60. After ordering at the cheaper price, we sat down to drink it and discovered that if you sat down, the coffee was more expensive as though it had suddenly donned a tuxedo. Not a great day for a walking tour as it started to rain but we decided to go along and see if it was still on. It was and there were already around eighteen other tourists waiting at the chosen spot — Campo Dei Santissimi. We shuffled forward and smiled in that slightly awkward way one does when meeting a group of strangers in the form of multinational tourists. Our guide was a tall, blonde and very pregnant Lithuanian woman who we learned was married to a local. She introduced herself as Simona which we had no reason to believe was untrue. She had a warm smile and quickly outlined her plan of the tour and so began a three-hour walk where we learned some amazing facts. We explored small churches, looked over green canals and she outlined the significance of some important buildings. She was a lovely mix of humour and information and we thoroughly enjoyed the tour. Some of the places she showed us included the birthplace of Valentino, buildings with special wooden poles that kept them strong as the wood they used petrified quickly when in contact with salt water making them ideal for a damp place like Venice.
She pointed out small back entrances built for servants but also because they were closest to the pathways.
Campos — or squares where ancient Venetians could grow vegetables and fruit and keep chickens, making them self-reliant — were found in between the buildings. The ornate wooden entrances were situated off the canals and lagoons so that the owners could alight easily from their boats and the beautifully crafted brass knockers were said to bring good luck. Simona told us that the painting of Chiesa Santa Maria was bought by a rich Venetian family and thought to also bring good luck so it was given to the people of Venice and a church hurriedly built around the large painting. Whilst I was loving the tour, my legs were tiring after three hours. I could only imagine how she must have been feeling with her baby bump. We came to an area called ‘The first ghetto’ in Venice’s Cannaregio district. While there was a memorial in the Venetian ghetto with gold-plated tiles indicating the names of people who suffered during the Holocaust, this was a relatively recent addition to honour the community’s painful history rather than an ancient architectural feature. The ghetto itself was the world’s first, established in 1516, and consisted of tall buildings and five historic synagogues.
Fresh water was brought inland via large trucks and dispersed to the people or filtered through sand to remove salt. Each ‘Campo’ had its own water reservoir for the locals and one for cats! Cats were brought in to assist with keeping the number of rats down. Some were even brought from Croatia.
As she told us this, I was thinking of my useless two tuxedo kittens, Fish and Chips, who’d let a rat wander into the house without stirring. She brought us to Sotoportego del Milion, a passageway located in the Corte Seconda del Milion; part of the area historically associated with Marco Polo. Marco Polo’s nickname was ‘Il Milione.’
We were led to, in her words, ‘The most beautiful bookshop in the world’. Most of the books lay inside wooden gondolas, apparently to prevent them from being destroyed when it flooded. The floods could be bad here and could rise higher than the back steps, which then flowed into the rooms along the canals. There were eleven cats in the bookstore and I found a kitty curled up inside one of the gondolas, who looked just like my cat, Fishie, with a white stripe on his nose.
Simona advised us that when in the narrow streets, you should keep to the right and when holding umbrellas, keep them higher than your head to avoid poking others in the eyes or hold them down. Made sense; I had almost removed an eyeball already that morning.
Venice being the city of some strange long-beaked masks; Simona explained that their significance came from the times when masks with a long beak were worn by doctors during the plague and were made from leather and metal. They also wore a gown with a protective waxy surface and a black hat. The other reason for wearing masks in Venice was to remain incognito for variously affairs de l’amour, clandestine business meetings and to avoid or remove social differences. After all everyone looked the same wearing an intricate mask. All classes could mingle then, men could be women, women could be men and the sheep were nervous.
There were so many shops selling masks that one felt as if we were in a scene from Phantom of the Opera.
Simona gave us some tips on good places to eat, a free map of all the sights we’d seen and wished us all well.
After thanking her for a really good tour we went in search of one of the restaurants she suggested for local, back-street food.
This was a cicchetti place; Bacarondo in Carte Dell’Orso. It was very cosy, busy and full of local dishes including cicchetti, which was a lot of spreads and crusty bread. I tried my first classic Italian Aperol spritz. It was okay but I wouldn’t be having another one.

The rain had abated somewhat so we took a water boat to the Fondamenti deli Mendicanti and walked through to the hospital to where priests of yore healed the sick. The hospital grounds boasted hundreds of cats; all accepted and therefore fed, before stopping at a small gallery of modern art on the way home. The exhibition concentrated on differing perspectives so you saw ‘fish’ in a lopsided tank, a canoe filled with water, a screen that rose to the ceiling depicting other perspectives of nature.
After the usual coffee and dry roll brekky we walked to the jetty and took a water taxi to Burano which was a vibrant, picturesque island in the Venetian Lagoon, famous for its brightly coloured houses, intricate lacemaking tradition and leaning bell tower. Its distinct hues were said to help fishermen spot home in the fog on their return from a day’s fishing.

The houses seemed different here — different architecture and slightly bigger than the mainland. Roses grew everywhere and their scent wafted gently in the warmth of the day.

Next we hopped over to Torcello; a sparsely populated island in the northern Venetian Lagoon, historically significant as the first settlement in the region from which Venice grew, then known for its peaceful, rural atmosphere and Byzantine-era treasures like the Cathedral of Santa Maria Assunta with its stunning mosaics and the ancient stone ‘Attila’s Throne’.
Torcello was six miles (15 kilometres) away from Venice,  surrounded by salt-water marshes.
The main reason people initially left this island was the increase in size of the marshes which led to an exponential increase in mosquitoes and thus malaria, which in turn was caused by the presence of the female Anopheles mosquito.
The people's return was largely due to the Sile River diversion; where hydraulic engineering efforts by the Venetian Republic diverted rivers to prevent further silting of the marshes and therefore the mosquitoes.
Torcello had the oldest church in Venice — called the Bell Tower where one might climb to the top of the turret.
Back on the water taxi to Mazzarbo. We passed a beautiful garden filled with more rose bushes of every colour and sculptures by Carole Feuerman. They were very lifelike, life-sized statues made from resin of women in swimming costumes and rubber caps. They looked so real they had ‘water’ droplets running down their bodies. I loved it.

From here we took the boat to nearby Murano. The island differed again — this time with its stretches of shops  famous for glass-blowing in the inimitable Venetian style. Some of this beautiful glass jewellery was on display in the shop windows as we walked by. Murano had quite a long canal called the Canale degli Angeli. As we walked around photographing the houses we noticed a large number of rabbits jumping around. Rabbits were popular subjects for the glass sculptures sold as unique works of art and collectible pieces.
Back at Hostel Boccassini we relaxed for a while before heading into town towards the Grand Canal.
The idea was to take the last boat trip using our passes before it expired, so we boarded a large water taxi that sailed down the canal where all the huge opulent old buildings were situated, some of which had changed very little for centuries. The entrances were particularly extravagant but became less so after entering the building in many cases.

After taking a chance with our newly expired tickets, we climbed aboard another water taxi to the Grand Canal.
In St Mark’s Square a newly married couple were having photographs taken as they ran among the pigeons. I took my own photo of them as it looked quite photogenic with the old building in the background and the pigeons mid-flight.

After paying a small sum to enter St Mark’s Basilica, we headed to the Piazza San Marco. Once inside, I was asked to remove my backpack by a stern-looking woman wearing an embroidered jacket, so I walked to the other room, removed the rucksack from my back and carried it like a handbag on my shoulder. She wasn’t impressed and told me again to lock it up. I briefly contemplated climbing into the straps and wearing it as a fancy pair of pants but as her eagle eyes remained fixed on me, decided against it and locked it away as requested. I mean it was too small to carry away a painting if that’s what she was worried about.
There were many lovely old paintings and meme-worthy themes, old leather atlases, books and coins. An exhibition by Italian artist Enzo Fione depicted plants, animals and nature with lifelike Madonna, Girl with Pearl Earring and a lot of resin-captured beetles, butterflies and other insects. Afterwards we wandered around away from the city in search of a cheaper cup of coffee than we’d been having so far. We were fed up with all the extra hidden costs of sitting taxes, service charges and general city taxes as they made each purchase significantly more expensive.

Eventually we found a small restaurant down a quiet side street and chose a selection of cicchetti (various things on bread) and an octopus, black squid-ink calamari with bruschetta.
Thus sated, we took another chance on a water taxi to go and see the Damien Hirst exhibition but jumped off a bit too soon and so found another church at St Mark’s Basilica. The Damien Hirst exhibition was fascinating with his use of fantasy, huge life-size exhibits with an undersea world as the theme and we wandered around for over an hour looking at barnacle-clad statues and fake underwater scenes, skulls of cyclops, all with a breathtaking view of the canal. I was amused to find a gold bust of Yolandi, one of the singers of the South African band, ‘Die Antwoord’.

I withdrew a handful of euros from an ATM that required either a very tall person or for one to stand precariously balanced on an inch of step to withdraw. I definitely fell in the latter category and teetered precariously on the short step with only my toes having any purchase with the cement.
After packing Tim and I wandered into the garden terrace for breakfast. While paying for the accommodation I was presented with an additional bill for four breakfasts. I told Hilda we’d assumed breakfast was part of the cost but she insisted we pay. I muttered that I’d only drunk coffee on two occasions so she reluctantly charged us three breakfasts. My euros were fast haemorrhaging into the watery depths of Venice.
Our last trip on a water taxi, this time weighed down with extra heavy suitcases. After the extra charges at the hotel, I felt vaguely justified in not paying again despite the fact this didn’t make any mathematical (or moral) sense. This time we hopped off before the last stop in case a ticket inspector was lurking.

After lugging all the heavy gear to the bus stop for a FlixBus we waited, and waited some more. No one else was waiting and although several buses came and went — none of them were going to Croatia. Tim started walking up and down, going, ‘Pula, Pula?’ Without any luck and many people just hurried past. Our bus was scheduled to leave at 1 p.m. and it was now approaching 3 p.m. so we were feeling somewhat perturbed. We had no Wi-Fi, cash, airtime or ideas. Eventually we ran to the nearby train station and booked a last-minute train to Trieste, which lies just before the border. It took five hours to get there so Tim immediately tried to book a bus to get us over the border between Italy and Croatia. The ticket master told us that we’d just missed the bus, but it was leaving as we spoke so Tim ran and jumped on while asking them to wait for me. We got the last two seats. This was the Crnja Tour bus. It wasn’t very comfortable and it didn’t have Wi-Fi so the challenges of the day continued.

## Pula, Croatia

We arrived at The Crazy Horse hostel. It was bright, clean and colourful and the receptionist was very welcoming. He showed us to our room and we gradually felt the stresses of the day slipping away. After chatting to some of the other backpackers in the communal lounge we headed off to bed.

I awoke early to the sound of chirping birds. Unfortunately this was my iPad alarm not the real thing.  It was a dullish, grey day. I took our dirty cups to be washed and had a look around the hostel. The walls were painted cyclamen pink and lime green as its primary colours that contrasted with the grey outside. I chatted to the owner a bit. He told me that he had worked in construction then bought the hostel only a year and a half ago. There were real bird sounds emanating from the nearby forest so we took a walk past the famous Amphitheatre arena (built in 900 A.D.). It was one of the most complete ruins of Roman times and as we passed I tried to imagine the violent public spectacles of the past; gladiator fights, wild animal hunts and even public executions that took place. These events were a form of mass entertainment and emperors used them to display their generosity and maintain control over the population through what was known as ‘bread and circuses’ (panem et circenses).
As evening drew dark curtains against the sky, we walked back to the hostel past hedges of lavender and lights the shape and colour of tulips.

The old town and Scampiccio House had cobble streets, stone walls and tall pillars, the Temple of Augustus; dedicated to the first Roman emperor, then walked through the large town square.
We found the recently unearthed Rimski mosaic. This was a complete mosaic floor in the middle of the old city and down several side streets. It was fantastic to stare at something that had been covered for so long and to imagine the sandals that last walked across the smooth albeit faded mosaic floor.
The hostel owner suggested we eat at The Terrace and we had a very tasty meat and vegetable dish. When paying, I said a timid ‘Hvala vam puno’ (thank you very much) to her before continuing the walk back to the Crazy Horse.
The bus to Split had Wi-Fi on board but no toilet. This information, having reached my brain immediately had the effect of filling my bladder to capacity. I made some enquiries and heard that we were stopping in an hour. The  toilet attendant sat outside the door demanding I pay in kuna; I told her I didn’t have any and ignored her. Long, tedious, cramped hours passed until we made it to Split.

## Split, Croatia

The Palace of Diocletian crested a hill before dipping down for a coffee at the side of the harbour. It was so early that people were still opening up their shopfronts. Later we came to an old yellow, double-storey building off one of the main roads, our accomodation,‘Marjanski put’. A small elderly woman led us slowly up some stairs. She spoke no English and motioned us to sit at reception until a young man arrived to show us our room. He offered us a liqueur in the meantime, which we both politely sipped at this early stage of the morning. Eventually, another young, dark-haired man with a receding hairline arrived and introduced himself as ‘Ivan’ (pronounced as ‘Eevahn’).
He looked distinctly hungover and admitted he hadn’t slept much. He gave us a map, showed us to our room and said he would take us for a coffee. We dumped our luggage and followed him downstairs where he chatted to us in good but accented English as we walked up a nearby hill to an outside restaurant overlooking the harbour and the town of Split. He bought us each a coffee and a bottle of water and handed a SIM card to Tim worth €35. This was exceedingly generous and I was wondering if he had quite sobered up yet.
After followeing him back to the hostel to get our bearings, we left to explore the town and came across a market, music stalls and a stall with a film crew. They were filming a short haired woman self consciously cooking pancakes, probably for some morning TV show. Later we returned to the B & B and sat in the common lounge to chat with a more animated Ivan before going out again to walk through the old city to the Palace of Diocletian. Stone walls were covered in wildflowers, the cobbled streets were narrow with old ornate lampposts.
I wanted to see ‘Froggy Land’, a place I had read about that had many ‘taxidermied’ frogs from a hundred years ago. The exhibits were brilliant and showcased beautifully stuffed frogs in various states of dress and positions. In each glass showcase, some were ‘playing golf’ with miniature golf balls, sitting eating a meal at a tiny table, swimming and various other activities of daily living. They had been well preserved and their costumes were delightful and I was really pleased we'd found the place.

Back at the hostel we looked into the ‘BBQ’ area of the hostel at the back of the building. Ivan was there and as soon as he saw us, he switched on a string of fairy lights before joining us on a bench. He presented us with his mother’s homemade cherry liqueur in orange juice. We raised our glasses in thanks and chatted to two of his older brothers. They were all really nice, friendly people. I nodded to two Australian girls watching some awful programme on the telly. 
Next morning we went for a walk up the mountain behind the hostel and down to another beach. It was cool outside, which was great for walking. We found the 314 steps that one of the girls mentioned after telling us she was leaving for home. The ‘314 steps’ led to the Telegrin peak on Marjan Hill. This was a popular hiking route within Marjan Park that offered panoramic views of the city and the Adriatic Sea; a shimmering blue colour. Tim caught a butterfly for his dad and tucked it away in his backpack. We looked it up and it was called the ‘rare swallowtail’, so he was sure to love it for his collection.
 At the Plaza Kašjuni, we grabbed a drink on Joe’s beach before finding a row of beach recliners that had white billowing curtains around a frame for extra privacy. We each settled into one and as I was sipping my drink I wondered why more people were not using them. The answer came not long after, when a waiter sprinted down to the beach and told me I needed to hire each recliner. I sprang up and assured him we would be fine with the cheaper deckchairs on the beach. I hobbled over the pebbly beach for a dip in the water but it wasn’t as refreshing as it looked and was full of sea urchins so I quickly hobbled back over the hot pebbles to my recliner where we spent the remainder of the afternoon.
An Uber took us to Solin in Klis, around 45 minutes away. The driver, Emil, was very informative. He told us about the Dalmatian mountains and showed us the old Greek city of Salona and a 2,000-year-old Roman aqueduct that was still in use. After dropping us off at Klis we walked up to the fortress to spend a few hours. This fortress was also used in the fourth season of Game of Thrones, specifically the sword fight scene.
It was a lovely sunny day and we had fun looking around before entering an old church through old, carved wooden doors. The acoustics were fantastic, so much so that I decided to chant as monks of yore would do. Tim joined in for some baritone. Suddenly, in the middle of singing, a blonde-haired girl appeared at the door. She said she’d heard a choir in the church and had come up to see. We were quite chuffed with ourselves if a little embarrassed. She introduced herself and said she was from Australia. After chatting for a bit she joined us for the duration of the visit. The place was full of cats, birds and butterflies, another one of which was the ‘rare’ Swallowtail. I was wondering how rare it actually was if we’d seen two already.

## Bosnia & Dubrovnik

After bidding goodbye to Ivan, his mother and the other guests we left for the bus stop near the harbour and bought a last croissant from Bobis bakery and a cappuccino at Kolodvor bus station before getting the bus to Dubrovnik. I gazed out of the windows after setting off and noticed the interesting names of places as we passed which sounded like the names of the members of a rock band; Šugi, Omiš, Priko, Rat, Podi, and Neum. At one station toilet in Ploče, a middle-aged woman wearing a long black dress with a grey scarf tied under her chin grabbed my shoulder and insisted I follow her in to photograph the men’s toilets. I shook her hand off me and said,
‘Absolutely not! Go away!’ A strange thing to ask I thought. We eventually stopped at Bosnia-Slivno for passport control which went smoothly followed by a coffee at Hotel Jadran where we could use euros again. There were a few dark forested areas here so it was nice and verdant with a pretty bay. All the houses seemed to have new red-tiled roofs, giving a homogenous feel to the town.
I weas annoyed to arrive later than scheduled in Dubrovnik as it ate into our time there. 

## Istanbul, Turkey

Tim popped into Bobis for a last croissant before our Uber driver roared into view. He had a low rating, but we figured we’d be late waiting for someone else. It was soon revealed why his ratings were low; he parked across two spaces, grabbed our bags with barely a word, chucked them in the boot and shot off again. His driving was scary and as we rolled around in the back, I tried to think of ways to calm him down. I asked him some questions about his Prius, which ran on battery and petrol, which opened him up slightly but did little to calm down his driving. He screeched to a halt at the bus terminus, threw our luggage on the pavement before speeding off again. I shakily picked up my bags and climbed aboard the bus to the airport. This bus was driven by a portly man who complained to his co-driver about his wife the whole way. There were a number of stop/go scenarios so we arrived later than anticipated before taking a small plane to Turkey where I sorted out the tickets at the airport for our return to Cape Town the next day. The Sultania hotel staff greeted us warmly and welcomed us back before presenting us with welcome drinks and some Turkish delight.
Basilica Cistern, Column of Constantine, the Blue Mosque, the Great Sinan Pasha, and lastly, a huge indoor market — the biggest in the Northern Hemisphere — was on our itinerary. So many stalls selling all manner of spices and sweets, Turkish delight, assorted nuts, and incense sticks. The air was redolent with exotic smells. Stall owners plied their wares, trying to bring you in with enticing and sometimes amusing comments:
‘Come and buy something you don’t need!’
When I told one stall owner that I was just looking, he countered with,
‘Well, I’m just selling!’, which I couldn't argue with.
Yet another persisted in asking me to look and buy, so I just smiled. He asked me,
‘So should I just give up or keep on trying?’ I had to laugh and ended up buying a few kilos of rose-flavoured Turkish delight, roasted pistachios and some pretty glass lamps and bowls from him.
As we continued with our perusal, I suddenly remembered that it was Ramadan and many of the stall owners had not eaten since pre-dawn.
I was feeling tired, so I could only imagine how they were feeling. It was also very quiet on the Western front, so to speak. A lot of the stall owners seemed lethargic and somewhat resigned. We walked up ‘lingerie street’, selling all kinds of underwear from granny pants to lacy thongs. I remarked to Tim that Saudi Arabia had been similar — not the lacy underwear but where entire streets sold the same thing: electronics or shoes or toys, etc.
Back at The Sultania, we made a booking for the Olive restaurant for later that evening then repacked our straining cases in the meantime, rested for a short while before heading towards the Olive. We were met by the same waiter as last time, the one with the quivering, theatrical presentation of food. He welcomed us like long-lost friends. As it was well after sunset by now, the place was full of families and friends post-Maghrib (evening prayer) and they were eating dates and drinking water post fast.
Thankfully there was time that morning to walk to the Archaeological Museum. It was a lovely, balmy day and the walk was really pleasant. The museum was interesting and had exhibits from Mesopotamia, Ancient Greece, Rome, and Egypt. Cats and kittens were hiding among the exhibits, curled up and fast asleep. We had a coffee sitting in the ‘archaeological garden’ amid several life-size statues and other pieces of antiquity. There was a lot to see and it took us a good two hours to complete the tour of the place.
After the museum, we walked over to the nearby Hagia Sophia Mosque, which had been the largest domed building in the world for over 800 years. First built by Christians, then taken over by Muslims, the result was that it displayed an interesting mix of the two. There was even a Viking runic inscription on one of the walls likely carved by a member of a Viking bodyguard unit for the Byzantine emperors. The inscription, which was discovered in 1964, read ‘Halvdan’ or possibly, ‘Halvdan was here’. A second inscription had been interpreted as ‘Árni made’ (Árni cut these runes). Well done Árni!
After passport control and X-rays I suggested we stop over in the priority lounge and handed over my credit card and imagined sitting in comfort, eating free food and drinks and using Wi-Fi until boarding time. My dream faded fast as the snooty woman sighed, handed me back my credit card with her fingertips as though contaminated and said it had been declined. I knew I had the funds and arranged use of the lounge before I left so I argued the point, but she was adamant we could not go in.
‘Speak to your bank,’ she suggested unfairly, as I could hardly speak to anyone at home at that hour. We admitted defeat and walked next door to use their Wi-Fi and eat an expensive meal.
So, no Cuba, but a really lovely Adriatic Coast holiday in five countries. Not too shoddy in my opinion. Next thing I did was request Facebook to change me back from Dora D’Ourabora to my actual name. They said I had to wait another two weeks. My friends were calling me Dora now.

## Zimbabwe (2017)

My dear brother died a month after returning from Europe, in hospital where a physiotherapist ‘forgot’ to check his charts before hitting him so forcefully on his back that he fell forward and broke both femurs. This was gross negligence as any health care practitioner would know that most long-term quadriplegic patients have brittle bones (osteopenia) that break easily. His weight as it fell against his thighs caused bilateral fractures, internal bleeding and ultimately death. Eight years later I’m still waiting for an apology from the hospital (yes I hear you, ‘Good luck with that!’). His death upset me so much I wasn’t sleeping and feeling uncharacteristically depressed. We were very close and I missed him terribly. So when my sister-in-law arranged a visit to Zimbabwe for a ‘wildlife count’ I was only too happy to go.

I was the last off the plane in Harare and sauntered over to the carousel to collect my bag. I noticed everyone else filling in forms and discovered it was an arrival form that all passengers needed to complete. My blood pressure shot up when I realised I couldn’t find my small black bag containing passports, US dollars, Rand notes and my boarding pass. I ran over the hot tarmac to the plane and found the cleaners already busy inside. They were somewhat startled to see me puffing at the entrance and wheezing out something that sounded like, ‘Small, black bag!’
They shrugged in unison and one lady directed me back to ‘Lost and Found,’ (I should have started there). Lost and Found were next to useless and merely reported, parrot-fashion, ‘Lost passport!’. I  them and go and look around myself and bumped into an American passenger who told me they were searching for the owner of a small black pouch and she pointed behind her to the security office where three men were sitting at a small wooden desk. I thanked her and rushed over. The contents of my bag  spilled onto the table and they were counting my dollars. I breathlessly claimed possession of the bag. One official picked up my passport with thick fingers and opened it to the front page. Although the sleep deprived, wild-eyed woman in front of them bore little to no resemblance to the passport photo, a dangerous glint in my eyes alerted them not to mess with me. I grabbed the passport, stuffed the money and passports back into the bag, then garbled a quick thanks before heading to where my sister-in-law was waiting for me, next to my suitcase that was moving forlornly around the carousel.
I gave Cecilia a hug, explained my delay and said I had not yet completed the entry form. I regretted the two glasses of foul red wine I had consumed on the plane, blaming them squarely for my temporary amnesia. She advised me to complete the form to avoid future detention as an undeclared visitor so I rushed back to passport control with my blank form. The official at the desk looked surprised to see me appearing from a direction that indicated I had already completed the passport check. She told me, somewhat officiously that I needed to have my passport stamped. This done, I realised that I needed her help in completing the form as I had left my reading glasses with Cecilia. She obliged and barked,
‘Sex?’ I was momentarily confused, about to tell her that’s none of her business then glanced up. Wait, couldn’t she tell? I realised I had to write down the answers myself and asked her to place her thumb where I needed to write the information. The font size was 4 or 5 and looked suspiciously like Wingdings to my blurred vision. It was a tedious exercise and her thumb moved ever faster down the page as she asked the questions, until I ended up scribbling anywhere on the page. It was a complete mess but I thrust the document back at her after signing somewhere near the bottom.
I met Cecilia’s friends; T and D who were joining us on the trip. I was here to partake in the annual ‘Game Count’ in the Hwange National Park. I was really excited for this, as spaces were limited and it was only thanks to Cecilia that I could join them. We dropped in at the Town Planning office en route to purchase a map of the park. 
T had short, grey hair and exuded confidence and a no-nonsense approach. D was her unrequited beau, grey-haired and in his early sixties.
I read through the itinerary in the sumptuous garden of a local coffee place. The proposed coordinates of the pan where we would take part in the count were provided and they studied the contour map with deep concentration. I looked around the lovely garden and watched a few bulbuls bobbing around a nearby jackal berry tree. Back at the house, I was introduced to Hans and Bert, his dad from Holland. I'd tried to nap but after half an hour of staring at the ceiling, I picked up my camera and announced myself to the assembled group by falling down the steps, grazing my knees a little and my dignity a lot then excused myself to wander around the garden taking photos of various plants and birds. 
We were packed and ready to leave by 5 a.m. D and T arrived in their 4x4, so we drove in tandem. It was quiet on the road with only four roadblocks. After stopping to fill up with petrol, it took nearly eight hours to get to Hwange in northwestern Zimbabwe.
En route; guinea fowl rootled around the edge of the road and some chacma baboons, wildebeest, zebra, and a couple of ostriches were spotted. I learned that termites created their mounds facing the morning sun while avoiding the intense midday heat, a process of thermoregulation that helps to maintain a stable temperature inside the nest. The hottest days in Zimbabwe is November so when we next came across termite mounds, I noticed their position.
Up early for another game drive; we didn’t see much at first, but then a beautiful male lion with a dark mane, his cubs, and two female lions came into view. It was a good sighting and we watched them for quite a while until they wandered off into the bush. Another big male lion called Cecil used to roam around Hwange, recognised by his striking black mane and role as a popular research subject for Oxford University making him an iconic attraction for tourists. His notoriety exploded globally after he was lured and killed by a trophy hunter in 2015, sparking massive outrage and debate about conservation and trophy hunting specifically. Ironically Oxford were researching threats from trophy hunting among other things when he was killed by an American hunter.
Cecilia, D, T and I headed for the restaurant meeting room to listen to the briefing for the big count; Africa’s longest-running census. Volunteers would sit at fixed waterholes for 24 hours to count every mammal that appeared, thus providing vital data for population monitoring, habitat management and anti-poaching during the dry season’s natural animal congregation at water sources. The information gathered would provide a reliable snapshot of the ecosystem’s wildlife.
We left the campsite at dawn after preparing for the trip to the other side of the huge park and the allocated area for the count. We stopped en route to meet other volunteers then travelled in convoy, dropping off people at their allotted pans. Our group was the second last to be dropped off so it took ages to get there, arriving just before midday. It was extremely hot and there was hardly any water in the pan. This did not bode well as it meant there would be fewer animals to count. The rules were to park at least 100 metres away from the pan so we parked the two vehicles next to each other and placed some shade cloth between them.
D helped us put a mattress in the back of Cecilia’s Land Cruiser. This was for the couple on the ‘sleep’ shift. We were to watch for four hours and sleep for two throughout the night. I munched on ham and cheese sandwiches and fanned myself to keep cool then walked around looking for a suitable bush to crouch behind for a wee; there wasn’t much on offer and it was dangerous to walk too far from the cars. A few roan and sable antelope came into view from the left and later a couple of female elephants but there wasn’t much for them to drink because the pan's pump wasn’t working. Cecilia set up a fold-up table between the two cars to play Scrabble. A beautiful sunset and a half moon set the scene but it soon became too dark to see much. T and Cecilia headed off to the mattress around 
8 p.m. while D and I took first watch till midnight. We were constantly lifting up the binoculars to peer into the pan and I joked that at least our arms were getting some exercise after which we had a giggling fit. Chatting helped to keep us awake and alert until we saw around four elephants wandering down to the pan. I took my notebook and the counting page we’d been given and marked them off under the column marked, ‘elephants’.
Next a beautiful bull sable antelope came into view and we watched him for a while after marking him off. One of the huge bull elephants left the pan and started wandering straight up to us. I held my breath, hardly daring to breathe as he walked slowly up to the vehicles and put out an inquisitive trunk before continuing onwards. He was easily twice the height of the Land Cruiser. Cecilia and T woke up and it was our turn to sleep. Cecilia woke us at 3:30 because a wild dog was walking straight past the vehicle and it was too good to miss. I slept again until 5:30 and joined Cecilia and T on their watch. A couple more elephants and a fox wandered to the pan sniffing disdainfully when it found no water. I stretched my legs and went to my open-air loo. The morning air was cool and smelled of dust and thorn bush. A short while later we headed back to main camp, stopping briefly at the hide to hand in our count sheet data.
Cecilia drove me to the airport after lunch and with time for a last coffee before I went through customs. It had been a good break and I was able to take my mind off John’s recent death for a bit. I really missed him and it was strange not bringing back some Tanganda tea leaves for him.
