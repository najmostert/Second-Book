# Chapter 17: Madagascar 2003: The Costa del Sol Quote

Madagascar had interested me for many years, ever since we’d watched an excellent television documentary on the island's unique wildlife. The time had finally come to prepare for a month-long expedition to this amazing island to see it for ourselves. At first, we’d communicated with a travel company whose recent emails promised they would organise our entire itinerary for a fee equivalent to the price of a brand-new house on the Costa del Sol. Col told them to ‘take one of their own journeys’ though not not in so many words and grumbled to the kitchen that he would organise the trip himself. Which he did—with a little help from me and Delia, my friend from our Saudi days who happened to be visiting us in Cape Town at the time. With a few quick taps on the computer keyboard, she had our itinerary linked with all the relevant operators needed to get the holiday from paper to plane. And so we found ourselves hurtling along the highway toward the airport terminal, driven by my nephew Dan, whom we were relentlessly bombarding with last-minute details he was to commit to memory—such as our bank account numbers and which of our household services were due to expire in the next few days should they not see an immediate and sizeable exchange of moola. Dan was handling all this  with remarkable calm for a twenty-one-year old, I thought, as he smoothly steered the car into the drop-off zone at Cape Town International Airport. Col discovered the main plastic clasp on his backpack was missing—a discovery which necessitated a leather belt and thick thread being produced from my hand luggage and wound tightly around the bulging canvas which would hopefully prevent its guts from spilling out all over the shiny airport floor. This classic Heath Robinson repair resulted in a minor, heated family exchange, the  gist of which was who was to blame for this unfortunate state of affairs.

The Usurper of Seats

I’d had just seated myself comfortably on board the domestic flight, strapped into our seatbelts and verified that the free airline magazine was in place, when an irate woman with a shock of black curly hair and a disgruntled expression marched up to our row and insisted that I’d taken her window seat which she claimed she had ‘especially’ booked. We checked our boarding passes and sure enough, Tim was sitting in her seat—the computer system had given us separate seats. I apologised and moved over to the next aisle while Col and Tim moved up a row. My new seat was positioned between two very large gentlemen. I explained to them and to all other interested parties (i.e. those who sit smugly in the knowledge that they possess the correct seat but love to watch others make a public nuisance of themselves) that my family were situated just across the aisle and I would unfortunately have to keep leaning across them to chat. The men begrudgingly allowed me to have the aisle seat, though not without a round of loud muttering and excessive, unnecessary clicking of their seatbelts. I glanced over at Col and Tim and noticed the irritating ‘usurper of seats’ having moved into Tim’s recently vacated seat promptly fell asleep. She woke up only once during the flight to consume her breakfast and slam the porthole cover down, instantly shutting off a magnificently hued sunrise from our view. I amused myself envisioning exactly poking her in the eyes with my fork the moment she woke up.
We were flying Air Madagascar for the international leg of the journey and had the usual frantic rush around Johannesburg International Airport. I was ready for coffee by the time we cleared check-in but Col insisted there wouldn't be enough time before boarding. Undeterred, the moment his back was turned I rushed up to a coffee shop and quickly ordered two coffees and a milkshake. Col had no choice but to slurp his hot drink while looking anxiously at his wristwatch and irritably at me. We made the take-off gate with time to spare and I kept my expression neutral. Once in the air, Col tried out his linguistic abilities and asked a pretty air hostess with dimples if he could have some more ‘vino por favor’—until I pointed out to him that he was speaking Spanish to a Malagasy crew. He said, somewhat snippily that I could do all the foreign speaking from now on. It was a pleasant flight nevertheless and we settled back into our cushions and sighed in contentment, both looking forward to the weeks of wilderness ahead.

As our aircraft approached Madagascar, the plane tilted its wings and the coastline came into view; beautifully fringed with white-sandy beaches and a number of small coral atolls. The outside temperature was reported as 32°C and intensely humid. From the air, Madagascar looked incredibly dry, though there were large, wet areas of terraced rice paddies dotted around the countryside. The indigenous forests are unfortunately disappearing at a devastatingly rate due to a combination of mostly human intervention and environmental factors. The primary drivers of deforestation on the island include widespread slash-and-burn agriculture (known locally as tavy) for subsistence farming, commercial logging, charcoal production and frequent forest fires.

Antananarivo and the Seven-Zero Notes

The airport terminal was small and crowded but we located our cling-wrapped rucksacks easily on the carousel and wandered over to the passport control section. A tiny Madagascan woman claimed our attention with her upheld cardboard sign featuring our name written in lopsided black ink. She introduced herself as Harinambinina—or Hari for short ( I went with the short version). She was a representative of the local agency we had communicated with and proved helpful with navigating the bureaucracy at the bureau de change. It seems Madagascar had recently introduced new banknotes called the Ariary. They featured different denominations, boasting several more zeros on paper. It appeared the new notes were still confusing the locals though. We handed over 400 Euros cash and received a mixture of Ariarys and Malagasy Francs 3,600,000 in return, reminding me of the hyperinflation scenario in Zimbabwe with their dollars. I spent the next ten minutes merrily chatting to a local youth under the misapprehension he was part of our official SETAM travel entourage. It turned out he was simply an airport porter and I was forced to part with a crisp Ar10,000 note after he carried my cling-wrapped luggage a mere twenty paces outside the glass doors. I immediately thought of a few choice French words for him but wisely kept them to myself. A taxi-brousse—or local van—was waiting to transport us to the hotel.
I settled into its spacious interior and gazed out at the passing countryside. It was strange to think we’d been standing on South African soil only a few hours previously and here was this totally new country unfolding. It was reminiscent of India with the number of chickens, ducks, people, traffic and thick dust everywhere. People were milling around the roadsides, buying and selling or just wandering aimlessly down the tracks. Their dress was Western; nothing typically local or traditional around the streets of ‘Tana’ at least. Driving was also the typical ‘anything goes’ affair that is the norm in the global south. We were slightly apprehensive about our allocated hotel as it was thrown in as an added bonus to our plane tickets but I heaved a sigh of relief when we finally arrived at the Hotel Glacier and discovered it to be a solid, historic building. Built in the 1930s perhaps, but spotlessly clean and centrally situated in town. A narrow wooden stairway led up from the street to a simple reception desk. We claimed our room keys with minimal fuss;  a good thing because I was beginning to feel exhausted and my command of the French language was slipping fast. The room was spacious, equipped with three beds, a television and a telephone. Small windows fitted with heavy wooden shutters opened out over the busy streets of Antananarivo.

Headbags in the Tana Rain

The area immediately outside the hotel entrance was bristling with city life. Antananarivo—commonly known as 'Tana'—is Madagascar's bustling capital city. Built across a series of steep rocky hills, the city features a unique architectural blend of historic French colonial buildings and traditional Malagasy multi-story brick homes. Characterised by its red-roofed houses, chaotic open-air markets and winding cobbled streets, it serves as the economic, political and cultural heart of the island. As I gazed down from our window, street children were lying around on pieces of cardboard or just staring vacantly at the passing throngs. We were targeted  the moment we stepped out of the lobby and one little tyke attached himself to me like a puppy to a shoe, asking alternatively for ‘un cadeau’ or ‘un peu de monnaie’.
It started to rain heavily the minute we were too far from the hotel entrance to run back (bloody sod), so we continued down the narrow, cobbled streets of Tana desperately searching for the SETAM tourist office. I asked a few passers-by and was given conflicting directions, accompanied by pointing fingers and a barrage of rapid French. We couldn’t locate the building in the downpour so stepped into a small, corner shop to huddle for a few moments, trying to decide what to do next. Col asked the two girls serving behind the counter if they knew where the SETAM office was located. They shook their heads blankly but smiled encouragingly asking if we wanted to eat something. We didn’t, but purchased a litre bottle of Fanta, which one of the women carefully brought down from a dusty shelf behind the counter. She babbled something at me in French and I shook my head indicating I hadn't understood. She bent down behind the counter and emerged holding three plastic shopping bags and proffered them to us with broad smiles and the second woman mimed that we were to put the bags over our heads. I recognised the words ‘pluie beaucoup’ and gathered they were trying to help keep our hair dry. After thanking them we made our way outside wearing a bag each. Clutching a tepid bottle of Fanta and feeling slightly ridiculous we walked back to the Hotel Glacier. The streets were slick with rain; reminding me of a beautiful watercolour painting with glowing, narrow cobbled streets reflecting colourful buildings in the puddles. After a quick shower we set off down the street to find a suitable restaurant for supper—‘suitable’ being the euphemism for something edible that would travel its way down the alimentary canal without undue haste. We’re careful when visiting a developing country where water consumption can be a hit and miss affair. Le Bistrot at the Hotel de France would do nicely. The décor was pleasing and we sat in the corner close to the entrance. A waiter handed us some menus which we studied with deep concentration as the text was in French. At least here I felt at ease as most of the French words describing the culinary fare were more or less instantly recognisable. While sipping our cold ‘Three Horses’ beers, I noticed a couple at a nearby table who looked familiar. They were the Belgian couple who’d been on the same plane as us and with whom we had chatted in Johannesburg.  Seeing me staring, the blonde woman came over to our table and we enjoyed an interesting conversation in a mixture of ‘Franglais’.
‘Bonjour Janette, Coleen! Ca va? ’Ow are you bose?’  She asked with a smile. Her husband; a farmer in Windhoek, learned over and waved. Later on we heard them arguing with the waiter regarding the bill. I ordered supper, testing my powers of French recall to the maximum as I was also very tired. So, to follow our deux bières, we had une omelette au beurre avec fromage et tomate for myself, un hot-dog for my fils, and un pain au fromage for my mari—not bad, I congratulated myself, seeing as Col was gazing into space with that fixed expression I knew so well and Tim was rolling his bread into vehicle shapes across the table.

Glacier Number Two

The next morning Col and I tried to explain in broken Franglais to the receptionist that we weren’t paying for our previous night's accommodation as the airline had paid for the room as part of our flight package. If that isn’t difficult enough to convey in French, try saying: ‘But we want a room again tonight and we’ll pay for that one,’ followed by, ‘Oh, you don’t have a room available tonight? Well, can you try and find out where we might get a vacancy for tonight and by the way, we also want to book a room here for two weeks’ time,’ in fractured schoolgirl French. Something eventually got through to her because she told us that she could book us into their other Hotel, located on the corner a few steps away from this one and for some reason, went by the same name. She appeared to understand that we wanted to book for later in the month—we’ll test her understanding of that one when we return. Col and I went to inspect Glacier Deux. It seemed to be in a worse state of repair and the entrance looked unwelcoming; a dangerous mess of exposed electrical wires hanging at the top of some very dilapidated wooden stairs. The bedroom, however, actually seemed better than the one we had occupied last night. Col bought eight bottles of water from a shop and we returned to the hotel, dodging aggressive hawkers and street sellers as we navigated the pavement. Many cigarette vendors, fruit sellers and bread hawkers were out in force. Later we located the tour office we’d been looking for and discovered that we’d been standing right outside it yesterday in the rain but hadn't seen the dusty, faded, half-hidden sign board. It was situated down a narrow lane and up some steep stone steps. Three women were in the office that remained dark despite the large circular windows. They were sitting behind their computers but didn’t look particularly busy. They handed over a envelope addressed to Col which revealed several domestic plane tickets and accommodation vouchers. We told them that we’d return later and returned to Glacier Number One to pack. Col and I carried most of the heavy luggage down the street ourselves, taking several hot trips. Each time I tried to find someone at reception to pay for the phone calls we’d made earlier, no one was at the counter or else a completely different girl tried to help but didn’t understand a word of what we wanted to pay for.
A short while later a soft knock at the door announced a petite girl with dark, shoulder-length hair and a timid smile. She introduced herself as Noro Harinambinina. She spoke haltingly and seemed to understand English well enough. It seems the office where she works is located a little distance away from the main SETAM headquarters. Col asked her to organise us a taxi for tomorrow morning while I set about brewing her a cup of tea with my trusty, well-travelled heating element, bottled water and a tin cup.

The Cracked Egg of the Elephant Bird

The taxi zoomed expertly towards the museum—the ‘Parc Botanique et Zoologique de Tsimbazaza’. Tsiry the driver (pronounced ‘Steer’) agreed to wait for us in the lot. We didn’t have much time to spare but with the unsolicited help of a knowledgeable local guide who spoke reasonable English, we saw exhibits of rare endemic birds and chameleons. Then we entered the Musée de l'Académie Malgache, housing natural and cultural history exhibits. The most fascinating of these has to be the skeleton of the extinct elephant bird (Aepyornis), with a colossal, intact, if somewhat cracked, egg nestled perfectly between its large, horny fossilised toes. There were also a few prehistoric remains—mostly of giant lemur-type creatures, an Indri, the strange nocturnal furry creature called the Aye-aye, and a tenrec. So many fascinating, unique creatures; I thought that if we got to see even one of them in the wild, I’ll happy. We trooped out of the dusty, sterile gloom of the museum building into the remains of sunshine where the guide escorted us to a few traditional burial exhibits constructed on-site. We were impressed with him; he seemed to know his stuff, even reciting the Latin scientific names of all the creatures he had shown us. As we parted company near the entrance gates he asked us what we had read in the latest Lonely Planet guidebook about Madagascar and specifically the park. Not having read the chapter on Madagascar, we ad-libbed enough travel jargon to satisfy his professional pride and bid him farewell. Tsiry was still waiting patiently and drove us back through heavy traffic to our hotel. All the municipal vehicles seemed to be going one way, and that was unfortunately our way. With the added frisson of disorganised pedestrians launching from all corners, it made for a thoroughly nerve-wracking drive. We arranged to meet up with Tsiry again when we return to Tana. Our hotel lobby was already a bustling metropolis of people when we got back; they were lounging around, generally doing a good impression of a crowd scene in a film.
I think I may have developed a minor tummy bug; Malagasy malaise or Tana tummy perhaps. I drank most of our remaining bottled water and went down with the boys to have petit déjeuner. I knew we were early for service because we accidentally caught one of the young waitresses changing into her uniform in the dining room corner. We politely pretended we hadn't just seen her standing in her bra and panties when she brought us the breakfast menu and sat outside. My French was tested again; Col always tells me what he wants then waits expectantly for me to translate to the staff when served. ‘We’ managed to get ourselves deux thés et un verre de Coke, pain avec beurre et confiture, et deux croissants. Tim, as the unofficial ‘supplement’, didn’t get a plate, which was okay as I was not feeling well enough to eat, so he munched away happily on my pastry and slurped his Coke—a healthy morning combination-not.

Deforestation and the Mandraka Safes

Our driver for the next leg, Dina, was an affable chap who spoke some English—probably a lot better than I speak French. As I wasn’t feeling too good yet, I found the conversation taxing, especially as Dina punctuated all his answers with a long, drawn-out, ‘Aaaaaahhhh…’ before he finally located the most suitable word in his admittedly limited English vocabulary. I thought at first it was because he was mentally searching for the correct English translation, until I heard him speak his native Malagasy and realised it was worse; he possessed a greater vocabulary in his own language and therefore had a lot more choices to sift through I suppose.

I noticed the devastating effects of deforestation as Dina drove us down the highway. Just tiny, isolated pockets of forest were left once we cleared the outskirts of Tana, though the landscape became noticeably more ‘foresty’ the further away we moved towards Andasibe, a three-hour drive from the capital. Similar style of rustic buildings to Nepal; small, haphazardly built wooden and palm-leaf houses featuring small open alcoves as windows. Many of the roadside houses doubled as little shops selling anything from the ubiquitous French loaf to tin pots. Chickens clucked and strutted about, though most looked in desperate need of a bit more meat under their feathers. Traditional style buildings appeared closer to the mountain ridges: typically rectangular, multi-story clay houses, often featuring steeply angled thatched roofs, constructed using locally sourced red mud, wattle, woven mats and grass. We pulled over at the Mandraka Butterfly Farm, run by a Frenchman with a Friar Tuck hairdo. We paid a small entrance fee to a grey-haired old man sitting inside a thatched-roofed reception room that featured a few dusty T-shirts for sale and not much else. He showed us a couple of well-thumbed, plastic-covered menus and asked us to choose our déjeuner before entering the enclosures. We decided on zebu steak, pork chops, and pommes frites—meat well-done, s’il vous plaît, to lessen the chance of swallowing a tapeworm or two; apparently tapeworm is quite prevalent in Madagascar. The important business of food taken care of, we were formally introduced to our guide who showed us various wire cages and their contents housed nearby. The dwellings were a sort of basic meat-safe size really, but held the most amazing creatures— from the Brookesia (the smallest chameleons of which there are two specimens,) to the largest chameleon in the world: the Parson’s chameleon. A big, magnificent green chap who sat calmly on Tim’s bare hand, his eyes swivelling independently while he adopted an expression of quiet resignation. There were also horned chameleons—bright red and yellow in colour. Our guide explained that one kind of Brookesia pretends to be a dead twig, lying completely rigid if it accidentally falls off a branch. Two types of Madagascan boa constrictors were on display: a greenish-brown tree boa (which just lives in trees but eats from the ground) and a ground boa—a beautifully patterned brown snake. The guide later showed us the beautiful Madagascar moon moth, or comet moth with its long, trailing crimson-and-yellow wings. The various stick insects that we were shown were amazing as well; masters of natural disguise, they looked like branches, twigs and leaves - hard to distinguish from the real foliage.

The Bright Tomato Frog

Next in line were the various colourful frogs. Small, neon green ones, huge red ones, yellow and mottled frogs—they came in a variety of brilliant colours and sizes, from thumb-sized to hand-sized. I loved the big, fat, bright red tomato frog (Dyscophus antongilii). Our guide was knowledgeable and also knew all the scientific names as well as the common terms, as did the chap in Tana. We were quite impressed with the calibre of Madagascan guides so far. This one was also very helpful when it came to photography; gently blowing on a little Mantella frog in his cupped hand to get the creature to keep still long enough for Col to get focus. He placed the specimens against natural backgrounds conducive to a good photograph. It was fascinating and we we’re more than happy to pay a little extra for his expertise. He then led us up a steep hill where our lunch was awaiting our arrival inside a large, open-style wooden pavilion that offered a magnificent view of the surrounding green valley. I washed my hands in a bathroom that led straight off the large wooden verandah, then entered the airy, sparsely furnished dining room where a small, round wooden table had been laid with a beautiful embroidered tablecloth. Dina shared well-cooked zebu steak and chips with us. The contented sound of munching was punctuated only by the occasional long, ‘Eerrrhhhh…’ that announced Dina was preparing to speak. After this pleasant repast, we took our leave, thanked our hosts and departed for Vakona Lodge, situated in Andasibe (which was named Perinet during French colonial times). The changing landscape led us further into the dense, humid coastal forest. Vakona Lodge sat amid a beautiful, tranquil forest; a collection of pagoda-style dining and entertainment rondavels surrounded by a large pond, complete with wooden bridges, walkways and lush tropical flowers. The guest bungalows were dotted along the edge of the mountainside and were accessible by a number of wooden steps. The view from our room was fantastic.

Andasibe and the Leech on the Crown

It started raining heavily shortly after we arrived and everything in the forest glistened with fresh water drops. The pink rhododendrons and wild Madagascan orchids practically glowed in the waning light. I took out the specialised forest-wear we’d brought from Cape Town in preparation for our stay; this included canvas leech protectors, head torches and waterproof capes in lurid plastic colours. I laid these on our bed before walking down the steps to the main pagoda for afternoon tea. The view was great; the tea, unfortunately wasn't. After dressing up in our hiking gear, we set off into the forest with Dina and a local tracker named Etienne. The first wild animals we spotted were fairly close to the roadside: a couple of grey bamboo lemurs high up in the bamboo treetops. It was our first sighting of wild lemurs and although the view was a bit obscured by branches, it was still thrilling. I took several photos of dark smudges against some trees, reasoning that I could always edit later on my laptop. Dina drove the Cruiser up a dusty roadside and parked next to the forest boundary and we started our foray into the dripping forest canopy, climbing steadily up some steep pathways covered in thick vines and springy undergrowth. The trees were tall, thin and sparse, interspersed with a number of strangely patterned palms. I found an interesting little fungus—white and waxy, glistening in a ghostly fashion on a fallen log and we trampled past a bush bearing the colour of a pale blueberry that practically screamed ‘poisonous’. The forest trees were covered in thick lichen and hanging cobwebs of ‘old man's beard’ moss. It was very damp under the canopy, with a constant, rhythmic dripping from the leaves. I stopped halfway up the ridge with Col to catch our breath while Etienne scampered off ahead to look for lemurs for us and we came right across our first wild brown lemur sitting high above us in the branches. As we climbed, I noticed the presence of small brown leeches looping along the bushes. Col and Etienne casually brushed a few off their bare arms. Tim pouted from the rear, he announced his wish to also be sucked on by a wild leech! His wish was granted a short while later. He stopped suddenly on the path and said he felt something cold on top of his head. I peered closely and found a fat, blood-filled leech attached to his scalp. I pulled quickly and it came off in my fingers, leaving a small puddle of blood trickling down his forehead. Tim, being satisfied with his injury, happily carried on up the forest path.

The Wail of the Indri

Etienne was looking out for the rare, endemic diademed sifaka. Suddenly, a haunting wail rent the still, humid air- It was an Indri (Indri indri). We immediately quickened our pace and followed the direction of the echoing sound. It was reaching a massive crescendo now—a truly eerie, whale-like, primeval sound.  After panting and scrambling over the slippery, muddy slopes we finally came across a lone Indri. It was sitting on a thin vertical tree trunk and unhelpfully presented its bottom to our cameras. As we stood craning our necks and gazing up in rapt attention, it slowly turned its head towards us; it had a small, black, almost dog-like face with round, woolly ears. Its body was a striking black and white and unlike other lemur species, no tail. It gradually moved on through the canopy and we returned to Dina via the bramble-twisted path. After coffee at the lodge, we collected our attire for a sortie de nuit—a guided night walk along the road. I met the guest from the next bungalow; he only spoke French so I tortured him with my petit peu de français. He joined us and another small group of visiting Americans on the night walk. Our ‘supplement’, as Tim is officially referred to, actually left his Game Boy behind long enough to join us—he also wanted to try out his head torch in the jungle. Our guide for the night walk carried a very powerful spotlight and as his beam played over the surrounding roadside foliage, he gave a low grunt and motioned our group over to a bush. There sat a small, bright green chameleon, sitting perfectly motionless on a light branch. The guide shone his light high into the canopy of trees, and the small, indignant face of a mouse lemur (Microcebus) was revealed, looking similar to a bushbaby— the African equivalent. It was a dark brown colour with enormous, reflective yellow eyes and a longish tail. A frantic flurry of whirring and clicking camera noises ensued. After watching the tiny primate for ten minutes we returned to the pagoda for supper. The American group featured a rather loud, irritating woman who was making herself heard across the dining room. She was aggressively asking the rather bemused Malagasy woman behind the reception desk in strident tones to change her double bed booking for a single one, loudly announcing that she didn’t want to sleep with her husband tonight. Col and I guessed the feeling was mutual.

Feon' ny Ala and the Giraffe Beetle

After breakfast the next morning we left Vakona Lodge and made our way down the track to the Hotel Feon' ny Ala, located not too far down the road as it turned out. Dina arrived in the car to move our bags. Feon' ny Ala was also pretty and a lot less pricey on our budget. The accommodation consisted of rustic A-frame timber huts constructed along a stretch of path that ran parallel to the Mandrare River. The dining hall came straight off the main car park and an open verandah overlooked the flowing river where wooden tables and easy chairs invited guests to sit and relax with a cold drink. Etienne, acting as our guide again met us at the hotel gates where we followed him a short distance to the official park entrance office to show our respective tourist visas. A large, mottled chameleon greeted us at the front steps, sitting calmly on a small bush. We all oohed and aahed and Etienne showed us a tiny Brookesia nana—the smallest chameleon in the world. It was literally as small as Tim’s thumb; a dull greenish-grey colour with a short, thin tail. Etienne demonstrated his incredible tracking expertise in the forest by spotting three sleeping woolly lemurs (Avahi) at the top of a huge palm tree. They cuddled tightly together in a ball against the fronds. Etienne pointed out a bizarre giraffe weevil (Trachelophorus giraffa) resting on a leaf with striking red and black colouring. A long, thin, jointed neck gave the insect its descriptive name. The highlight for me was seeing a Parson's chameleon, sitting like a giant emerald on a thick branch just off the path. The Parson's can exhibit a wide range of bright colours, including turquoise and yellow, but this specimen was a beautiful green. It’s the largest chameleon in the world and is the size of a large domestic cat. I was so pleased to see one and exclaimed to Col that I had now seen the smallest and the largest chameleon in the world on the same walk. Another sleeping woolly lemur lay on a branch somewhat closer to the ground than the first three we had seen. It started to rain heavily in the forest so we donned our brightly coloured plastic ponchos; giving a moving pack of Smarties—Col had a bright blue one, Tim’s was red, and mine a loud banana yellow.

A soft rain was falling as we awoke. The air felt warm and damp and the nearby forest hung heavily veiled in a misty curtain of light. I brewed some tea in our tin mugs and we made our way in our smelly, unwashed ‘three walks’ clothes down to the restaurant for breakfast. As we had arrived late for our scheduled trek, we were forced to bolt our food down in under five minutes—not a pleasant sensation at all. Predictably, the taste of scrambled eggs au beurre repeated on me as we walked towards the main gates to meet Etienne to continue along the forest path. Etienne wanted to show us a leaf-tailed gecko (Uroplatus) that he’d spotted from his bicycle during his commute to work. When we arrived at the tree, I was amazed that he’d managed to pick it out from a moving bicycle; it was by far the most flawlessly camouflaged creature I’d seen. Even when looking directly at the trunk, it was incredibly hard to identify. Leaf-tailed geckos utilise their leaf-shaped tails, dermal flaps and mottled skin to flawlessly mimic dead leaves or rough tree bark, helping them remain invisible to forest predators.
A bit further on, several groups of tourists were staring intently up into the high branches. Following the line of their gaze, I saw our first Indri from this side of the reserve. I was completely enamoured; they look like giant pandas or plush teddy bears, possessing small, remarkably dog-like heads topped with tufted woolly ears, positioned on large black-and-white torsos.. I was totally smitten and like the rest of the group, stood transfixed at a small group of six or seven individuals perched within a few feet of our heads. It was difficult to photograph them clearly against the heavy, grey backdrop of morning light so after a while we set off again up the slippery slopes. Along the ridge, we came across a Madagascan coucal (Centropus toulou); it’s a unique species of cuckoo and it sounds like our local African coucal when calling through the thicket. We also logged a few Madagascan bee-eaters flitting around the bushes, while a female Parson's chameleon sporting brilliant emerald-green colouring stared rigidly down at us. Brown lemurs clung casually from the branches and blue kingfishers, red-tailed vangas, crested drongos, and later a much better view of the spectacular blue vanga (Cyanolanius madagascarinus) flew around the lower branches of the forest canopy.

The Fish Farm and the Shandy

At the nearby rustic fish farm enclosure, we came across a large troop of brown lemurs jumping noisily around the overhanging trees. Col lent Tim his old camera for the afternoon and he was hooked; he started snapping away merrily at all and sundry, taking several close-up shots of the lemurs that actually came out remarkably well. Etienne then led us to view a sleeping Madagascar tree boa (Sanzinia madagascariensis), curled up neatly near the water's edge, and slithered away slowly into the reeds as we approached. Fortunately, the snakes in Madagascar are mostly non-poisonous with only a few mildly venomous back-fanged varieties on the island so I wasn't nearly as anxious as I normally am when wandering around a thick tropical forest. We returned to the entrance, chatting in low, excited whispers about our early morning wildlife experiences. Rum, Death, and the Concept of Fady

Our dilemma was what to do regarding this somewhat grisly find. We were to leave the following morning for Maroantsetra and I felt a little relieved to escape the area. The Malagasy people possess very definite, strict cultural rules and rituals surrounding death and we were afraid we may have unwittingly committed some serious fady. The term fady refers to sacred cultural taboos or spiritual prohibitions that govern various aspects of daily life—ranging from specific actions and holy places to certain foods and animals. The concept of death carries some of the most serious fady on the island including the famous Famadihana (the turning of the bones) ritual.

Masoala: The Simpleton and the Sea

The boat trip across the open water to Masoala was one of the absolute worst maritime experiences I have ever endured, mostly, I suppose, due to my permanent fear of capsizing, paired with a sizeable mistrust in our boatman. The man turned out to be the exact same simpleton who had joined our forest walk yesterday— clearly not playing with a full deck of cards. This cognitive deficit translated into some incredibly hairy moments in the rough, heavy sea. I was seated right in the front bow and spent the entire two hours clutching the metal safety railing with my eyes tightly closed. At one point, I opened my mouth to shout an instruction to Tim, and instantly received a massive, choking mouthful of salt water for my pains. We eventually arrived at the beach, and as the boat was being tied up to the shore, I looked over at a circle of traditional banana-leaf huts constructed on raised wooden platforms. The setup looked rustic but deeply pleasant. I uncurled my stiff limbs, unclenched my frozen fingers from the railings, and hopped off the hull onto the beige sand. Tim passed me my heavy rucksack and camera gear, and we manually lugged all our bags up a short, steep hill to the campsite clearing where the lodge owner, Olivier, was waiting to greet us. He wore a typically classic Gallic visage, characterised by a large nose, dark curly hair, and a mouth set in a permanent expression of bored moue. He showed us to our respective huts, and I immediately stripped off my wet clothes, had a cold shower, and found some dry things to put on. Supper was fresh crayfish and sautéed vegetables served with riz. The camp cook was a tall, gangly Madagascan girl with two thick plaits of hair and a very welcome smile. Suddenly, our French friend from Nosy Mangabe came bounding out of the forest trees looking exactly like a character straight out of an Asterix and Obelix comic book. He announced to the table in his heavily accented voice that he had just spent six gruelling hours searching the canopy for the rare ‘wed-wuffed’ (red-ruffed) lemur, with nary a single one in sight. Our new local guide, Seraphin, subsequently took us on a late hike up the mountain ridge; the short, steep incline soon transformed into a dense, rocky, slippery, and multi-coloured forest ecosystem. Along the trail, I spotted some beautiful blue-feathered couas flitting through the branches, and a tiny Brookesia chameleon resting on a low branch. After a breakfast of omelettes, toast, and tea the next morning, we set off for another long forest walk. Seraphin proved excellent, pointing out various unique animals as we passed: a bright green leaf frog, some more blue coua birds in the upper canopy, and towards the end of the trek, a really gorgeously hued bird—the spectacular blue vanga. The bird possessed a bright, vivid blue back and head contrasted with a startling blue beak. We also logged Madagascar’s answer to the hornbill—a brilliant red fody— but we had absolutely no luck locating the rare red-ruffed lemur (Varecia rubra). Seraphin explained sadly that the primates had recently been heavily poached in this sector and were now terrified of humans, hiding exclusively in the uppermost parts of the deep forest. How thoroughly horrid humanity is in such cases, and how tragic for the local wildlife. We had sardines for lunch, then I joined the boys for a spot of snorkelling off the beach. The water was a beautiful, clear tropical blue today. I found a massive, empty clam shell in the sand and decided it would make a perfect souvenir to take home with me, carrying it back up the hill to camp where I had a short sleep, as I was suffering from an intense, hormone-related headache. Later on, I took a walk down to the shoreline with Col, and we shared a cold beer while watching the dark sea swallow the setting sun. The distinct, white Cheshire Cat smile of a crescent moon rose in a cloudless sky as the evening's second act.

Tenrecs in a Rusty Bucket

The next morning, we were up early to catch the return boat. After slurping down my mug of hot tea, I struck up a conversation with a new guide named Arnaud. He told me that there had been a healthy population of fifty red-ruffed lemurs sighted in this sector back in February. However, when he returned to the site in July accompanying an American tourist, a group of military men had arrived that afternoon and loud gunshots were heard echoing through the trees. The trackers hadn't seen a single lemur after that incident, and the American had apparently contacted ANGAP and the local police, but Arnaud wasn't sure if any official action had ever been taken. All things considered, Col was remarkably lucky to have seen them yesterday. The sea was beautifully calm today, so the boat ride wasn't nearly as terrifying as before, for which I was extremely grateful to the heavens. After disembarking, we walked over to the Café Rive for a drink of local coco rum, when suddenly Racoute appeared out of the bushes, waving his arms in an excited manner. ‘Come, come!’ he shouted. ‘I have a tenrec for you to see!’ We rushed outside the café to find a rusty metal bucket sitting on the ground. Racoute pointed excitedly into the depths, and we bent down to take a look. A tiny, striped tenrec was resting quietly at the bottom of the tin. Tenrecs are a unique family of mammals endemic to Madagascar. While they look remarkably similar to common hedgehogs due to evolutionary convergence, they are actually entirely unrelated genetically. Col desperately wanted to photograph the tenrec in a significantly more natural wilderness surroundings than a rusty bucket, so Racoute tipped the creature straight out onto the green lawn. A hilarious five minutes immediately ensued, with the three of us frantically chasing the poor thing around the dark garden equipped with our head torches and cameras.

The Pig in the Underbelly

While Tim and Racoute disappeared for a final quad-bike ride around the property, Col and I sat quietly on the verandah attempting to photograph a beautiful fody that was flitting through the nearby forest foliage, before finally departing for the local airport. Our ‘toy’ plane eventually arrived on the tarmac. It was exceptionally small—possessing no more than eight passenger seats in total. I was allocated a seat right next to the window and watched in absolute amazement as the ground crew manually loaded a live, squeaking pig into the aircraft's tiny underbelly cargo hold. The captain pushed the cockpit door open, leaned back into the cabin, and loudly asked the stewardess for his lunch box. I buckled my seatbelt and cast my eyes over the incredibly shabby interior, most of which could have desperately used a severe structural makeover. We stopped briefly at a very dusty, hot, and windblown landing strip called Mandritsara, where a few passengers—and the live pig —were offloaded onto the tarmac. We finally landed at our destination with a huge, jarring bump on a runway that was significantly more potholed than the lunar surface. The coastal air was intensely hot and dry, making it somewhat bearable for my lungs. Col hailed a taxi to take our family straight to the Hotel de la Plage. The hotel was run by a French couple, and we checked into a room featuring plenty of traversins—those long, cylindrical French bolster pillows—on the beds, which Col immediately grumbled at and flung straight onto the floorboards. We wandered down to the residents' lounge, where the three of us sat somewhat dejectedly trying to figure out what to do with our afternoon. Suddenly, the hotel chef marched into the room and gruffly told us to leave the lounge immediately because it was closed for the day. Feeling completely unwelcome on the premises, we took a walk down the local road. The neighborhood didn't possess a single shred of the charm or natural beauty of the other places we had visited on the island; instead, it was dusty, windblown, and featured a massive amount of rubbish strewn around dozens of derelict, crumbling buildings. We passed the carcass of a dead dog on the side of the road with its guts spilling all over the concrete pavement, which put our family into an even deeper melancholic mood. After walking back to hotel ‘Grubby’ feeling intensely dehydrated and irritable, we ordered a supper that took an absolute age to arrive from the kitchen, then retreated to our room to tuck ourselves tightly inside the fly-spattered mosquito net. This was definitely the absolute low point of the entire holiday.

Listing Ferries and Petrol Tankers

The next morning, we were all up early, slowly moved our heavy luggage over to an alternative hotel, and checked out of the De la Plage. The hotel owner barely even looked up from his book when we dropped our room keys onto the front counter. I somewhat uncharitably imagined him choking to death on a mouldy, stale croissant, and instantly felt much better about our departure. Our new room was smaller but significantly more tastefully designed. I brewed our family some hot tea, and then we caught a lift with a local construction worker down to the pont de bateaux— or the vehicle ferry, to the uninitiated. The ferry transported us across the water to a small fishing village named Katsepy, located northwest of Majunga. There, we found our absolute haven for the day at Madame Bouchard’s establishment—a beautifully furnished, elegant niche in the otherwise squalid village surroundings. Madame Bouchard’s pretty daughter took our order, and soon we were happily drinking hot tea poured from a gold teapot and munching on fresh chocolate croissants. I immediately felt so much better in spirit. Tim and Col set off to catch butterflies along the paths, while I sat chatting with Madame Bouchard the younger about regional politics, the war, and international tourism (she owned a small hotel and restaurant in central Mahajanga). Later on, I joined Col and Tim for a long walk along the beach picking up shells, watching the local fishermen casting their nets wide into the ocean next to massive piles of shrimp drying on rattan mats in the sun. Later that afternoon, we boarded the return ferry and sat on the top open deck. The vessel turned very slowly in the surf and moved close to the beach line to allow nine large zebu cattle to clamber on board the deck. We set off across the bay again, but it wasn’t long before the engine was suddenly cut and the boat stopped dead in the water. It seemed a passenger had fallen overboard. People on the deck immediately lunged over to the left railing to watch the drama, locating a figure swimming crazily in circles in the current. Several lifebuoys were flung at his head, and eventually, he was hoisted up the hull by several onlookers. I was feeling intensely anxious, because all 250 passengers on board had moved completely to the left side of the deck, and the ferry was listing ominously into the water line. I marched resolutely over to the right side of the deck in a completely futile, solitary attempt to balance the weight of the ship. I called out in an authoritative voice, ‘Never mind gawping at the drowning man! Come back to your seats!’ Naturally, absolutely no one took the slightest bit of notice of me. The captain eventually restored order on the deck, and we set off again into the choppy sea, tilting and rolling aggressively against the high, angry waves. To add severely to my nautical discomfort, there was a large commercial petrol tanker loaded on board the deck with neither a driver inside nor a single physical restraint, and the massive truck rolled backwards and forwards with the motion of each passing wave.

Ampijoroa and the Side-Dancing Sifakas

The inland heat was intense, and the surrounding countryside was incredibly dry and completely denuded of trees. The taxi driver brought our family to the Parc National d'Ampijoroa more by pure luck than actual navigational design. Remarkably, given that we hadn't booked any reservations, they had two rooms available for us. We dropped off our suitcases and went into the park in search of Guy the guide (pronounced the French way, ‘Gee’). Eric was also at the camp and joined our family on a guided night walk through the dry deciduous forest ecosystem. Along the flat dirt paths, our head torches picked out a horned rhinoceros chameleon (Calumma nasutum), a forest turtle, and some blue vangas. Then, suddenly, we encountered a troop of Coquerel's sifakas (Propithecus coquereli) resting in the branches—beautiful lemurs with rich mahogany and chocolate-coloured arms contrasted with snowy white backs. They possessed large, round green eyes and looked exactly like giant white teddy bears. The following morning, we went looking for the specialized Angonoka tortoise enclosure. The Angonoka tortoise—also known as the ploughshare tortoise (Astrochelys yniphora)—is considered the rarest tortoise species in the world, globally renowned for its unique, highly domed golden shell. The local breeding enclosure is managed by the Durrell Wildlife Conservation Trust (Gerald Durrell's famous zoo based in the U.K.), which was originally established on the island back in 1995 to protect the species from extinction. The Angonoka tortoise is critically endangered primarily because it is highly sought after by poachers for the illegal international exotic pet trade. Col and I spent a wonderful hour photographing some more sifakas in the main camp area. It was highly entertaining watching their social antics and the unique, rhythmic side-dance they perform on two legs when moving across open ground. Along the highway, I noticed a local man selling fresh bananas, pickled mangoes, dried fish, and small yams at the side of the road, and I purchased a bunch of bananas. He wore an old blue cap, and his wife, who joined him from the bushes, wore a dress while balancing three straw hats stacked simultaneously on her head. The next morning, we joined Guy for a forest walk and scored a sighting of the Madagascar fish eagle (Haliaeetus vociferoides), which is apparently exceptionally rare. Guy had brought along a portable tape recorder to attract wildlife, and the moment he pressed play, the three of us collapsed with laughter. There was a faint recording of the rare asity bird playing, but the bird's call was almost completely drowned out by the loud sound of a car engine on the tape. Col whispered dryly that he didn’t know asities could drive. The ultimate laugh was on us, however, because not half an hour later, we came right across a live, rare velvet asity perched high up in a tree trunk, perfectly visible to our lenses. It was very exciting.

A Swiss Cheese Door in Tana

The road back to Tana was completely crisscrossed with bridges of every imaginable size and structural description. We crossed over the massive waterfalls of the Betsiboka River, then pulled over again at a horrible, dry, dusty, and intensely humid roadside village. My bladder was bursting for the loo, and a small, smiling local woman dressed in a faded yellow dress escorted me down a rat-infested alleyway to a dirty wooden door that featured more holes than a Swiss cheese. I opened the creaky timber door with intense trepidation and peered into the gloom. I instantly clapped my hand over my nose; flies were swarming around the open pit latrine like military drones. I noticed a plastic jug and a bucket of dirty brown water sitting on the floor. I finished my business as quickly as humanly possible and sprinted out of the alley, feeling instantly remorseful that the local people have to live with these unhygienic conditions on a daily basis. As we drove higher into the highlands, the weather became really cold. A huge, blood-red moon hovered in the night sky, casting a pale crimson light over the mountainous landscape. Our driver kindly lent Tim and me a heavy blanket to share in the back seat. He entertained our family with music played from an old car cassette deck, and we listened to a tape whose musical genre randomly ranged all the way from The Ink Spots to classic Reggae, finally arriving back at the Hotel Glacier at one o'clock in the morning. The half-asleep receptionist at the desk flatly told us there wasn't a room available for us tonight. After consulting a tattered paper register, he announced that our reservation was actually for tomorrow night. Paul, our excellent driver, saved the night once more by driving our tired family to the rather more expensive Hotel de la France down the street. At least we secured a clean room for what was left of the night.

The Five-Star Teabags

The next morning, I marched my own personal tin container of powdered milk and my box of choice tea bags straight into the five-star hotel dining room, much to Col’s intense amusement. One simply has to be fiercely careful with the local tap water, and it was essential for my sanity that I start the day with a proper, strong cuppa. Our ground-floor room back at the Hotel Glacier faced a very busy municipal thoroughfare, and we had more than a couple of Vazahas—the local Malagasy term for white foreigners— nodding a polite ‘Bonjour’ at us through the window frames. After enjoying a simple lunch, we caught a taxi to visit the Tsimbazaza Zoo, which had been originally founded back in 1925. The grounds included a large central lake, a botanical garden, and the zoo enclosures themselves. Whilst our family doesn’t always enjoy seeing wild animals in zoos, it can sometimes present a unique opportunity to view rare, elusive species that we wouldn’t otherwise encounter in the deep forest. Col unexpectedly bumped into an old tracking guide from a previous safari visit, and we must have significantly overpaid the man back then judging by the enthusiastic, booming welcome we received. By 5 p.m., most of the daily visitors had departed the park gates, and we met up with Bruno—our specialized night guide whose services we had purchased specifically to view a captive Aye-aye. These fascinating creatures are strictly nocturnal, so dusk was the golden hour to observe them active. Night descended rapidly over the cages; one minute we were following Bruno into the timber enclosures, and the next minute, darkness had completely enveloped the park, removing all vision. Bruno switched on his hefty spotlight torch and shone the beam directly into a large enclosure. The very first things that illuminated in the dark were two huge, round orange eyes, then gradually the entire strange body was revealed in the dim light like the Cheshire Cat. He was significantly bigger than I had imagined from books— the actual size of a small domestic dog, equipped with a huge, bushy tail. I carefully noticed his extra-long middle finger and his coarse, dark brown fur. This specimen darted frantically around the branches of the cage, making photography a bit tricky, but we managed to capture some excellent footage of this magnificently weird primate. Upon returning to the Hotel Glacier, we found Harinambinina waiting for us in the lobby, so we warmly invited her to join our family for supper. In her honor, we ordered a traditional Malagasy meal consisting of spinach, rice, humped zebu steak, and shredded manioc leaves. We hugged her a fond farewell at the entrance, as we would not be seeing her again before our flight. À bientôt, Madagascar!
