# Chapter 17: Madagascar 2003: The Costa del Sol Quote

Madagascar had interested us for many years ever since we watched an excellent documentary on the wildlife there, and the time had come to prepare for a month-long visit to this amazing island and see for ourselves. First, we had to deal with the so-called ‘exclusive’ company whose recent emails promised they would organise everything for us for only the same price as a new house on the Costa del Sol. Col told them to ‘take one of their own journeys’ (though not in so many words!) and muttered that he would organise the trip himself. Which he did—with a little help from me and Delia, my friend from Saudi days who was visiting at the time. With a few quick taps on the computer, she had us linked with all the relevant people we needed to get the holiday going from paper to the actual thing.

And so that day we found ourselves hurtling along to the airport, driven by my nephew, Dan, whom we were bombarding with last-minute details that he was to commit to memory, such as our bank numbers and which of the services were due to expire in the next few days should they not see an immediate and sizeable exchange of moola. Dan was handling all this with remarkable calm for a 21-year-old, I thought, as he smoothly drove into the dark stillness of Cape Town International Airport.

We discovered that Col’s rucksack clasp was missing, a discovery which necessitated a belt and thread being quickly produced and wound around the bulging rucksack, which would hopefully prevent its guts from spilling out all over the shiny airport floor. This Heath Robinson affair resulted in a minor heated exchange, the gist of which was who was to blame for this unfortunate state of affairs. Col gave a final yank to the belt, muttering that he hoped all our camping gear within would remain in situ—at least until we got to Johannesburg airport.

The Usurper of Seats

We had just seated ourselves comfortably, strapped in and checked that the free airline magazine was in place, when an irate woman with a shock of black curly hair and a very disgruntled expression came to my seat and insisted we had taken her window seat, which she had ‘especially’ booked. We checked our seat numbers and sure enough, Tim was sitting in her seat—it seemed we had been given separate seats. I apologised and went over to the next aisle while Col and Tim moved up one. ‘My’ seat was supposed to be in between two large gentlemen. I explained to them and all other interested parties (i.e. those who sat smugly in the knowledge that they had the right seat but loved to watch others make a public nuisance of themselves) that my family were just across the aisle and I would have to keep leaning across them to chat. I was allowed to have the outer aisle seat, though not without loud muttering and excessive and unnecessary clicking of seatbelts.

I glanced over at Col and Tim and noted the irritating ‘usurper of seats’ had moved into Tim’s newly vacated seat and was fast asleep. She woke up only once during the flight to eat breakfast and close the porthole cover, instantly shutting off the magnificently hued sunrise from view. I amused myself envisioning how I’d poke her in the eyes when she woke up.

We were flying Air Madagascar and had the usual rush around Johannesburg International Airport. I was really ready for a coffee by the time we checked in, but Col said there wouldn't be time. So, when his back was turned, I rushed up to a coffee shop and quickly ordered two coffees and a milkshake. Col had no choice but to slurp his while looking anxiously at his watch and irritably at me. We made take-off with time to spare and I kept my expression neutral.

Col thought he was doing well with his linguistic abilities and asked the pretty air hostess with dimples if he could have some more ‘vino por favor’ until I pointed out he was speaking Spanish. He said, somewhat snippily, that I could do all the foreign speaking from then on. It was a pleasant flight nevertheless, and we settled ourselves into our seats and sighed in contentment, both looking forward to the weeks ahead.

As we approached Madagascar the plane tilted and the coastline came into view, fringed with white-sanded beaches and a number of small atolls.

The outside temperature was about 32°C and humid. From the air, Madagascar looked really dry, though there were large, wet areas of rice paddies dotted around the countryside. The forests were unfortunately disappearing at a fast rate due to a combination of human pressures and environmental factors, including widespread slash-and-burn agriculture for subsistence logging and the illegal trade of wood, charcoal production and fuelwood extraction for energy, as well as forest fires. It was all very worrying.

ANTANANARIVO

The airport was small and crowded but we found our cling-wrapped cocoons of rucksacks easily then wandered over to the passport section. A tiny Madagascan woman claimed our attention with her upheld board with our name written in lopsided black ink. She introduced herself as… gosh, forgotten already, but I knew it had several syllables and sounded foreign. Ah yes, Harinambinina, or Hari for short. She was part of the agency we had communicated with and she was very helpful with the bureaucracy at the bureau de change. It seemed Madagascar had new banknotes called Ariary. They had different denominations (several more zeros, I noticed with interest). It appeared the new notes were still confusing the locals, though. We handed over 400 euros and received 3,600,000 Malagasy francs in return. It reminded us of the present Zimbabwe situation with dollars.

I had been merrily chatting away to a local youth under the misapprehension he was part of the SETAM entourage. It turned out he was a porter and we had to part with 10,000 francs after he took our cling-wrapped luggage 20 paces outside. I immediately thought of a few more French words but wisely kept them to myself.

A taxi-brousse—or van—was waiting to take us to the hotel. I settled into its spacious interior and gazed out at the passing countryside. It was strange to think we had been on South African soil only a few hours before and here was this totally new country. It was reminiscent of India with chickens, ducks, children and dust everywhere. People were milling around, buying and selling, or just wandering aimlessly.

Their dress was basically Western; nothing typically local around ‘Tana. The driving was also the typical ‘anything goes’ affair that was the norm in the Middle East. We were slightly apprehensive about the hotel as it was an added ‘bonus’ to the ticket but heaved a sigh of relief when we arrived at Hotel Glacier and discovered it to be a solid building; built in the 1930s, perhaps, but clean and centrally situated. A narrow wooden stairway led up to a simple reception area. We claimed our room and keys with minimal fuss, which was a good thing because I was beginning to feel tired and my command of the French language was slipping fast. The room was spacious with three beds, a TV, and a phone. Small windows with wooden shutters opened out to the busy streets of Antananarivo. We set about unwrapping our three cocoons of luggage, which had been cling-wrapped for security at Johannesburg airport.

The area immediately outside the hotel entrance was full of city life.

Tana was Madagascar's bustling capital, a vibrant city built on steep hills, blending French colonial architecture with Malagasy traditions, featuring red-roofed houses, busy markets, and winding streets, serving as the nation's economic, political, and cultural heart, a gateway to the island, and home to unique history and biodiversity.

As I gazed down, street kids were lying around on pieces of cardboard or just staring vacantly at the passing throngs of people. One of the children joined us as soon as we were spotted, and hung on persistently like a puppy to a shoe, asking alternately for ‘un cadeau’ or ‘un peu de monnaie’.

It started to rain the minute we were too far from the hotel to run back (sod's law), so we continued down the narrow cobbled side streets of ‘Tana, looking for the SETAM tourist office. I enquired of a few passers-by and we were given various directions, accompanied by pointing fingers and a barrage of rapid French. We couldn’t find the building in the rain, so entered a small open shop and huddled for a few moments, trying to decide what to do. Col asked the two local girls serving behind the counter if they knew where SETAM was. They shook their heads but smiled encouragingly and asked if we wanted to eat something. We didn’t, but we bought a litre bottle of Fanta which one of the women carefully brought down from a narrow, dusty shelf behind the counter. She babbled something at me in rapid French, and I shook my head, indicating I hadn't understood. She bent down behind the counter and came up with three plastic bags. They were proffered to us with broad smiles, and the other woman mimed that we were to put them over our heads. I recognised the words ‘pluie beaucoup’ and gathered they would help keep our heads dry from the persistent rain. We thanked them both and made our way outside with plastic-covered heads, clutching a tepid bottle of Fanta and feeling slightly ridiculous, we walked back towards the Hotel Glacier.

The streets were slick with rain, and I was reminded of a watercolour painting with glowing narrow cobbled streets that reflected colourful buildings in the puddles.

Back at the hotel, we each had a quick shower and changed into something warmer, then set off down the street to see if we could find a suitable restaurant for supper—‘suitable’ being the euphemism for something edible that would travel its way down the alimentary canal without undue haste. We were always careful when visiting a third world country. Our hotel restaurant was closed but we discovered Le Bistrot at the Hotel de France and decided it would do nicely. The décor was pleasing and we sat in an enclosed area in the corner close to the entrance. A Filipino waiter handed us some menus, which we studied with deep concentration as it was all in French. At least here I felt at ease, as most of the French words describing the fare were more or less instantly recognisable.

While sipping our ‘Three Horses’ beers, we noticed a couple at a nearby table who looked familiar. It turned out they were the Belgians who had been on the same plane as us and with whom we’d met in Johannesburg. The blonde woman came over to our table and we had an interesting conversation in a mixture of ‘Franglais’. He was a farmer in Windhoek but she was French. Later, we heard them arguing with the waiter about the bill.

I ordered supper, which tested my powers of French recall to the max, as I was very tired by now. So, to follow our deux bières, we had une omelette au beurre avec fromage et tomate, et un hotdog pour mon fils, et un pain au fromage pour mon mari—not bad I thought, congratulating myself seeing as none of my family were. The bread rolls were huge French loaves. We almost couldn’t finish them.

Col and I tried to explain in Franglais to the receptionist that we weren’t paying for our accommodation as the airline had paid for the room as part of the ticket. If that wasn’t difficult enough to say in French, try saying, ‘but we want a room again tonight, and we’ll pay for that one,’ followed by, ‘Oh, you don’t have a room tonight? Well, can you try and find out where we might get a room for tonight, and by the way, we also want to book a room here for two weeks’ time’, in fractured schoolgirl French. Something got through to her because she told us that she could book us into the other Glacier Hotel, which was literally on the corner a few steps from this one and, for some reason, went by the same name. She appeared to understand that we wanted to book for later in the month—we’d test the extent of her understanding later.

Col and I went to look at Glacier Deux. It seemed in a slightly worse state of repair and the entrance looked positively unwelcoming; a mess of electrical wires at the top of some very dilapidated stairs. The room, however, seemed better than the one last night, so we booked in then bought eight bottles of water and walked back to the other hotel with them, dodging hawkers and street sellers as we went. Lots of cigarette vendors and fruit and bread sellers were around that day. We eventually found the office we were looking for; and discovered that we had in fact been there yesterday in the rain but hadn’t seen the dusty, faded, half-hidden sign. It was situated down a narrow lane and up some steep stone steps. We found three women in an office that remained dark despite the large circular windows. They were sitting behind their computers but didn’t look too busy.

They had a letter addressed to us which, when opened, revealed several plane tickets and vouchers. We told them that we would return later and walked back to Glacier number one to pack. Col and I carried most of the luggage ourselves, which took several trips. Each time we tried to find someone at reception to pay for the phone calls we had made earlier, no-one was at the desk, or else a different girl tried to help but didn’t understand what we wanted to pay for. A short while later, there was a slight knock at the door and a petite girl with dark, shoulder length hair and a timid smile entered and introduced herself as Noro—Harinambinina. She spoke haltingly but clearly and seemed to understand us well enough. It seemed the office where she worked was a little distance away from the main SETAM office, but as she had been expecting us, she discovered we were there earlier. Col asked her to organise us a taxi for tomorrow while I set about making her a cup of tea with my trusty well travelled heating element, bottled water, and a tin cup.

This morning we found a taxi quickly enough to take us to the museum—or more correctly, the Parc Botanique et Zoologique de Tsimbazaza.

Tsiry the driver (pronounced ‘Steer’) agreed to wait for us. We didn’t really have much time, but with the unsolicited help of a knowledgeable local guide who spoke reasonable English, we managed to see some exhibits of rare birds and chameleons, then went into the Musée de l'Académie Malgache, which housed natural and cultural exhibits. The most interesting of these had to be the extinct elephant bird, with an intact, if somewhat cracked, egg nestled between its large, horny toes.

There were also a few dinosaur remains—mostly of lemur-type creatures, an Indri, the strange furry creature called the Aye-aye and a tenrec. So many fascinating creatures—if we got to see even one of them in the wild, I’d be happy. We trooped out of the dusty, sterile gloom of the museum into the remains of the day’s sunshine, and the guide took us to a few burial exhibits which were on-site. We were impressed with our guide, who knew his stuff and the Latin names of all the creatures he had shown us.

As we parted company near the entrance gate, he asked us what we had read in the latest Lonely Planet guide about Madagascar and specifically the park. Not having read it, we ad-libbed enough to satisfy him and bid him farewell. Tsiry was still waiting and drove us back through heavy traffic to the hotel. All vehicles seemed to be going one way, and that was unfortunately our way. With the added frisson of disorganised pedestrians coming out from all corners, it made for a nerve wracking drive. We arranged to meet Tsiry when we got back to ‘Tana.

Our hotel was already a bustling metropolis of people when we got back; they were lounging, waiting, selling, and generally doing a good impression of a crowd scene.

I thought I may have a tummy bug; Malagasy malaise or Tana tummy, perhaps. I drank most of our bottled water and went with the boys to petit déjeuner. I knew we were early because we caught one of the waitresses changing into her uniform. We pretended we hadn’t just seen her in her bra and panties when she brought us the menu and sat outside on the verandah near the restaurant. My French was sorely tested again (Col always told me what he wanted and waited expectantly for me to translate when served). ‘We’ managed to get ourselves deux thés et un verre de Coke, pain avec beurre et confiture, et deux croissants. Tim, as the ‘supplement’, didn’t get anything, which was okay as I was not eating, so he munched away happily on my croissant and slurped his Coke—a healthy combination.

Dina, our driver, was an affable chap who spoke some English—probably a lot better than I spoke French. As I wasn’t feeling too good, I found conversation taxing, especially as Dina punctuated all his answers with a long, drawn-out, ‘Aaaaaahhhh…’ before he found the most suitable word in his admittedly limited vocabulary. I thought at first it was because he was searching for the English word, until I heard him speak Malagasy and it was much worse (a greater vocabulary and therefore a lot more choices I suppose).

I noticed the devastating effects of deforestation as Dina drove—just pockets of forest were left once we were outside Tana, but it became more ‘foresty’ the further away we moved towards Andasibe, which was about three hours away from Antananarivo.

There were many of the same style of buildings we had seen in Nepal—small, haphazardly built wooden and palm leaf houses with small alcoves as windows. Many houses doubled as little shops selling anything from the ubiquitous French loaf to tin pots. There were chickens and cockerels everywhere, though most looked in need of a bit more meat under their feathers. There was a different type of building which we found closer to the mountains; typically rectangular houses, often with steeply angled roofs, constructed using locally sourced materials like mud, wattle, woven mats, and thatch. The specific materials and construction techniques varied by region, with some areas utilising woven bamboo or overlapping wooden planks.

At the Mandraka butterfly farm run by a Frenchman, we paid a small entrance fee to a grey-haired old man inside a thatched-roofed room that had a few dusty T-shirts for sale and not much else.

We were shown a couple of well-thumbed, plastic-covered menus and asked to choose our déjeuner. We decided on zebu steak, pork chops and pommes frites (potato chips)—meat well-done, s’il vous plaît, to lessen the chance of tapeworm, which was apparently quite prevalent in Madagascar. The important business of food taken care of, we were introduced to our guide who showed us the various cages and their contents which were housed nearby. The dwellings were a sort of meat-safe size, really, but held the most amazing things, like the Brookesia—the smallest chameleon, of which there were two types—to the largest chameleon; the Parson's, a big green chap who sat on Tim’s hand, eyes swivelling independently, while he adopted an expression of quiet resignation.

There were horned chameleons—red and yellow in colour. Our guide explained that one kind of Brookesia pretended to be a dead twig and lay rigid if it fell off a branch. Two types of Madagascan boa were on display; a greenish-brown tree boa (which just lived in trees but ate from the ground) and a ground boa—a beautifully patterned brown snake. The guide later showed us the beautiful moon, or comet moth with its long, trailing wings. The various stick insects that we were shown were absolutely amazing as well. Masters of disguise, they came as branches, twigs, and leaves and were hard to distinguish from the real thing.

Next in line were the various types of frogs. Small green ones, huge red ones, yellow ones, and mottled frogs—they came in a variety of colours and sizes, from thumb-sized to hand-sized. I absolutely loved the big, fat bright red tomato frog.

Our guide was very knowledgeable and also knew the Latin names as well as the common names, as had the chap in Tana. We were quite impressed with the Madagascan guides so far. This one was also very helpful when it came to photography, gently blowing on the little mantella frog in his cupped hand to get it to keep still long enough for us to get a good focus. He placed things against a background that was conducive to a good photograph. We found it fascinating and were more than happy to pay a little extra for his expertise. He led us up a hill where our lunch was apparently awaiting us in a large, open-style house that had a magnificent view of the surrounding area. We washed our hands in a bathroom that led straight off the large wooden verandah, then entered the airy, sparsely furnished room where a small, round wooden table was laid with a beautiful embroidered tablecloth and napkins.

Dina shared a very enjoyable meal of well-cooked zebu and chips with us. The contented sound of munching was punctuated only by the occasional long, ‘Eerrrhhhh…’ that announced Dina was about to speak. After this pleasant repast, we took our leave, thanked our hosts, and left for Vakona Lodge, which was situated in Andasibe (named Perinet in colonial times).

The changing landscape led further into the coastal forest. Vakona was situated amid a tranquil forest setting; a collection of pagoda-style dining and entertainment rondavels with a surrounding pond, complete with wooden bridges, walkways, and lush tropical flowers. The rooms were dotted along the edge of the mountainside and were accessible by a number of steep steps. The view from the rooms was fantastic.

ANDASIBE

It started raining shortly after we arrived and everything was glistening with water drops. The lovely pink rhododendron and Madagascan orchids glowed in the fresh light. I took out the special forest-wear we had brought in preparation for our stay. This included leech protectors, head torches, and waterproof capes in lurid plastic colours. I laid these on our bed and we went down the stone steps to the main pagoda for some tea. The view was great; the tea, unfortunately, wasn’t. After dressing up in our special gear for the ‘big walk’ that day; hats, leech socks, jackets, and cameras then set off with Dina and Etienne, our guide, for the forest.

The first thing we saw was fairly close to the roadside—a couple of grey bamboo lemurs high up in the treetops. It was our first sighting of wild lemurs and even though the view was not very clear, it was still exciting. I took several photos of dark smudges against some trees (I reasoned that I could always edit them later).

Dina drove up a dusty roadside and parked right next to the forest. The four of us started our foray into the dripping forest canopy, climbing steadily up some steep pathways that were covered in vines and springy undergrowth. The trees were tall, thin, and sparse with a number of strangely patterned palms. There were many pretty forest plants and little flowers—with small orchid-type heads. I found an interesting little fungus—white and waxy, glistening in a ghostly fashion on a fallen log, and trampled past a bush the colour of a pale blueberry that screamed ‘poisonous’.

The forest trees were covered in lichen and hanging cobwebs of ‘old man's beard’. It was very damp so there was a constant dripping from the trees. I stopped halfway up with Col to catch our breath while Etienne scampered off to look for lemurs for us and we came across our first wild brown lemur sitting high above us.

As we climbed, I noticed the presence of small brown leeches on the bushes. Col and Etienne brushed a few off their arms, and Tim pouted, feeling left out—he also wanted to be sucked on by a leech he said. His wish was granted a short while later. He stopped suddenly and said he felt something on top of his head. I peered in the vicinity of his crown and found a fat, blood-filled leech and pulled quickly and it came off, leaving a small puddle of blood. Tim, thus satisfied, carried on up the forest path. Etienne was looking out for the rare, endemic Diademed sifaka for us. Suddenly, a haunting wail rent the still, humid forest air. It was an Indri. We quickened our pace and followed the direction of the sound. It was reaching a crescendo now—a truly eerie, whale-like, wonderful sound!

After panting and scrambling over the slippery slopes, we came across one Indri. It was sitting on a thin tree and presented its bottom to us. As we stood craning our necks and gazing in rapt attention, it turned its head; a remarkable small, black, almost dog-like face with round, woolly ears. Its body was black and white with no tail, unlike other lemurs.

It gradually moved on, and we returned to Dina via the bramble-twisted path.

Refreshed after a coffee, we collected our attire for a sortie de nuit—a night walk. I met the chap from the room next to ours; he only spoke French, so I tortured him with my petit peu de français. He joined us and a small group of Americans on the walk. We felt a trifle ridiculous with our huge head torches, but they turned out to be very useful. Our ‘supplement’, as Tim was referred to in most hotels, left his Game Boy long enough to join us—he also wanted to try out his head torch.

Our guide for the walk had a very good torch and as his light played over the surrounding foliage, he gave a grunt and motioned us over. It was a small, bright green chameleon sitting motionless on a light branch. Next, the guide shone his torch up into the canopy of trees, and the small, indignant face of a mouse lemur was revealed, looking very like a bushbaby; the African equivalent. It was a dark brown colour and had enormous yellow eyes and a longish tail. A flurry of whirring and clicking camera noises ensued.

After watching it for ten minutes we returned for supper. The American group had a rather irritating woman with them who was making herself heard. She was asking the rather bemused woman behind the desk in strident tones to change her double bed for a single one as she didn’t want to sleep with her husband. Col and I surmised that the feeling was probably mutual.

After brekky we bid farewell to Hotel Vakona and made our way to Hotel Fe’ony Ala,…not too far down the road as it turned out. Dina arrived in the car.

Feon’ ny Ala was also very pretty and a lot less pricy.

The accommodation consisted of A-frame huts along a stretch of path that ran along the Mandrare river. The dining area came off the carpark. There was also an open verandah overlooking the river down some stone steps. Tables and easy chairs invited one to sit and relax with a drink.

I was amused to note a faded notice behind the door of our room stating that the ‘Direction’ declared it a potential problem to use very hot water which could ‘burn you.’

Etienne, our night guide, met us at the hotel gate and we followed him a short distance to the park entrance and showed our respective visas. A large mottled chameleon greeted us at the front, sitting on a small bush. We oohed and aahed then Etienne showed us the tiny ‘brookesia nana’, the smallest chameleon in the world, it was as small as Tim’s thumb. A greenish-grey colour with a short thin tail. Etienne showed us his expertise in the forest by spotting three sleeping woolly lemurs (avahi) at the top of a huge palm tree. They were all cuddled up together. He continued to another spot deeper in the forest for another small chameleon, then pointed out a giraffe beetle with its red and black colouring. A long thin ‘neck’ gave it the name. The best for me was our first sighting in the wild of the Parsons chameleon sitting like a giant emerald on a branch, just off the path.

Parson’s could exhibit a wide range of colours, including turquoise and yellow, but mostly a light or dark green. It was the largest in the world and was the size of a large cat. I was so pleased to see one and I could say I’d seen the smallest and largest in the world. Another sleeping woolly lemur lay somewhat closer than the first three. It started to rain heavily so we donned our brightly coloured plastic ponchos; the colour of a pack of Smarties—Col had a blue one, Tim’s was red and mine a banana yellow. Although the forest floor was slippery with mud it wasn’t too hard going as the path was relatively flat. Back at the hotel Feony Ala we had hot showers in the somewhat cramped space of the A-frame.

It was still wet that morning with a soft rain falling as we awoke. The air was warm and damp and the nearby forest hung veiled in a misty curtain of light. I made some tea in tin mugs and we made our way in our smelly ‘three walks’ clothes to the restaurant for breakfast. As we had arrived late for our walk, we had to bolt the food down in five minutes, not a pleasant sensation and the taste of scrambled eggs and eggs ‘au beurre’ repeated as we walked towards the entrance of the Park to meet Etienne and continued along the same path as yesterday. First Etienne wanted to show us a leaf tailed gecko that he had spotted from his bicycle on his way here.

When we came to the spot I was amazed that he’d ever managed to spot it from his bicycle as it was by far the most camouflaged animal I’d ever seen; even when looking directly at it, it was hard to identify. They used their leaf-shaped tails and mottled bodies to mimic dead leaves or bark, helping them hide from predators.

Several groups of people were staring intently up at the trees and following their combined gaze,…I saw our first Indri from this side of the forest. I was instantly enamoured; they looked like giant pandas or teddy bears. Small heads rather doglike with tufted woolly ears on top of a large black and white body—their hands were monkey like. I was totally smitten and like the rest of the group stared transfixed at the small group of six or seven that were within a few feet of us. It was difficult to photograph them against the backdrop of grey morning light so after a while we set off again up the slopes and found a Madagascan Coucal. It was a species of cuckoo and sounded like our local (African Coucal) when calling.

A few Madagascan bee eaters flitted around the bushes, a female parsons chameleon with emerald colouring stared rigidly at us, brown lemurs clung from the trees and blue kingfishers, red-tailed Vangas, crested drongos and later a better sighting of the beautiful blue vanga flew around the lower branches of the forest.

At the nearby fish farm we came across many brown lemurs jumping noisily around the trees. Col lent Tim his old camera and he was hooked; he started snapping away merrily at all and sundry. He took several of the lemurs which came out rather well. Etienne led us to a sleeping tree boa, all curled up near the water, where it slithered away slowly as we approached. The snakes in Madagascar were mostly non poisonous with a few venomous varieties so I wasn’t as anxious as I normally was wandering around the thick forest.

The weather began to clear up which was pleasing for photography. We returned to the park entrance chatting in low excited whispers of our early morning experience. We were rather thirsty by now so I bought some drinks from the small room that served as a kiosk, although it was nothing more than a long low counter with a few dusty bottles on a single shelf. I discovered a drink called ‘Fresh’; a kind of beer shandy and purchased a few for us.

The woman behind the counter, on hearing that Tim was collecting bottle tops, disappeared behind the counter and emerged after a few seconds of scrabbling, with a small brown paper bag full of bottle tops. She presented them to Tim with a flourish and he was delighted.

Later we walked to the verandah overlooking the river and sat in the waning sun listening to the sounds of the forest. A pretty emerald green gecko sat blinking quietly near the waste bin outside our A-frame. Col decided he was hungry at around two. The waiter standing outside the restaurant informed us that we were too late for lunch so I booked a table for 5pm. We continued sitting with our drinks when the manager appeared and chatted to us. When he heard we hadn’t eaten lunch he insisted we order something. Which we did much to the confusion of the waiters who were expecting us at 5pm. In any case we ate well on zebu steaks with eggs, chips and rice. As we ate, the sun slipped in and out of the clouds.

Diary Entry

A busload of tourists had just arrived! We would be going on a night walk with Etinenne at 6pm. Col told him to keep OFF the path; should be interesting!

We donned our various head gear and set off tiptoeing somewhat quietly into the forest as it wasn’t strictly allowed to be wandering around after dark off the beaten path. Actually it was totally forbidden.

At first it was a bit scary walking up the slippery wet slope in the dark, getting tangled up in branches and tripping over roots but one soon got used to it. It was eerily quiet in the forest,—not a sound except for our laboured breathing and the crackle of dry leaves underfoot. We covered the same route as during the day and found some tree trunks with aye-aye scratch marks and stepped up the pace in anticipation. There was a fat tailed dwarf lemur in a tree,—the difference between them and the mouse lemur lay in the thickness of tail and their habitat.

After walking around for about an hour and a half, we passed the bridge and found a beautiful Madagascan leaf frog, one of the tiny yellow-green ones.

To add to my discomfort, there was a large petrol tanker on board with neither a driver nor restraint and it moved back and forwards with each wave.

AMPIJAROA

The heat was intense and the surroundings were dry and denuded of trees. The taxi brought us to the ‘Parc de Nasionale a Ampijarowa more by luck than design. Luckily, (as we hadn’t booked,) there were two rooms for us. We dropped off our suitcases and went in search of Guy the guide (pronounced the French way, ’Gee’. Eric was also here and joined us on a night walk through the dry deciduous forest. Along the flat paths we came across the Horned or Rhinoceros chameleon, a turtle and some blue vangas then suddenly a group of Coquerrelles Sifakas—beautiful rich mahogany, chocolate arms and white backs. They had large round green eyes and looked like giant white teddy bears.

The following morning we went looking for the Angonoka tortoise enclosure (also known as Ploughshare tortoise) which were the rarest in the world with unique, high-domed shells. The enclosure was managed by Jersey Wildlife (Gerald Durrell's zoo in the U.K.) which was established in 1995.

The Angonoka tortoise was endangered mainly because it was highly sought after in the international pet trade.

Col and I photographed some Sifakas in the camp area. It was fun watching their antics and the special side dance they performed on two legs. I noticed a man selling bananas, pickled mango, dried fish and small yams at the side of the road and bought some bananas. He wore an old blue cap and his wife who joined him from the bushes, wore a dress with three straw hats on her head.

The next morning we joined Guy for a forest walk and saw the Madagascan Fish eagle which was quite rare apparently.

He had brought along a tape recorder and pressed play and we collapsed with laughter. There was a faint sound of the rare Acity bird but it was almost completely drowned out by the sound of a car engine. Col said he didn’t know Acity’s could drive. The laugh was on us though because, not half an hour later, we came across the rare Acity, high up a tree but well visible. It was very exciting.

The road to ‘Tana was crisscrossed with bridges in all manner of size and description. We crossed over the many waterfalls of the Betsiboka River then stopped again at a horrible dry, dusty, humid village. I was bursting for the loo, a small smiling woman in a faded yellow dress took me to a dirty wooden door with more holes than a Swiss cheese, down a rat infested alley. I opened the creaky door with trepidation and peered into the gloom. I instantly covered my nose. Flies were swarming around the pit like drones. I noticed a plastic jug and a bucket of dirty brown water. I finished quickly and sprinted out feeling instantly remorseful they had to live with these conditions on a daily basis.

It became really cold. A huge blood red moon hovered in the sky casting pale crimson light over the landscape. The driver lent Tim and me a blanket to share. He entertained us with music from an old car tape and we listened to music whose genre ranged from the Ink Spots to Reggae and arrived at Hotel Glacier at one in the morning. The half asleep receptionist told us there wasn’t a room for us. He said, after consulting a tattered register, that we had booked for tomorrow night. Paul, the driver, saved the day (or night) again by taking us to a rather more expensive Hotel de la France. At least we had a room for what was left of the night.

TANA

I brought my powdered milk and tea bags into the five star hotel dining room much to Col’s amusement. One had to be careful with the water and it was essential I started the day with a good cuppa. Our ground floor room at hotel Glacier faced a very busy thoroughfare and we had more than a couple of Vaasa’s nodding a ‘bonjour’ at us. After a simple lunch we took a taxi to the Tzimbazaza zoo which was founded in 1925. It included a large lake, a botanical garden and of course, the zoo itself. Whilst we didn’t always enjoy zoos, it could be an opportunity to see some of the animals we wouldn’t otherwise see. Col bumped into an old guide from a previous visit and we must have overpaid him judging from the enthusiastic welcome we received.

By 5pm most visitors had left the park and we met up with Bruno, our ‘night guide’ whose services we had paid to see the Aye-aye. These interesting creatures were nocturnal so dusk was a good time to see them. The night descended rapidly and one minute we were following Bruno into some cages and next minute darkness had enveloped us and almost removed vision, so he switched on his hefty torch and shone it directly into a large cage and the first thing we saw were huge, round orange eyes, then gradually the entire body was revealed in the dim light like the Cheshire Cat. He was bigger than I had imagined, the size of a small dog with a huge bushy tail. I noticed the extra long middle finger and dark brown fur. Aye-aye’s were the world's largest nocturnal primate. This specimen darted around the cage making photography a bit tricky but we managed to get some nice footage of this magnificently weird creature. Back at Hotel Glacier, we found Harianbinana waiting for us so we invited her to join us for supper. In her honour we had a typical Malagasy meal of spinach and rice with Zebu and shredded manioc leaves. We hugged farewell as we would not be seeing her again.

À bientôt, Madagascar!
