# Chapter 43: BALI 2024 — 28th  July

And on the road you meet those who come before you.
 "A person often meets his destiny on the road he took to avoid it" — Jean de La Fontaine. 

Tim and I arrive on Air Asia from Singapore at 10pm. My first thought is how much cooler it feels here than in Singapore. As a resident, Tim goes through customs in ten minutes but I still have to work my way through passport control, visa and luggage collection.  We walk quite far with the suitcase to where Tim has parked his bike. I order a Gojek and wave Tim off for the long drive back to Pererenan. It takes an hour and a half to get to my accommodation and I’m surprised to see Tim there already, talking to two hotel staff members. It turns out they’d given my room to another couple and Tim is not happy about this as he’d chosen a nice room for me but he keeps his calm. Eventually I’m given another room at the back under the understanding that they ask the couple to move in the morning. It’s late and Tim still has to ride back to his place so I say goodbye and head tiredly to my room.

Tim arrives after 9am this morning to take me for coffee and I climb onto the back of his bike, with a quick,’Sorry’ to indicate, I’m sitting comfortably on the back and we may proceed. We ride over to ‘Openhouse’ where he is greeted warmly. It’s a very Bali scene. Lots of tanned youngsters on their laptops, wearing flip-flops and not much else. We climb up some cracked cement steps and find a table.
A friend of Tim’s called Jing comes to say hi. She’s a petite Asian girl with dark wavy hair and an intense personality. Tim takes me to see his house. It looks smaller than on the video he’d sent me but more homely. His bedroom is nicely furnished in green with bamboo and rattan furnishing. I meet Bella, his recently acquired dog. After a short rest, I set off for a walk to the beach. It takes a while as I wander past construction sites, rice paddies, and very narrow, potholed pathways. After confirming I am heading in the right direction, I finally glimpse a strip of blue. This is Lyma Beach accessed via uneven steps to the beach, but I remain at the top overlooking the bay. There are tables dotted around with bright yellow umbrellas and a small cane bar. I sit for a while watching locals flying kites on the beach below before heading back to my room. 

I set off for Front Café for my two coffees. On the way, I pass a couple of locals sitting at the side of the pavement, fishing out of water from the open drain.  Apparently there are fish in there although they are only sardine sized.
Tim takes us to the black sand beach for a walk before riding to Black Sand Brewery. It is different from the last time I was here during lockdown. Now it is so busy that we have to share a table with five noisy Russians even though they have extended the area. The vibe is festive and the music has people jiggling in their seats. My beer tastes like passionfruit and is tasty. Tim’s school friend, Jack, is in Bali, and he joins us. He has hooked up with a girl he met via Hinge from Australia. She brings a friend as well and she tells us they have literally just arrived off the plane. One of the girls has dark hair and very intense eyes with thick eyebrows that swoop down like birds of prey. The conversation at the other end of the table becomes quite heated with quite a bit of Russian swearing. One of the girls notices our alarm, leans over and says, unlike her friends, she is from Ukraine, which kind of explains a lot. I phone for a Gojek and hug everyone goodbye (I am getting rather fond of this intense hugging between the Gen Zs; I feel my dopamine levels rise with every hug). 
My Gojek driver hands me a helmet; I have to confess it is the first time I have worn one since getting here.
After depositing Bella back home, Tim and I head to a place called ‘What’s that Crab’; a seafood restaurant overlooking a huge rice paddy with beautiful sunset views. We are meeting Tim's friends from his Games evening. Huge paper bibs are placed over our heads and settle in front. Rianna is an investigative journalist, so conversation turns to the recent past and the difficulties for Chinese living in Jakarta. Some local politics chat enters the conversation. Our crab arrives and we all tuck in. Tim manages to eat every part of his crab including the shell. I enjoy the evening and the interesting discussions.  The sunset over the rice paddy sends streaks of red, yellow, and orange hues across the sky. Tim and I notice that Jack is wearing his bib back-to-front and we have some fun making allusions to King Crab and his magic cape. We find this really funny for some reason and cackle to ourselves.
This morning I walk back to Front, to meet Tim. I photograph some colourful drain covers and Balinese women dressed in traditional garb. They look lovely with their lacy tops and colourful skirts. They are walking to a shrine with daily offerings carefully balanced on their heads. I glance across the water to the other side of the beach, where the giant 75m-tall Buddha’s head in Uluwatu peers out of the mountain side like some cartoon mole. Garuda Wisnu Kencana (GWK) is apparently taller than the Statue of Liberty. 
Tim suggests I try an 'es dawet,’ which is a milky drink originating in Java made from rice flour and coconut milk and served with shaved ice. It is so deliciously refreshing that I order another one. 
I have to leave my room by 8 a.m. today as they are going to spray the rooms with anti-mosquito stuff. It is a nice, cool start to the day as I walk to Front cafe taking a different route which runs past pretty houses and green rice fields. The cooler weather also helps. Jing passes by on her bike. She is wearing the green Gojek jersey again. 
Danny is the young man who is leading the philosophy club tonight instead of Tim. Danny is from Holland and reminds the group that the is the topic is ‘the moral and ethical effects of war’. We are sitting at a large, round table, but as people arrive, we quickly decide it is not big enough. There’s a short Indian man with long hair; Carol, a French woman of fifty; a German girl with long blonde hair; a Serbian girl with red hair and a dour expression (perhaps she is nervous). Lastly, a tall man in his mid-forties called Carlos joins us. He is Afro-American and tells us he was in the marines. Danny opens the meeting and explains the topic for the evening. Another man, in his late forties, arrives. He is also American and introduces himself as Alec. He says he fought in the Afghan war. We’re all seated in a wide circle on cushions. Danny asks Alec what his feelings are about the effects of war and he tells us how he is suffering from PTSD following the Afghan war where he flew planes and was in charge of several other soldiers. He explains that in order to save 30 people, he has to make a split-second decision to kill six and that has haunted him to this day. It does sound extremely traumatic. He says he joined the army at 18. We discuss the question,’ is eighteen too young to be killing people and watching people die’ and the group is divided into two. A long discussion takes place with each of us giving our opinion (which is divided). 
Lima Restaurant sits at the top of the hillside where we’re watching kites flying against the sunset. Tim tells me that the local women are generally fed up with the amount of time their husbands spend flying kites. There does seem to be a large number of mostly male kite flyers. We have dua kelapa and share a plate of cheesy chips then ride to the Red Gunpowder restaurant for an Indian meal. The decor is all dark wood and red fittings with gold finishes.

AMED
I attach my helmet and we head towards Amed in the North East coast. At first, the road is really busy and he has to negotiate between trucks, lorries, and hundreds of other motorbikes. More than a few times we are sandwiched between two huge trucks, which is quite a hairy experience. For two hours I watch the scenery change from city to rural with rice paddies, huts, chickens, cows and roadside palm trees. As Tim rides high in the mountains on a dirt road along a small village, we find ourselves in the middle of a religious procession of sorts, which means we have to slow down. To get to the accommodation, we ride through a very narrow gap between two buildings into a square adjacent to the sea and wait until someone comes to let us in through a small, rusty gate. We leave the bike outside and head to a small, open  entrance before being led upstairs by a young local lad. He shows us two rooms. My room has a bed, a cupboard and a toilet which is set up on a small cement pedestal for some reason. The toilet is also the shower. Water lies inches deep on the floor and there’s algae growing on the wall from the damp. I take a deep breath and feel the need for tea so I’m about to venture downstairs when I bump into a Scottish woman from next door.
‘ Hi. We’ve just arrived, do you know where I can make a cuppa tea?’ I ask hopefully.
‘Hmm dunno really, I asked yesterday and they gave me some hot water in a frying pan.’ She flinches slightly at the memory.
There’s no one at reception so I go and have a look at the beach. It’s untidy with detritus of sea faring items, old boats,  wooden oars, rusting fish traps and nets, drift wood etc. 
I meet a local woman while walking down the street. She likes my dress and flowers. 
She says, ’Ping is my favourite colour too’. 
En route for a massage we pass locals buried up to their necks in black beach sand and later discover that it has some healing properties, according to local legend anyway. It comes from the nearby active volcano Mt Agung. The spa is well rated but also very busy. I think they may have overbooked us as there’s a lot of frantic rushing around and we are asked to sit in low armchairs for half an hour before being led to another open area and given some rosella tea and a cold cloth. We hear them calling to each other in loud whispers and fancy they’re trying to work out how to give us a massage when they’re out of masseuses. We reckon they’ve asked someone to pull in their grandma for the job. Eventually we are led over to a room with long curtains on either side that don’t meet in the middle so I try and undress as quickly as possible. I’m given some of those impossibly tiny panties that look like a hair net which sits mid buttock. Tim tells me his barely covered his ‘family jewels’,  and we’re both laughing when our masseuses enter. I turn onto my stomach face down through the hole. She begins by yanking the netting down from my buttocks until both pale globes are entirely exposed to passers by and begins  massaging my back with well oiled hands. Unfortunately the room is very dusty as it appears to be open to the outdoors on one side. I can feel both nostrils block up till I’m breathing through my mouth. As the massage continues, my blocked nose starts to run and my open mouth dries up. I’m more than relieved when she asks me to turn round which I do red faced, open-mouthed and semi naked. Shades of my Korean massage experience all over again.
‘ I’m going to write a short book on massage experiences all over the world.’ I say to Tim as we slide outside on well oiled feet.
Aftwerwards we have supper at a local warung and I order a nasi goreng (Indonesian fried rice with a fried egg) which is very tasty. Tim has fried fish which arrives complete with head and tail which he tucks into with gusto. He pops one of the gelatinous eyeballs into his mouth much to my disgust. 
The air conditioner has two settings; ‘extremely cold’ and ‘off’.  So I spend the night alternating between freezing or boiling and I toss around miserably until morning.
After stumbling downstairs to the reception area, I ask the young girl behind the counter if she’ll make me tea and I give her one of my teabags. She nods and takes out a small frying pan, pours water into it and sets it on the stove. I stand non plussed for a few moments then turn saying,  ‘Right then’ and march back upstairs. Ten minutes later she comes up the stairs with a small mug of tea and a cup with milk. Luckily I sniff and discover the milk is totally off. It’s so off it practically shakes hands with me before I tip the congealed foul smelling glob into a bush.
When Tim wakes up we head off for ‘One way espresso ‘ in Amed town. After the obligatory coffees we decide to explore the area around Mt Agung as we have a clear view of the volcano which is not always the case. Tim takes the back roads and we find ourselves riding up some steep paths, past rural huts, forests and fields. The villagers seem friendly; waving and smiling at us as we ride past. Some look on in disbelief and we get the impression that not many ‘bule’ ride this way. After a few wrong turns we find ourselves at the top of a narrow road where a small temple is nestled among the trees. There are a few feral dogs lying around the crumbling steps and they come at me, snarling too close to my ankles for comfort. Another side road and takes us past chickens, dogs, people carrying straw baskets of spinach and onions and locals riding scooters before stopping at a small cafe overlooking the huge rice fields of Tista. We walk down some precariously balanced stone steps into a narrow section with a rocky overhang as a roof and a long, narrow wooden table that runs from one end to the other. Our seat overlooks the lush rice paddies with purple mountains in the distance. I order ginger tea and the local delicacy; fried banana, from a young waitress wearing a colourful cloth skirt. A huge pot of ginger tea arrives with large pieces of raw ginger and a plate of delicious fried banana. The owner comes over to chat to us. He asks where we are from and we ask him about this place. He says he opened just after Covid to supplement his rice produce. We continue our exploration of the countryside. The road narrows becoming very steep so Tim rides around the mountain till we come to Purwakerti. 
After a short rest in our respective rooms, we head out for the Blue Earth village. The view is beautiful. Yachts and fishing boats lie on a deep blue sea with a purple mountain backdrop. A smiling waiter leads us to a large low, round table with scattered cushions as seats. I find a beanbag and sink into it. My friends from home; Guido and Helene are in Bali on a diving holiday. Guido arrives ten minutes later. He says he’s glad they found parking. They have a hired car and he says parking has been really difficult so far. Helene appears and we hug. They tell us all about the great diving experiences so far. It sounds exhausting as they seem to have to get up really early and have at least three dives a day. Tim discusses the  muck diving thankfully, as I have no experience of it. Muck diving takes place over dark volcanic sand, silty river mouths, muddy bottoms, and fields of dead coral or marine debris.
While these environments look like barren underwater deserts at first glance, they are actually bustling with some of the most evolutionarily bizarre, well-camouflaged, and rare creatures on the planet.

This morning I splash cold water on my face, step gingerly over two inches of water in the shower/toilet and climb up onto the cement dais for my wee. You have to dangle your legs over the cement square and elevate them over the algae covered floor. I won’t be sorry to see the back of this place to be honest.
After packing my rucksack and making tea with barely hot saucepan water, I wait for Tim to emerge which he does a short while later. Our plan is to just leave without saying anything as we have already paid in full, but we bump into the owner on the way out. He sees our bulging rucksacks and asks if we’re leaving. I tell him I don’t want a refund but we have to leave a day early. He nods and we close the small wooden gate behind us. I feel a little bad as they are nice people but I can’t spend another sleepless night. Our destination for today is Candidasa, a seaside town on the eastern cost of Bali that rests on the edge of a fresh water lagoon, called the Candi Dasa Lagoon. The ride is long and sore on our respective nether regions. Tim rides along the sea for a while before the road takes a turn inland and rises exponentially. It’s a difficult ride with all the traffic on the newly tarred road. Tim handles the bike and the extra weight well but I know it must be exhausting for him. A small troupe of monkeys scamper on the side of the roads at Bugbug. There’s a beautiful garden with different varieties of rose in our new accommodation.
Although not as busy as before the traffic is endless taking us two and a half hours to get to Jimbaran. Tim booked a place for himself called ‘The Pink Balangan for ‘fun adults!’, but decides to stay with his friends Mel and Chris instead. I’ll stay in his place but I’m not sure what kind of ‘fun adults’ they have in mind. Seeing as the most fun I’ll be having is an early night to watch a Korean series on my tablet, I’m not sure it’ll be the right place for me. 

The decor is barbie pink and the colour is repeated everywhere from the pool, plastic flamingoes to pink towels. The woman at reception welcomes us,

‘Selamat pagi dan selamat datang.’  and ties a pink bracelet around my wrist. She hands us both a cold cloth and some pink juice. I’m shown to my…pink room. There’s a huge outdoor shower room. Tim nods his approval but says he’s glad he’s not staying here and heads off to work. I make some tea and drink it while sitting on my pink chair outside. There are quite a few young couples as one would expect and I hope they won’t be too noisy tonight (miserable old barnacle that I am). I wash some clothes and take a shower. There’s no shower head per se, so the water gushes out with force and hits the sore muscles. Tim messages me to say he has met Elena in a place called Bambi. I arrange for a Gojek to pick me up. We seem to ride for ages and I’m hoping he’s not lost. I always feel so heavy on the back of the local’s small scooters. My core is working overtime as I try to stay on whilst going up very steep roads without having to hold onto driver.
Mel has arranged a table at ‘Mu’, a restaurant that sits on top of the cliff with outstanding views of the sea below. We came here briefly during lockdown but it is almost unrecognisable now, bristling with people. The sunset is absolutely amazing with huge swathes of gold and bright orange. I order a gnocchi olio and we catch up with each others doings since we last saw each other.

The receptionist gives me another pink ribbon to tie onto my rucksack when I check out of the pink maison. The traffic is really horrid as we approach Canggu forcing us to ride on the pavement at times. Tim negotiates the bike past huge trucks and in and out of the vast sea of bikes. My thighs are sore from clinging on. It takes us over an hour and a half to get to Canggu and Tim drops me back at Wiwin, where I discover another huge pole has fallen, this time in the bathroom. 
Tim picks me up at 5 p.m., and we head to an Indonesian place near the philosophy club. There are cute little bunnies hopping around; black, white, and multi-coloured. We sit outside in the verdant garden. I order something called ‘Timbungan’, which is chicken cooked in bamboo. When it’s ready, the waitress opens one end of the thick bamboo and slides the contents onto my plate. Chicken in a spicy sauce with rice and is very tasty with the Balinese spices.
This morning I inform the manager about the second pole that has fallen. I tell her that I want another room to avoid being injured by further falling debris. Later I note that the pole has been fixed, but the room has barely been cleaned; so I march off to find the manager again. She tells me that there is no other room here, but they will give me a room at their ‘new’ place down the road. I mooch around until it is time to go and just hope it is not going to be a 'frying pan into the fire' scenario.

Just before 2 p.m., they bring a car around and we pack my big suitcase and head to the new hotel- literally as it’s only been open a week. The new place is called ‘Noju’ and is situated down a very narrow back road, not far from Tim’s apartment. I am greeted by a nervous-looking manager who may have been told I am a difficult customer; I would say 'assertive' is a better word. In any case I make a point of being as friendly and accommodating as possible. I see from the room that it is sparsely furnished with a bed, a side table, and a small cupboard. My eye twitches slightly with the effort of trying to be nice and I enquire about the making of tea. He twists his hands nervously and says he will buy a kettle for me tomorrow, but in the meantime, I can use the kitchen facilities. As I am placing my bag on the floor, he tells me that I am moving again tomorrow into a ‘better’ room, so I make a note to keep things as simple as possible. The manager of their other villa offered me a free massage so I ask when this will take place. The manager looks stricken and says he will phone to confirm and let me know later.
I wake early and go in search of tea-making facilities with a tea bag in one hand and wearing a hopeful expression.
I am back in time from coffee to find a local woman waiting to give me my free massage. She is in her late forties and carries a large bag with her. She informs me that she will provide the massage on the bed so I clamber awkwardly onto the cloth she has laid out, having taken off my dress. The king-size bed makes it difficult for her to manoeuvre around me and I have to squash my face into a pillow instead of using the usual face opening with massage tables. It is difficult to relax and breathe when my nostrils are covered, so I breathe noisily through my mouth while she jumps around, smearing oil on me. I kind of feel the lack of music as it’s so quiet, save for the sound of exertion and my laboured breathing. All in all, not the best massage I have ever, but I thank her and wrap a towel around me as I open the door to let her out. Naturally, this is a good time for three people to stroll by.
I dress quickly and sit in the kitchen area overlooking the pool until my other room is ready. The new room is further down the path and is bigger but otherwise contains the same amount of furniture—which is to say, three items.
This morning I walk down to ‘Truth Coffee’ and have a deep conversation with Tim about…. truth. After our beverage, we go to fill up the bike at a Pertamina station and set off through the beautiful rice paddies to Pangkung. I have a dragon fruit juice after stopping at Joshua. We came here in 2021 and it’s not changed much. The beach at Kedungu is a dirty beige colour. A public toilet lies in the corner under the steps and  a young woman sits behind a small wooden desk. Above her head is a notice providing all the prices for various requirements: a ‘mandi’ (shower) is IR 4,000, and a ‘bab’ (defecation) will set you back IR 4,500. A pee will cost you IR 2,000. I am wondering how they would know if you defecated along with a wee and hope she doesn’t have to check! I am guessing toilet paper is an optional extra. Thankfully, neither of us need the loo, so we walk to the bike and head back to Pererenan.
Tim decides it’s already too late to go North as planned and suggests we ride to Jatiluwih instead. It’s a two-hour trip, but we travel through some lovely forests and past pretty bright green rice paddies. The sign for a chocolate factory piques our interest and we stop to take a look. It’s open which is surprising as it is both a Saturday and a public holiday. We’re greeted by a smiling local woman and led to a table outside. The smell of chocolate permeates the sultry air and I take in a few deep calorie-free breaths before ordering a chocolate drink each. The waitress has taken a shine to Tim, and leads him to the entrance of the chocolate shop. He speaks Indonesian to them and they are delighted. I beam proudly. They discuss the holiday today and he tells them that last year he was asked to hold the special bamboo pole during the procession, something that usually only certain local men do. I smile proudly again at my wonderful son. I think they also like the fact he is with his mum (as do I, naturally). We are given numerous tastings of chocolate. I buy some bags of chocolate beans as presents. As we head back to the bike, we pass a few chocolate pod trees so I stop to take a photo. I clamber on the back of the bike with a quick, ‘Sorry’ and we head further up into the mountains. The air feels cooler and much more pleasant the higher we go. We ride from the mountain a short way and then; there’s Jatiluwih lying below in beautiful, verdant splendour. Undulating rice paddies lie like a giant patchwork quilt. Jatiluwih lies 700m above sea level and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The ticket counter is located at the entrance to the rice terraces. We find a restaurant called Batu Karu Kopi that has views overlooking the rice fields below and order toasted cheese and a coffee each. After eating we head down to one of the paths that takes you into the rice fields. There are a few directions to choose from and we choose the path that takes us through the middle of the paddies. Other paths are longer and can take a few hours to navigate. We head back after a forty-minute walk and ride the crest of the mountain for a while, enjoying the cool breeze before heading slowly down. 
Later Tim and I arrange to meet again next year and have a long hug goodbye, as I am leaving early tomorrow morning. I finish packing and put all my electronics on to charge. I try to sleep, but knowing I have to be up early, I doze on and off…
… and wake up just after 3 a.m. groggily wash and dress. At 4 a.m., My Uber driver that I’d booked yesterday phones me. He tells me he needs cash. I’m astounded and tell him it’s my last day and I have no cash. He says I should cancel as he doesn’t have enough petrol for the drive to the airport. I can’t believe I am hearing this. I tell him I can’t cancel because it’s too early in the morning to arrange another car. I’m really angry with him but decide that the only thing to do is to have him stop at an ATM en route. He agrees to this (naturally, as he has already been paid by Uber). I find the gate locked so shout in the general vicinity of the manager’s office, but no one comes to my aid. Eventually I find a way to prise open the door and leave it open just in time to see my Uber driver arrive. He also sits in the car as I stagger out with all my luggage. I am monosyllabic with irritation by now. He takes me to an ATM and I ask how much petrol will take him to the airport. He shrugs and says he doesn't know. I grit my teeth withdraw IR 3,000 and thrust the notes at him. We drive in silence for the hour it takes to the airport.
