# Chapter 14: NEPAL — Sarnath and the Silver Ohms

Our new driver for the next leg of our travels was a man named Budhos Kumar, a fervent and proud Buddhist. He showed us a few beautiful Buddhist temples as we left the city limits for the hamlet of Sarnath—a major historic Buddhist centre located not far from Varanasi. While we stopped to look at another temple along the route, I purchased a tiny glass bottle of silver dust from a street trader at the side of the dusty road. The bottle top featured a raised stamp depicting the sacred Om symbol; the idea is to stamp the icon lightly over the centre of your forehead, leaving a beautiful silvery outline on the skin. Tim managed to get a good dusting of silver Om symbols all over his bare hands and clean clothes in a remarkably short space of time. 
It was while he was driving along the highway that Budhos casually mentioned in passing that he was currently suffering from malaria. When pressed for details, he admitted that his temperature had hit 40°C this morning. At our look of immediate concern—and if truth be told, a little irritation that he hadn't mentioned this earlier when he would have been a easier to replace—he hurriedly informed us that he had taken some Chloroquine, assuring us with a series of nods of his head that he was perfectly fine for driving us, ‘All the vay to Neypal!’ We were not convinced and told him to let us know the moment he started to feel really sick. The little villages we passed along the route were fascinating, featuring tiny stalls constructed of straw and mud selling jars of bright yellow buns, old tyres, bolts of cloth and dozens of traditional brass implements, utensils, and earthenware pots. In a typical rural village in this province, daily life moves to the rhythmic hum of tractor engines and the timeless cycle of the agricultural seasons. Clusters of brick houses, known locally as pucca homes are regularly painted in vibrant shades of blue, pink, or yellow. Narrow, paved lanes wind between the buildings, often leading to a central chaupal—the communal meeting space. Every home centres around an open courtyard where women sit on traditional rope beds to peel vegetables, while the elders watch the street. Depending on the season, the surrounding fields are dominated by the brilliant yellow of mustard flowers in winter or deep green stretches of sugarcane and wheat. A roadside shop sign proudly declared the name, ‘Stop, Look and Go’ and I rather hoped there was some pecuniary reward for the owner. We pulled over at a motel for a brief bathroom break. Colin and I cast worried glances at the perspiring Budhos as we alighted from the cramped, sweat-drenched confines of his cab; we seemed to be magnets on this trip for drivers suffering from some tropical disease. Tim entered the door clearly marked ‘Gents’ as he had been taught. A balding, portly man dressed in a sweat-stained vest stepped out of the room and motioned with one out flung arm that I was free to go inside. I politely declined his offer and found my own way around to the ladies' facilities. Having done the necessary I stepped out of the cubicle to find the same portly man and another standing at the ladies' hand basins. They noted my startled, horrified expression and leered in unison, displaying a row of cracked, betel-stained teeth in tandem like some kind of cheap horror show. I decided to forego the hand-washing and fled to the safety of the car, to lather my slightly trembling hands with waterless hand gel—an absolute, non-negotiable in any third-world country, by the way. 

The Border to Chitwan 

The driving was particularly atrocious. Huge, colourful Tata lorries came straight up behind, having aggressively overtaken vehicles at the very last second, leaving poor Budhos to perform several miracles involving a lot of fast wheel turning, frantic sideways nodding and swerving onto the dirt shoulder of the road. This he managed to do several times, spraying red murram dust high into the air and onto passing chickens. Bicycles pedalled in wobbly lines in front of the bumper, having entered the fray from all sides. Likewise, the ubiquitous auto-rickshaws, inebriated pedestrians, emaciated cattle and the occasional hairy black pig kept us on edge. Col’s knuckles remained locked in the white, clenched position on the dashboard. We stopped for lunch at a roadside motel to dine on egg fried rice and chicken fried rice respectively. Tim stuck to dry toast and Budhos presumably swallowed another fistful of his antimalarial pills. Later on, we had him stop outside a shop featuring a sign that read STD hanging crookedly above the door. This, as we had discovered on our previous journey, was simply the logo for the local telephone company rather than a public declaration of any nefarious disease. Col phoned ahead to confirm our upcoming Chitwan lodge booking. This administrative task complete, he shopped at a small stall nearby and emerged carrying a hairy brown coconut for me, some mandarin oranges, fresh mangoes and a packet of small, flower-shaped sweets, along with something that tasted like burnt molasses for Tim. Meantime, our pal Budhos was not looking at all kosher, and I was certain his malarial cycle was returning to his bloodstream with a vengeance. He was sweating profusely, coughing and looked far too ill to be steering a motor car. ‘He really shouldn’t be driving at all,’ I muttered to Col under my breath. But we wern’t allowed to drive ourselves as tourists in Nepal so the situation was difficult. We continued steadily towards the border crossing of Sonauli and Belahiya, where we encountered the usual frustrating customs. Everyone was irritable and exhausted. Budhos ambled off towards the office clutching our passports, vehicle papers and cash. While trying to climb out of the passenger side, I accidentally trapped my finger hard in the car door, completely removing the nail off my finger as a result. My eyes watered instantly with the agony and the finger rapidly swelled, turning every bit as blue as the expletives that flew out of my mouth. The entire process of filling out customs forms took over an hour and did absolutely nothing to lessen our irritability. The single longest namaste exchange took place on the Nepalese side of the border post. Once we finally cleared the gates into Nepal, there were numerous roadblocks required by the local police and traffic authorities to check the temporary licence plate that Budhos had attached earlier and for which he— read: we—had forked out quite a pile of rupee notes and several extra baksheesh cash payments. Col was asked by the guards to hand over thirty dollars each for our visas, but they claimed it was ‘free’ for Tim, provided Col greased their paws with an extra fifteen dollars first. Illogical bureaucracy and blatant corruption at its absolute best. 

The Avian Charm of Chital Lodge 

Having entered Nepal after our long and wearying journey through India, it quickly became apparent that the remainder of the route would be just as long and tiring; firstly because we were already exhausted and secondly because we simply couldn't find the lodge called Chital Lodge in Meghauli. Our guidebook informed us that Chital Lodge was the absolute ‘only place to stay’ if one wanted to see rare birds. Well, ‘one’ certainly did want to see birds, and so we pressed on into the receding twilight asking for directions along the way. I noticed the gradual change in the vegetation from the flat Indian plains as the terrain became significantly more mountainous. There was a truly lovely view of the Himalayas at one point when the clouds suddenly parted, revealing the peaks in all their snow-topped splendour. Whilst it was still light enough to see anything on the horizon we peered out eagerly at the passing houses; they seemed noticeably more solid than the dwellings in India, if I can use that term—well, I just did. They appeared far less precariously constructed, though the locals utilised a lot of mud, wattle, and bamboo rather than brick or stone and many of the structures were elevated on wooden stilts. They looked cosy from the car as small wood fires or gas lamps illuminated the interiors with a warm, orange glow. A typical Nepalese hillside village is a vibrant tapestry of ancient traditions and rugged natural beauty, perched on a steep slope or nestled in a fertile valley. Houses are traditionally constructed two or three stories high from stone or mud-brick with thick walls to provide insulation against the mountain cold and connected by narrow, winding stone paths. Roofs are traditionally made of slate or corrugated metal and every house featured a central courtyard where grain is spread out to dry, goats are tethered and neighbours gather to drink yak butter tea. Surrounding the residential clusters are breathtaking agricultural terraces carved directly into the mountainside, utilised to grow rice, millet, corn, or mustard. I asked Budhos the purpose of the huge, circular bales of straw-like material that were balanced precariously on tall wooden poles outside the houses. He explained that they were the residue of grass stems from the rice crops after threshing, which were left elevated as both shelter and winter food for the many water buffaloes belonging to the villagers. We finally arrived in the little village of Meghauli at around 10 p.m. and quickly discovered that it was Nepalese New Year’s Eve, meaning most of the local population were heavily in their cups having celebrated for most of the day. This proved highly unfortunate when attempting to ask for directions to Chital Lodge as it elicited a number of incoherent, slurred replies involving various incredibly complicated directions, some requiring all four points of the compass in the explanation. Budhos remained remarkably calm throughout these heavily booze-fumed exchanges—or else he was simply semi-delirious with malaria by now, it was hard to tell. In any event, we eventually arrived at the gates of Chital Lodge at around 11:30 p.m. I sat in the passenger seat peering out anxiously into the gloom at this long-awaited destination, waiting to hear our luck regarding room vacancies. The view from the car window was not reassuring. There seemed to be several giant birds' nests sitting atop a series of rickety-looking wooden steps. A low building to one side appeared to be the main house from which three shadowy figures now emerged into the candlelight. Col marched back to the car beaming his absolute delight; this was indeed the wilderness outpost he wanted and he announced happily that they had a bed available for each of us. I groped my way out of the car holding a limp, exhausted Tim and we were led up the wooden planks towards our room. I peered into the deep gloom by the dim, flickering light of a single candle, as there was no electricity at the site. The room was completely bare with huge, visible gaps in the wooden floorboards; the ceiling and walls were constructed entirely of woven bamboo, and two naked wooden beds and a single small window completed the ensemble. Bare wooden planks served as the floor, several of them featuring vital bits missin, through which I could dimly make out the dark ground below. The owner of the lodge and his wife were bustling about getting us a blanket and arranging with Col for an early morning bird walk with the owner—a well-known regional bird expert named Jib— scheduled for 5:30 a.m. They didn't notice my dismayed reaction to the accommodation. I was convinced that the many openings in the room would allow nocturnal access to all things small, furry and possessing sharp teeth. My bladder had by now become pressing and in a voice rapidly rising in desperation, I asked to be shown the toilet facilities. Clutching my tiny penlight torch tightly in my hand and with a sinking heart, I followed the owner’s wife down the wooden steps to the bottom of the garden towards another small timber hut. She pushed open the door and stood back; following the weak beam of my light, I cautiously peered inside. ‘Rustic’ was definitely pushing it as a description. It was dark, dank and smelled strongly of unmentionables. By the candlelight, I was shown the shower setup—a single brass tap in the wall from which water continuously trickled onto a bare concrete floor that was bright green with slippery slime. On the cold floor, my light beam picked out the distinct outline of two huge, black, croaking bullfrogs. Jib’s wife was just about to show me the toilet bowl through the secondary door, but I had seen enough. I squeaked my thanks, rushed past the startled woman and dashed down the dirt road, waving my penlight torch into the foliage. Finding a suitable clearing in the bush, I squatted gratefully. I felt terrible about my rudeness, but was exhausted and anxious. I walked back and told Col I was going to sleep in the car. I won’t give you his exact reply, but it involved a lot of walking, so I finally admitted defeat and climbed back up the steps to the bamboo room where Jib and his wife were still fussing over musty, damp blankets and candles. I thanked them profusely but all I wanted to do was lie down somewhere that didn't necessitate sharing my mattress with a variety of local wildlife. We insisted that Budhos take over the second room so he could rest his fever. Tim and I shared one narrow bed and Col took the other. I huddled miserably under the damp blanket still fully clothed, clutching my penlight tightly in case I should have to identify anything untoward in the dark night. Eventually, I fell into an uneasy sleep and dreamed of flying insects, giant rats and enormous, croaking wet bullfrogs. 

A Sociable Weaver's Nest 

I heard Col getting up for his walk at 5:30 a.m., and lay stiffly in bed staring up at the thatched ceiling, listening intently to the rustling sounds emanating from the bamboo walls and floorboards. Suddenly, something small flew directly into the room from an opening above and I let out a muffled squeak— it was only a harmless sparrow. Several more began flying around the rafters and I had the sensation of sleeping inside a giant bird’s nest. The morning air was redolent with the rich sounds of wild birdsong especially the infernal quail cuckoo which sounded similar to the Indian brain fever bird, calling out its repetitive cadence: da-da-da-dah (co-pper-kettle). I got up feeling strangely better in the clear light of day and somewhat shamefaced about my entitled, dramatic behaviour the previous night. Tim woke up shortly afterwards and I took him to the bathroom hut. There was no sign of the giant bullfrogs this time and the facilities were in fact spotlessly clean, if a bit damp. Tim was fascinated with the concept of the long-drop toilet but flatly declined to use it for fear of falling in. We washed our hands and faces at the dripping brass tap. Much refreshed, we left the damp hut and sat outside in the early, weak sunlight at a wooden bench and table. A wide river curved its way gracefully in the distance below our vantage point where Col and Jib had left for their bird walk. A number of large water buffaloes were wading lazily in the shallows. The mountain air felt beautifully fresh and crisp and I took a few appreciative deep breaths. Tim extracted his Game Boy from his pocket and started a game while I sat catching up with my diary. The lady of the house kindly offered me some tea and I was over-effusive in my thanks to try and make up for my earlier ill manners. I did however, explicitly ask for black tea—not wanting to risk any Nepalese version of Delhi belly. It arrived in a tall glass, to which I added my own powdered milk mixture. So, much restored in humour if not in sleep, I was able to enjoy a long chat with one of the residents—a young university student studying physics named Basu-dev. He explained that Jib was his uncle and that the lodge had been operating since 1989. His family lived just across the river. We discussed a few arbitrary academic topics until he was called away to help with chores. I amused myself by reading the many entries in the guest visitors' book and noted that almost without exception, all the international travellers enthused about the beauty of the place and the warmth of the owner, Jib Narayan Bhusal. His brother (Basu-dev’s father) assisted in the running of the operation. I felt a lot better about our location although it certainly hadn't lost its très rustic charm overnight. If anything, in the broad light of day our room appeared even more nidiform; with swallows flying casually in and out of the eaves. The entire setup was not unlike a giant sociable weaver's nest. I passed the next few moments idly imagining the Bhusal family in various bird forms and had just decided that Jib’s wife possessed the demeanour of an orange-bellied green pigeon when Col finally returned from his early morning walk with Jib. He was in high spirits, having logged quite few rare birds including the coveted twitcher sighting of the Bengal florican, as well as minivets and the lesser adjutant stork, among others. He had also spotted a wild hog deer in the grass. I watched the bird life while sitting under the thatch, observing the village children swimming in the river alongside the massive water buffaloes. Heavy rice threshing begins after the summer monsoon but traditional ox wagons were already out in the fields to collect the early rice harvest. Later on some local schoolboys arrived at the benches; they were Ram Prasad, the younger brother of Basu-dev and a classmate. They smiled shyly at us, whispering ‘Namaste’ and ‘Good day.’ Tim showed them his Game Boy and collection of Pokémon cards and the ice was broken. They spent the afternoon happily exchanging postal addresses, sketches and old Archie comics. 

Heading into Tiger Tops 

The Chitwan guards from the famous Tiger Tops camp arrived around lunchtime; Tiger Tops was to be our next destination. We said a temporary farewell to Budhos, warning him to continue taking his antimalarial medication and get some proper rest, then climbed onto the back of the National Park jeep and set off for the Tiger Tops tented camp nestled in the Surung Valley. Although the camp was a mere eight kilometres away through the bush, the drive took us nearly an hour due to the terrain. We pulled up at the riverbank and offloaded our heavy luggage onto a low wooden boat that was bobbing in the water awaiting our arrival. We were rowed across to the far bank where the process continued in reverse and luggage and ourselves were piled into a second waiting jeep. On finally arriving at the tented camp we were greeted by a smiling staff member holding out ice-cold face towels—which were absolutely brilliant in the heat—and a glass of cold orange juice which more than made up for the slight inconvenience of the hot journey. I met Jib’s father who greeted me with a hearty,‘Namaste’ and a clasp of his hands in front of his chest as if in prayer. His English was non-existent so we exchanged pleasantries by means of several mutual nods accompanied by warm smiles—the universal language of travel. Our hostess was a blonde German woman in her mid-thirties, dressed in the standard khaki shirt and skirt typical of national park rangers around the world. She gave our group the general layout of the perimeter and cautioned us about straying too far off the dirt paths at night. Tim became quite animated at the sight of his luxury camp bed and the canvas carpet. After enjoying tea on the small wooden deck overlooking the park, we set off on foot towards the elephants to look for the famous Indian armoured rhinos— so called because their thick, folded skin looks exactly like a heavy suit of medieval plate armour. It was a two-hour ride through the tall, aptly named elephant grass, which was over eight metres high in some sections of the floodplains. After a short walk down the wooden observation deck we stepped directly onto the elephant's back and set off with that now-familiar swaying rhythm towards the river channels where the rhinos were known to graze. Our guide walked on foot beside us and the mahout sat in front, positioned just behind the animal's bi-domed head so he could perform the traditional toe-steering behind the ears, while the three of us perched behind on the wooden howdah rail. Our elephant was a medium-sized female with a remarkably cute, hairy head. We trundled happily through the grass and navigated swampy places where only an elephant could go as the earth was wet and muddy in places. We saw barking deer, chital and a lone hog deer grazing in the clearings as we swayed past. Later while ambling through the thick grass, we surprised a massive grazing rhino. He was none too pleased with our sudden presence in his feeding zone and gave an ungainly mock charge, snorting and blowing his anger into the air, which sent our elephant into a trumpeting fury at the sheer audacity. Much frantic photo-snapping followed this encounter before coming across a group of lesser adjutant storks. The sky suddenly darkened and it began to thunder; forks of lightning streaked the mountain sky as we headed back to camp, making for a highly atmospheric if slightly anxious, return to the clearing. After disembarking at the deck, I thanked our head-scarfed mahout and headed to the main deck for supper. That night, I heard incredible, wild sounds echoing outside our canvas tent walls, constant rustling, creaking branches and an eerie, low groaning sound. 

Sloth Bears and the High Peaks

Despite the comfort of the luxury tent, I didn’t sleep well due to the wilderness noises described above so kept waking and drifting back to sleep until around 3 a.m. when the birds began to call the morning in. Col left early to go for a bird walk with a guide named Shukram this morning, while Tim and I embarked on a second elephant ride through the forest. It was beautifully refreshing in the crisp, early morning air and we spotted barking deer, hog deer, two massive armoured rhinos, a woodpecker, and a troop of langur monkeys in the canopy. Tim had brought his Game Boy along on the ride. I gently admonished him for not appreciating the spectacular surrounding sights and sounds of nature so he obligingly glanced around the forest with exaggerated deliberation before returning his eyes to the screen. Suddenly he perked up and announced happily, ‘When we get back to camp, I’m going to get Bulbasaur evolved into Ivysaur!’ I had to laugh, remembering what it’s like to be an eight-year-old on a family holiday. After climbing onto another jeep already loaded with our eleven pieces of luggage, we drove off towards the main Tiger Tops Jungle Lodge, or TTTL as it is also called. This was a brief twenty-minute drive through the grassy bush. As the name implies, it’s Nepal’s answer to Kenya's famous Treetops; a large, beautiful wooden building elevated on tall wooden stilts. The grand surroundings were dominated by deep forest, lush gardens and vibrant yellow mustard fields. We enjoyed lunch inside the golghar—a local term meaning a circular, thatch-roofed dining area. It featured a huge central space designed for sitting around an open fire pit, surrounded by dining tables. The interior was decorated with large oil paintings, one in particular depicting a roaring tiger that covered a good portion of the timber wall. Sain, our beaming lodge hostess explained the layout of the property to the new arrivals. She mentioned that many global celebrities had visited Tiger Tops over the decades; the photographic evidence hanging on the wall behind the bar attested to their enjoyment. Some of them returned season after season, Sain confided. Everyone in the room peered around expectantly as though Richard Gere was going to suddenly pop up from behind a potted palm and say, ‘Hi!’ 
A formal game drive followed the briefing at around 3:30 p.m. We shared our truck with two German couples and an Englishman travelling with his young son, so it was a bit of a squash with the driver and guide on board as well. The Germans immediately moaned at Col for a) standing up in his seat while the vehicle was in motion and b) for asking the driver to switch off the loud engine while he was attempting to photograph a rhino in the bush. I was annoyed with their attitude and asked the guide if they hadn't dealt with many professional photographers before. ‘It’s essential to minimise engine shake, which will completely spoil a fast film!’ I announced firmly. Naturally no one took any notice of my rant. We drove on while I seethe a bit on Col’s behalf. We didn't see much wildlife at first, aside from an Indian roller perched on an overhanging branch with a large scorpion clamped firmly in its beak. I was beginning to feel despondent at the lack of game when Col, who’d remained standing resolutely, suddenly shouted, ‘Sloth bear!’ Everyone turned in their seats and followed the line of his pointing finger and sure enough, a shaggy black bear shape was foraging in the tall grass about twenty-five metres away from the track. The driver stopped the truck but stubbornly refused to turn off the engine. The bear was walking along the edge of the forest adjacent to our vehicle, although still a bit of a distance off. We watched it move slowly towards the dirt road at an angle. I was desperately trying to capture it on my camera, but kept getting the guide’s blasted red baseball cap or the German couple’s heads blocking my frame, so completely missed what would have been a brilliant piece of footage when the bear suddenly stood straight up on its hind legs to look over at our vehicle. As we drove on, I said loudly to Col, ‘Thanks for spotting that, Col. We would never have seen it if you hadn’t!’ Hopefully, the point was taken by the moaning couple, but couldn't have cared less if it wasn't. I felt lucky to have seen the bear. Towards the end of the drive, we came across a riveting, panoramic view of the Annapurna range of Himalayas—all snow-capped, jagged and gleaming like spun sugar in the afternoon sunlight. The following day we secured an elephant to ourselves—a big one by Indian standards—and enjoyed a pleasant but uneventful ride through the dense yellow grass. It waded up to its grey, leathery thighs in a deep section of the river further along the channel,  before climbing a fairly steep verge to the grassy floodplains above where we found a rhino and a few hog deer contentedly nibbling at the fresh grass. 

Chakka Jams and the Long Detour 

The TTTL staff came to collect our family for the return trip to the riverbank. We piled our countless pieces of luggage onto the jeep and said goodbye to Sam and Sain. At the river's edge we crossed the water by dugout canoe to reach the far bank and our waiting jeep. At the wilderness airstrip we were entertained by a small group of Nepalese musicians who played a traditional type of stringed banjo. From there we were driven to meet our driver; a man named Chin who was to take us on the five-hour journey to Daman in a microbus. We set off for about an hour in the relative luxury of the air-conditioned bus before pulling over for some exorbitantly priced Cokes and a quick toilet break. We drove for another hour or so before noticing that the mountain road ahead was blocked by a massive wall of vehicles—huge lorries, cars and crowded buses. After fifteen minutes of inactivity we asked a passing pedestrian what was going on. It appeared that the previous day, two drunken men had been knocked off their motorbikes by a lorry. One of them had tragically died of his injuries so now angry family members had completely blocked the public highway with huge timber logs and flatly refused to let a single vehicle pass until the government authorities formally compensated them for the death. The queue of stranded vehicles grew steadily longer on the pass and we could see we were not going to be moving anywhere soon. So we opened our packed lunch boxes and prepared to wait for the highway police to arrive and sort out the dispute while nibbling on our cheese sandwiches and hard-boiled eggs. The authorities eventually arrived but news filtered back to us was not good; rocks were pelted at the officers, so we would likely have to wait it out until at least 4 p.m. (it was currently around 1:15 p.m.). This localised practice of holding up highway traffic is known as a ‘chakka jam’—a common form of political blockade in Nepal we later came to hear. Meanwhile our driver struck up a conversation with a passer-by who mentioned there was a small mountain village not too far back, down a secondary dirt track and we unanimously agreed to throw the bus into reverse, and drive back along the grassy verge to the turn-off to take this significantly longer but hopefully unimpeded route around the blockade. I was glad to get moving and have the air-conditioning whirring back on. It was actually a fair distance back down the pass before we located the turn-off, then spent hours driving the dusty road through beautiful rural pastures and tiny mountain villages dotted with terraced rice paddies and wading water buffaloes. It was lovely and Col and I felt we’d have completely missed seeing the true heart of rural Nepal if we hadn't been forced to take this detour. I took photographs of the dark-haired Nepalese girls as they walked along the roadside balancing massive bundles of firewood on their backs. 

The Everest Panorama 

Eventually our microbus made it back onto the main highway and we began climbing the steep mountain passes once more. The mud-and-wattle houses became fewer and further between, dotted along the high ridges. We reached an elevation of over 8,000 feet when we finally arrived at the Everest Panorama Resort. Once again we needed to leave the vehicle and manually lug all eleven pieces of luggage up a long walking pathway and a series of steep stone steps to meet an awaiting welcoming party at the main entrance. A pretty Nepali girl greeted us with a warm ‘Namaste’ and presented each of us with a fresh sprig of wild mountain flowers. I wheezed back an answering ‘Namaste’. We were led into the narrow reception area where Col signed the resort register and acquired room keys. Having signed in, we went to the dining area for a cup of tea. The room featured a sunken seating area with heavy cushions surrounding a low, highly polished timber table. I gazed out of the large windows at the spectacular view of the surrounding valley with blue mountain ridges rolling into the distance. The main mountain range of Everest was as yet, completely cloaked in thick smog. It was intensely cold—certainly much colder than anything we had experienced to date. We slurped our hot tea and cocoa then the porters escorted us down to our room down quite a few flights of long stone steps. I quickly realised these steps would have to be climbed in reverse when it came time for supper; the thought was not a happy one, as the high altitude had already rendered my breathing laboured. Later Tim organised three hot water bottles for us which steamed the sheets and ourselves. We’d asked the manager for the best time to view the Everest range the following morning and were informed that 5:15 a.m. was the golden hour. They promised to knock on our door to wake us. 

Hyperventilation on the Ridge 

We were therefore surprised and more than a little annoyed to be woken at 5:25 a.m. so by the time we’d got our stiff bodies out of hibernation mode and dressed, it was nearer 5:35 a.m. Of course it was now too late to see anything clearly; the Everest summit was clouded over for the day, although the Annapurna range remained visible, though not quite as spectacular as the view we’d enjoyed in Chitwan. Chin arrived with the microbus shortly afterwards and took us to another hotel nearby called the Daman Mountain Resort and Everest View Hotel. They featured a high observation tower on the roof where we hoped the telescope would reveal Everest in all her glory. It didn't but we were lucky to catch the faint outline of the massif as it’s frequently shrouded in a heavy blanket of mist. Later we stopped to visit a beautiful Buddhist temple situated about a thirty-minute walk down the mountain ridge and it was during this trek that I began to feel the ill effects of high altitude. We followed Chin down a winding, narrow footpath and as we arrived at the shrine there were long strings of colourful prayer flags flapping in the mountain breeze. I climbed up to the Buddhist monks at the top to an offer of a cup of warm yak butter tea which I had to refuse. After half an hour I decided to start my walk back to give myself a decent head start but managed to lose my way twice in the trees and this disorientation somehow increased my panic. My breathing became incredibly laboured and I felt nauseous and dizzy. After continuing up the incline I began hyperventilating and was forced to sit flat on the stone steps, blowing frantically into my cupped hands feeling really silly. ‘It’s not as if the climb is even steep!’ I thought irritably. The others caught up with me, looking surprised at both my lack of progress and loud impression of a steam engine. After another five minutes of resting I started a slow, deliberate walk back to the summit with Col and Tim peering at me with concern every once in a while. I made it back to the bus and sat inside for a while until my pulse slowed. I felt much better after that. 

Into the Capital Chaos 

We descended from the mountains into the Kathmandu Valley, a drive that took us three and a half hours. Kathmandu is a city where ancient spirituality and modern chaos exist in a constant, vibrant, noisy collision. Situated in a bowl-shaped valley at an elevation of roughly 1,400 metres, the capital of Nepal serves as the primary historic gateway to the grand Himalayas. We arrived at the Tibet Guest House situated in the middle of bustling Kathmandu, late afternoon. I stayed behind to chat with Chin while Col haggled at the counter for the cheapest room available; we intended to use this hotel as our primary base for the next five days of exploration. Tim and I climbed up to the roof deck; we could see across the entire sprawling expanse of Kathmandu. The air in the capital was a thick tapestry of intense smells and sounds. Fragrant scents of burning incense and fresh marigolds from street-side shrines mingled heavily with the exhaust fumes of swarming motorbikes and the savoury aroma of steaming momos, (steamed fried dumplings in soup) drifting from local cafes. There was a rhythmic blend of chiming temple bells, the low hum of Buddhist chanting, and the incessant, deafening honking of taxi horns navigating the narrow, winding alleys. In the historic core of the city the streets were a dense maze of ancient architecture, while the tourist hub of Thamel was a neon-lit labyrinth of trekking gear shops, rooftop cafes and bookshops. Later that evening we took a walk along the busy, narrow streets surrounding our hotel. Cars, motorbikes, rickshaws, and bicycles all honking and hooting their way along the narrow lanes. It was incredibly noisy and Tim looked a bit intimidated by it all; he detests dense crowds and loud noises and we were experiencing plenty of both. After stepping into a side shop he had a wonderful time trying on traditional Nepalese woollen hats. 

Fire and Prayer Wheels 

At around 3 a.m. in the morning, we were suddenly awakened by a huge commotion echoing in the streets outside our window—shouting and screaming voices. Col peered out of the window and announced that the building immediately adjacent to our hotel was on fire! I ran to the window to have a look and saw massive sheets of flame licking the night sky. Just then, an almighty explosion erupted; the entire building lit up like a bright orange balloon. We panicked because the flames were so close, I was certain our hotel building would be next. I woke Tim and we dressed hurriedly in the dark, threw our belongings loosely into our suitcases and rushed outside into the street to assess the damage. There were hundreds of local people milling around everywhere in the smoke. It looked bad at first but when the fire engine finally arrived on the scene, the firemen got to the main flames immediately. Soon, a huge, billowing grey ball of smoke rose into the leaden sky. We remained on the pavement until the authorities gave the all-clear and the fire was out before returning to our room to doze uneasily until about 6 a.m. 
Later that morning we took a taxi to visit the Swayambhunath Temple positioned high on the hill. The site featured a colossal stupa depicting the face of Buddha with those massive, slightly slanted eyes that you see everywhere in Kathmandu. The eyes are artistically depicted as half-closed or half-open to symbolise a perfect spiritual balance between the inner world of reflection and the outer world of reality. The temple grounds were teeming with macaque monkeys, considered sacred and revered here so remain safe from harm. Colourful prayer flags and local souvenir stalls nestled beside little stone alcoves containing fresh flower buds. We came across a huge, long row of copper prayer wheels and took turns flicking them with our hands to send prayers to the various deities. I took some photos before entering the main temple chamber where a huge, gold statue of Buddha, as big as the entire doorway, resided. Dozens of flickering butter candles lay at his splayed gold feet. Tim carefully lit two butter candles and made his wish. I stepped into a dark stone corridor to follow the beautiful sounds of low chanting and slow drum beats. The music emanated from a side room, packed with orange-clothed, shaven-headed Buddhist monks kneeling before a low table, either murmuring a chant or playing a traditional instrument. It was incredibly atmospheric, with the strong, aromatic smell of incense and the low, drone-like voices. I videoed the scene for a few minutes before I noticed a paper sign stuck to the wall stating that photography was strictly forbidden. I quickly returned the video camera to its bag and went back to light my candle by the large Buddha. 

The Shoe Mender of Durbar Square 

Tim, Col, and I subsequently travelled by bicycle rickshaw to the famous Durbar Square, my bottom perched uncomfortably on the narrow rim of the wooden wheel frame. It actually helps to travel this way for photography (the mode of transport I mean, not my bottom), as you amble along the streets at a nice, steady pace. The square featured many old-world, Victorian-type décor and historic buildings which was quaint, even if the structures were derelict and ramshackle. narrow cobbled streets flanked by tall, narrow brick buildings with small intricately patterned timber windows from which people leaned over to survey the busy scene below. The street butchers were a sight to behold; anaemic-looking pieces of goat or ‘buff’—they never call it beef so as not to offend the Hindu population—alongside whole sheep heads and raw innards hung around the stalls, completely fly-spotted and rather unappetising. Tim looked thoroughly put off by the display but announced dryly that he was not prepared to become vegetarian. Durbar Square was another typically busy, bustling, hawking mass of humanity. As we waited at the kerbside to cross the road, my ankle was suddenly grabbed from below and I found myself hopping wildly on one foot. The foot-grabber turned out to be a local shoe mender sitting on the pavement with some simple tools of trade. He held my foot firmly in his calloused paw and announced authoritatively that my shoe needed mending. I hopped again to regain my balance, looked down and sure enough, the rubber sole was coming apart from the leather upper. In no time at all he had re-stitched the shoe before shoe and rupee notes exchanged hands. Thus properly shod I continued gazing at numerous shrines and temples; they all featured the traditional red and yellow ceremonial colouring, fresh flowers and melting wax in small stone alcoves. A little local tyke with round brown eyes began following us around; he was seven or eight years old. He challenged me to name any country in the world and when I somewhat uncertainly answered, 
‘Italy,’ he instantly shot back with, ‘Rome!’ ‘Ask me another!’ he squealed excitedly. ‘Denmark,’ I said, uncertain of where this was going. ‘Ah, Copenhagen!’ he answered triumphantly. The boy could name every single capital city in the world and even managed to proudly shout, ‘Pretoria!’ when I thought I’d stymied him with ‘South Africa.’ I smiled broadly and handed him some coins for his cleverness.

Pulchowki and the Search for Butterflies 

I read in the Kathmandu Times that morning that the route we had taken today was relatively free of khates—the local term for beggars. I also discovered from reading the local rag that chakka jams were the preferred method for showing political displeasure in the country, as we had witnessed on our way in the previous day. Col returned to the room from his afternoon walk and announced sheepishly that he had slipped and slid down a steep mountain slope for about 500 metres. He claimed he had passed a rather interesting, rare butterfly en route sitting directly on a fresh animal turd, but had been completely unable to stop and have a good look at the specimen seeing as he was on a non-stop, trip on his bottom. Tim and I exchanged amused glances at his expense. Another snippet of interest was the tremendous amount of, how shall I put it, internal gas our family was emitting at regular intervals. Col informed me authoritatively that it had something to do with the high altitude and here’s me thinking it was the spicy lentils and eggplant we’d consumed two nights running. After breakfast the next morning we called a taxi to take us to the Pulchowki mountain. A tiny vehicle with a 800cc engine arrived at the kerb sounding like a motorised lawnmower. I had developed a painful, one-sided sore throat that I was keeping in check but wasn’t keen to miss the hike. We drove up a steep, rocky mountain track until almost at the summit when the car engine groaned, shuddered violently and simply couldn't make it up the final incline. We were forced to get out and walk the rest of the way to the top radio station, a hike that took us an hour. The driver told us he would wait for us at the turnaround; we hadn't yet paid him his fare, so were fairly confident he would remain there. Another beautiful temple sat at the top of the ridge featuring the usual reclining Buddha decorated with red and yellow flowers and melting candle grease. Col and Tim bickered over who had the right to use the butterfly net for the entire day but between them, they caught the following types of butterfly; Admirals (I caught one myself inside my sun hat), Blues, Fritillaries, Windmills, Swallowtails, Whites, and Oranges. While meandering slowly back down the 2,772-metre mountain of Pulchowki—a name meaning ‘mountain of flowers’—it started to rain heavily. Our patient driver had indeed waited for us at the bottom, and after purchasing Tim a packet of cheese-ball crisps from a roadside stall, we set off back towards Thamel and the Tibet Guest House. Had to bath in the dark, as the city's electricity grid had gone down with the rain. Afterwards, we walked over to the Everest Restaurant for a final platter of scrumptious, oversized, cholesterol-laden steaks and brownies, which ended our time in Kathmandu very nicely indeed. 

The Chaos of Kathmandu Airport 

At 3 p.m. our hotel transport vehicle arrived to take us to the airport. I gave the hotel receptionist, who sweetly called me ‘Mummy’, Col’s old spare tripod. We arrived far too early—the doors hadn't even opened yet so we whiled away the time chatting on the pavement with a British couple who were also heading to Delhi. It was a sparsely furnished waiting lounge once inside. I bought another wooden tea box at the small kiosk, as they made lovely gifts. Tim quickly made friends with an Indian boy who spoke with a distinct American accent; they both extracted their Game Boys and sat playing side by side. Then the announcement came that our flight was facing a two-hour delay—cue many groans from passengers. A long, wait later, I was forced to change another five-dollar note just to purchase some horrid tea and a few stale buns. We waited and waited, watching a massive Russian transport plane take off with a crowd of international backpackers on board. More than the stated two hours later the loudspeaker crackled to life to inform us that our flight had been cancelled—no explanation was offered, just the cold, hard facts. Everyone in the terminal murmured their intense dissatisfaction and the usual airport confusion followed, with around two hundred angry passengers milling around trying to find out who, what, why, and when, and especially where we were supposed to go now that we had already officially checked in. There was a chaotic scramble to reclaim all our luggage off the open tarmac and in the mid-air confusion, my favourite many-pocketed utility jacket which I had briefly removed, was stolen off a bench. We queued at the front counter again trying desperately to get the attention of the airline official. I was constantly prised away from my spot by shoving passengers, but I flatly refused to budge an inch, terrified we would lose our position in the queue. It wasn't easy because the staff kept changing the lines and closing down one side of the counter without warning. Col eventually managed to get on the phone and secure us a room for the night at the luxury Everest Hotel. The British couple from the pavement were also annoyed but the wife kept loudly blaming her husband for bringing her to Nepal in the first place. I was astounded at her misplaced anger; they had been staying in five-star, air-conditioned luxury accommodation for heaven's sake. I’d have proceeded with divorce papers if I were in his shoes ( which would never have worked; his were at least a size 13.) 

We finally left the airport terminal at around 10 p.m. on a very crowded shuttle bus after being stuck there for over eight hours. The hotel was pleasantly furnished and beautifully cool and we shoved our mountain of gear into the room, where eleven cases lay stacked in the narrow hallway for all and sundry to trip over. In any event we decided to leave for the airport early again the following morning to try and secure a place on the next plane; we reasoned that they surely couldn't fit all two hundred stranded passengers from yesterday on board a single standard aircraft. We were lucky to catch the first hotel shuttle bus but on arrival we encountered the usual chaos at the terminal with hundreds of people already milling around in confusion. It was frustrating that airline staff refused to provide passengers with information. 

The Battle of the Queue 

The counter for the Bombay flight finally opened and we immediately stood to ensure a good position; we were second in line, right behind the British couple. Having successfully claimed this prime real estate, we waited and waited and absolutely nothing happened for hours. At 2 p.m., they finally started checking in passengers and I held my place firmly with all eleven pieces of heavy luggage standing in the middle of the queue. A grumpy airport security guard ambled over and gruffly told Col to move, so Col shifted to another queue. The guard returned and ordered me to move out of the line. I looked him in the eye and said firmly: ‘Nope, I’m staying exactly where I am’. He insisted but still I refused to budge. I told him firmly I wasn't moving an inch and that if he wanted me out of the queue he would have to physically move me himself. This he actually attempted to do much to my absolute astonishment. He grabbed my bare arm and I immediately grabbed onto the metal security barrier pole with all my might. We caused quite a stir in the terminal, but there wasn't much else happening entertainment-wise. The official finally snapped that they were going to open up the counter for the Delhi flight at 3 p.m. and claimed this specific queue was going to Bombay, but I wasn't having any of it; as far as I was concerned, any queue would take our family out of Kathmandu and back into India, so it may as well be this one. There were some interested onlookers by now. The official eventually gave up on trying to get me to move from my coveted spot in the line, shrugged and shuffled off. I removed my hand from the barrier and another long hour passed before a plane arriving all the way from Luanda touched down on the tarmac. I speculated as to where it was scheduled to fly next. Luckily, it seemed the aircraft had been allocated for our stranded group. A good thing too as we were well past anxious at this stage and I was beginning to think we would be stuck in Nepal until next year—alternately spending long days inside the Kathmandu airport and short nights in luxurious hotels before starting the whole cycle all over again. At last the queue moved forward and before long we boarded the aircraft with no apology offered for the two-day delay. I was missing my stolen utility jacket and kept remembering important items left in the pockets, like my wooden tea caddies, specialised pens, and hand disinfectant. No doubt some really indispensable item will make itself known later down the line. Before boarding we had to identify all eleven pieces of luggage on the tarmac as we walked toward the plane steps—no easy task with the volume of gear we had. 

The Fleapit of Delhi 

Delhi was a cool 40°C, even at 8 p.m. I ran out of the terminal, flagged down a local taxi and asked the driver to find us an inexpensive hotel for the remainder of the night. He located a suitable fleapit. The bedroom door didn't actually lock, it featured a dank, algae-covered shower that utilised the traditional jug-and-bucket affair for hot water and the mattresses were clearly revamped billiard tables covered in dubiously grey sheets and a smelly, hairy blanket that made the prospect of cuddling a wild gorilla seem like a far better option. They featured a small black-and white television set in the corner that didn't even possess an electrical plug. The room was hot enough to bake scones. I managed to lighten our load from the big suitcase by transferring twenty kilograms of weight and redistributing the items among our hand luggage. This term was rapidly becoming redundant as no human hand that does not belong to King Kong could ever hope to clasp a bag that heavy without suffering instant herniation. It was an awful night. Some deeply embedded bedbugs bit my face causing my nose to swell up dramatically, so that I now resembled Jimmy Durante to add to my misery. Col and Tim fared no better; they had both slept fully dressed in their shirts and looked as though they had barely survived a session at the laundromat in the fast spin cycle. Four a.m. arrived all too soon and we blearily shuffled our way around the tiny, stuffy room, then staggered down to the lobby with our luggage—which we were now seriously contemplating leaving behind—and waited outside on the pavement for the taxi we had ordered. The Delhi airport was an oven and we were glad to finally board. In Muscat, Oman, there was another long, tedious wait sitting on hard airport stools. In fact, we waited nearly the whole day again before our connecting flight left. I eventually resorted to buying Col and myself a T-shirt each at the airport duty-free shop as we were beginning to smell a bit ripe and we didn’t want to push our refugee aesthetic too much. We were all completely fed up with sitting around international airports where Tim and Col had resorted to playing loud hand-slapping games on the benches to pass the hours. There were about forty veiled girls from Malaysia waiting near our gate; nursing recruits for the Kingdom, I surmised. They all looked completely shell-shocked—well, the tiny bits of their faces you could actually see did—and they moved in leaden steps towards the check-out counter. I looked at them and thought back to my time as an equally shocked woman starting my nursing contract in Saudi Arabia a few years earlier; it had not been a great introduction to the Middle East. 
They finally announced our flight.—unlike Air Nepal, who gave so little information that passengers were forced to use binoculars to see if the plane had actually landed, I kid you not. They were exceptionally strict with hand luggage sizes on this flight and an airline official was strolling around the gate with a tape measure draped around his scrawny neck, whipping it off the moment he spotted an oversized bag. Luckily, I had my heavy rucksack strapped firmly onto my back and I was leaning back against the wall über-casually, with a jacket draped over my shoulders even though it wasn't in the least bit cold so he didn't notice the bulk. My handbag and camera bag lay tucked neatly at my feet. Mr Tape Measure walked right past me, hesitated for a second when his eyes caught Col's camera gear, but then moved on down the lounge and I finally breathed again; it’s not easy trying to look nonchalant and not breathe at the same time. Col had shoved his second bag quickly under his seat, so he got away with the size restriction too. Boarding the aircraft was a bit hairy, as another security ogre was standing at the bottom of the plane stairs checking for luggage size and he was in the middle of telling an angry passenger off just as we arrived at the tarmac so we sidled past his clipboard and marched quickly up to our seats. 
It was wonderful seeing Cape Town again on landing and I relished sleeping in my clean, bug-free bed. Still, it had been a wonderful, unforgettable experience and I hoped to get the opportunity to see India and Nepal again in the future. Meantime, it was culture shock in reverse as we finally settled back into South Africa after more than two and half years in Saudi Arabia.
