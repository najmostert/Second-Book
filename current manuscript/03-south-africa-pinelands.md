# Chapter 2: South Africa: Pinelands

Having held up an ocean liner in Britain because we arrived late at the harbour was entirely normal behaviour for us as a family of five. By this time, I was thirteen, John was twelve, Jacqui was ten and our youngest sister, Jo, was born in the UK five years after Jacqui. They had to lower the gangplank again for us as we zoomed up to the ship's side, my Uncle Yvian bent over the steering wheel in a desperate attempt to get the car to speed up. Passengers were lined up on the deck, watching as we puffed our way to the first-class gangway. They cheered as we climbed the wooden steps. Once inside the SS Oriana, Dad thrust our papers at the steward then booted down into ‘cattle’ class. We spent three weeks on board, stopping briefly in Portugal before arriving in Cape Town harbour to our first awesome sight of a magnificent, cloudless Table Mountain. We were put up in Sea Point at the Crosby Hotel alongside other immigrants arriving in the country. My nose was pressed firmly against the taxi window as we drove past the promenade. ‘The locals are really well dressed here,’ I said, turning round to Mum and Dad. Both were silent but they nodded in unison. It was true that compared to Uganda, where torn, threadbare shirts and dresses were the norm, the local South Africans seemed remarkably well-attired. We arrived outside the Crosby, handed a stack of papers to complete and shown to our ‘apartment’. This was really just one large lounge with interlinking bedrooms. It was sparsely furnished with five single beds, bedside tables, a lounge suite of three easy chairs and a sagging settee. A narrow, galley-type kitchenette completed the rooms. The next few months staying there were spent frantically looking for accommodation—somewhere near the hospital for Dad and close to schools for us. Dad hired a second-hand car; a green Volkswagen beetle. In between looking at houses, we drove around the Cape Town area, exploring each suburb. Many Sundays were spent parked at the side of the road along various mountain passes where Bokkie signs warned drivers to avoid spreading fires. We always managed to find one of those concrete picnic spots featuring a round table and two cold cement slabs on either side for seats. There was usually a round fire-pit where Mum was soon scrunching up old newspapers to light a fire and cook long, fat sausages—called boerewors here—while Dad read the rest of the paper. He was a firm believer in a woman’s place being wherever there was cooking or cleaning to be done. The wind inevitably whipped everyone’s hair about, making any activity as thoroughly unpleasant as possible. Accompanying the sausages and bread rolls was the inevitable cardboard box of wine and a few beers. Alcohol was cheap and Mum and Dad had taken to its consumption with gusto. We played around the picnic site trying our best to avoid being run over by cars speeding along the road right next to us. We hated those outings. The drive back, while Dad manoeuvred the twisting mountain roads whilst being noticeably ‘three sheets to the wind’, was terrifying. Everything felt strange in the following weeks and months as we adjusted to South African life. People generally weren’t very welcoming which was deeply unsettling for us, especially for Mum and Dad, who were used to the instant bonhomie and warmth of Uganda. We found to our dismay that there was both a language divide and a rigid colour one. Coming out of the Crosby one morning, we saw that someone had painted ‘Go home British pigs!’ in lurid pink paint across the car. It took a tin of paint remover to rid the car of the nasty sign. I watched expressions of doubt sweep over my parents’ faces, followed by many anxious conversations murmured late at night. I made friends with a Spanish girl staying in another room at the Crosby. She owned a guitar and one day she sat cross-legged on the bed and sang Que Sera, Sera—‘whatever will be, will be, the future’s not ours to see’—which struck me as incredibly profound at the time. We ate in the Crosby dining room most days and came to accept the boiled, tasteless food on offer. The desserts, however, provided endless entertainment. Three days out of seven they served variations of bread pudding under increasingly exotic names, such as ‘Charlotte’s pudding’ or ‘Capirotada’. Sometimes it was just ‘bread pudding with raisins’ and occasionally ‘pudding surprise’—which was a misnomer if ever there was one. Eventually, we found a three-bedroom house to rent in Pinelands, in the southern suburbs. This meant that we three girls had to share a single bedroom with two single beds pushed tightly together, whilst John had his own room. We were each sent to different schools around the area. Unfortunately, they miscalculated my academic grade on arrival and I was placed in a class where I towered over everyone; I could walk through the lessons without even trying and spent miserable school breaks hiding in the toilets with a book. Eventually, I was transferred to another high school and placed in the appropriate grade. I was called out on my first day while waiting in line for morning assembly for having my hair loose, wearing shoes with small heels and wearing a bracelet. This had all been acceptable at my previous school in Ireland so I was mystified by the rigidity. 

We spent three years in South Africa. They were mostly unhappy years for my parents, who had a terribly hard time adapting to this new way of life. Dad was ostracised at work by his mostly Afrikaans-speaking colleagues for being an Irish outsider and not speaking the language. Mum was left to find out how things worked in this new country entirely alone as an English woman. There was no television in the country back then, which we found strange at first, but soon adapted to a lifestyle of playing outside. I made one or two good friends at school but it wasn't until a year later—when John had been accepted into the popular ‘Pinelands gang’ and given the nickname ‘Crow’ that my social circle expanded. Even then I was known merely as ‘Crow’s sister’. Life continued to be incredibly hard on Mum and Dad who had to rely almost entirely on each other for entertainment, given that local invitations were few and far between. As children, we adapted better but could see how painfully lonely they were. The ready availability of cheap drink didn't help. Furthermore, we found the apartheid system disturbing having grown up with a completely different lifestyle in Uganda, where our black friends could go wherever they wanted without a second thought or a moment of fear. Whilst we didn’t fully understand the politics of the apartheid government we knew instinctively that it was an unjust one. The following years continued with few changes so none of us were surprised when Mum and Dad called us into the lounge one afternoon to announce that we were moving yet again—this time to South West Africa, specifically to the capital, Windhoek. Naturally we weren’t happy about the move just as we were finally settling into our final years of school and making solid friends. But the folks had firmly made up their minds. Hey ho: another move, another house and another school where I would be the new girl joining mid-term. Dad had secured a position as the Chief Urologist at the Windhoek State Hospital and he reassured us that he had been promised one of the brand-new medical villas that had recently been built. Our last month in Cape Town was a chaotic blur: finishing off school exams, saying emotional goodbyes to friends and for Mum, packing up crates of furniture yet again. 

Final Nail in the Coffin

A week away from leaving by train, our furniture, such as it was, to follow by truck. I was walking home from visiting my friend Veronica and some other members of the ‘gang’. It was about a twenty-five-minute walk from the Spur Steakhouse to our house near the river and I was walking the familiar well-worn path towards the riverside, only one road away from home, when a man came out of the bushes and walked towards me. He was dressed in a white shirt and brown trousers, but wore no shoes. As he approached me, he asked, ‘Hello. Could you give me the time, please?’ His English was good and he was polite. I glanced down at my watch and said, ‘It’s five to four.’ He thanked me and I cut across him and continued walking towards the road. The sound I heard after this is one I shall remember for as long as I live. It was a heavy thudding—the sound of someone running fast on hard ground. I was just about to turn round when I felt his hands lock around my throat. ‘I’m going to kill you!’ the man to whom I had just given the time breathed into my ear. I struggled then struggled more desperately, clutching at his hands as his fingers tightened around my neck from behind. I was much smaller than his five-foot-ten frame and he was strong. I tried to remember what I had heard one should do in such a scenario but he had me pinned from behind so I couldn't knee him in the groin. ‘Please!’ I begged him in a croaky whisper but he merely tightened his grip over my carotid artery and repeated that he was going to kill me. I started to feel faint but continued to scratch ineffectually at his hands. I was panicking because I couldn't breathe; I opened my mouth, trying to suck in oxygen, but there was nothing. I remember the thought that it was a pity I would never know what Windhoek was going to be like, before everything went blank. I am writing this, so obviously I survived, but it was a terrifyingly close call. When trying to piece together what might have happened later, I reasoned that when I collapsed from a lack of oxygen, he must have thought he had killed me and run off—though not before taking my purse, which contained just a few cents. I didn't know how long I had been unconscious but when I opened my eyes, I was lying in the dirt. I staggered to my feet and without turning round or looking for the flip-flops that had come off my feet, I ran barefoot towards home. It was only a street away and I burst through the kitchen door breathing hoarsely through painful lungs. The next bit happened in slow motion. Dad took one look at me and disappeared, returning a moment later with a handgun. His face was pale and determined. Without even asking what had happened he said, ‘Come and show me where you were attacked.’ I got into the passenger seat, still breathing hard and feeling my neck, which was incredibly painful to the touch. ‘What did he look like?’ barked Dad, swinging the car out of the driveway and heading towards the main road. ‘He was a black man,’ I croaked. ‘He asked me the time,’ I continued, before starting to cry as the adrenaline receded and the shock of what had happened suddenly hit me. ‘Show me exactly where this happened,’ repeated Dad and I directed him to the river side of the road. Naturally, he was long gone, but Dad insisted on driving around the area for half an hour to see if I could spot him. I was exhausted by then and just wanted to go home, but dad said we had to report the incident at the police station. I repeated what had happened to the stern police officer with a thick Afrikaans accent as he recorded everything in neat, ponderous handwriting. I signed the form and we left. At home, Dad tended to my neck while Mum fussed over me with a cup of sweet tea. ‘Jeezus, a minute more and he’d have killed you!’ murmured Dad, looking at the skin where two large, finger-shaped bruises had already appeared. 
We left South Africa a week later with the police promising to provide us with an update and a possible court case if necessary. We never heard from them again. To this day, I can’t bear the sound of someone behind me, but I feel very lucky. I got to live a whole lifetime of experiences after being strangled. Not many people can say that.
