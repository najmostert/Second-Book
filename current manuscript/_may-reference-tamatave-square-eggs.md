# May version — Tamatave and the square eggs

*This is your May wording for checking only. Edit your July chapter beside it — do not treat this page as the finished book.*

---

Boarding for Tamatave takes place around 8. Luckily we’re in front of the queue as it turns out to be a kind of ‘Push in, first on’  type of scenario.
It’s a sixty seater airplane with one air hostess and steward.  
The flight is short with one stop at Marianara. The view from the plane of the East coast is good. It’s also known as the ‘route des contrabaniers’ as it was a pirate route many years ago. Azure atolls sit like bright blue rings amid their skirts of pale coral..


TAMATAVE ( TOAMSINA)

We land at the small airport in Toamasina, also called Tamatave, a port city on the east coast of Madagascar featuring Creole houses built on stilts, a large park shaded by banyan trees and home to a decaying colonial mansion. A wide, palm-lined road; Independence Avenue leads to a seaside boulevard.
We find a taxi driver in a short space of time. Wait for them to offload the plane, and ‘upload’ the next line of passengers and their luggage like some giant hard drive. A thin Malagassy man with a small neat moustache comes forward and introduces himself as Racoon. Actually we thought that’s what he said but later transpires it’s ‘Racoute’. He asks me which hotel we want. We tell him that we’re hoping there’s  room at the Coco Beach hotel (we’ve just seen the advert plastered on the airport wall). He says that he knows there’s room as he’s ‘in charge.’ We gather he’s the general factotum there and will also serve as our guide. It’s only a ten minute drive from the airport to the hotel but costs us mgf 35,000 which so annoys Col that he takes it out on Racoute and calls him a mosquito because he hovers around him. He seems undeterred by the insult and shows us the reception area with a smile.
Our room is a small leaf-covered bungalow with an adjoining shower and a large canopy bed. Racoute turns out to be a regular mine of information so he’s forgiven for his earlier indiscretion. He organises bicycles for Col and Tim and they set off for a spot of butterflying. 
I go for a walk to the nearby village but don’t find it terribly inspiring as I’m gawped at by everyone and none of my hearty, ‘Bonjour’s’ are returned. There’s a small field with a few Zebu in it so I amble over to have a look. The lads return later and we all have cold showers, there being a shortage of the warm variety.
Racoute has recommended the sea food so we order prawns for supper. We swallow Palludrine and Chloroquine for desert.

The following day at breakfast, Col asks the waitress for boiled eggs but she doesn’t  understand him so he takes out a piece of paper and draws what he thinks would be identified as a boiled egg inside a pot of boiling water. I’m not convinced and when she returns with two fried eggs and apologises that they aren’t ‘square enough’ I have to laugh. They were further thrown by the request that they be cooked for six minutes. Col sets off waving his butterfly net, and Racoute takes Tim and I off to see a vanilla pod plant and come to an old building with a group of people sitting outside on the hard baked ground, sorting rows of dried vanilla pods for export. We gaze at them for a while as they deftly sort the thin pods into rows then Racoute takes us to the nearby forest where wild vanilla plants (orchids) grow in abundance. He tells us they aren’t ripe yet and marches over to another tree and breaks off a twig and hands it to Tim,

‘Smell and taste zis tweeg’Tim eyes the twig doubtfully but obliges with a small lick and a sniff. He looks up and says,

‘Cinnamon?’

Racoute nods enthusiastically and breaks off another piece for me to smell and bite. He leads us to some clove trees after this and we smile our appreciation.I pay him for the impromptu tour and we go for a short rest in the cool bungalow. I later see Racoute polishing the wooden floor of the reception area with the husk of a dry coconut. It seems to work well and the floor looks clean and shiny. 

At breakfast the next day, I come prepared and ask for, 

‘Deux l’œuf vapour, s’il vouz plait.’

I receive two eggs in their shells, so raw they swim out of the cracked shell; a river of thin yellow yolk where I eye them on my plate for a minute or two before deciding I don’t have the energy to ask them to be ‘vapourised’ longer. I later realise I should have said ‘oeufs durs’ for boiled but I’m only on year two in Duolingo. 

We walk through the nearby ‘forét’ with the boys clutching butterfly nets. Some interested villagers ask what they’re for.

‘Les papillons,’  I inform them. One woman captures a shiny beetle in her hat and offers it to a bemused Col. 


NOSY MANGABE- MAROANSETRA

I wake up with a raging temperature which Col, being a positive lad, thinks is malaria. I’m leaning more towards a tummy bug. I drink a lot of water and we walk to the edge of the river and wait for a boat to collect us. Local porters from the hotel are taking our luggage on another boat. We are heading for Nosy Mangabe today; a small island reserve in Antongil Bay, around two kilometres from Maroansetra. 

Racoute loads up a small wooden boat with provisions, including a bucket of crayfish and a live chicken which squeaks pathetically in its straw basket. I’m not going to be able to eat it. I’ve already called him Clive. You can’t eat something you’ve named.
The boat ride is pleasant and cool and as I’m suffering from a mild temperature, it’s very welcome. We approach Nosy Mangabe, which lies in the shape of a  dark green chameleon. The remnants of a shipwreck, abandoned after the last cyclone lies near the small alcove that we are approaching. At the shoreline, the staff jump off and tie the boat to a short wooden stake. Immediately, all the provisions are carried off to what will be our camp site. A large wooden platform is ready for the tents, with a raffia roof and a few beams holding it upright. While our tents are being pitched, we set out our things and follow Racoute into the forests that surround the camp site. He points out variously, a leaf tailed gecko, two beautiful Montella’s (Golden or poison frogs) which are endemic frogs and the aposematic colouration warns predators that they’re poisonous. As I’m feeling off again, I return to camp for a lie down in the tents which have been made up for us with camp stretchers. I’ve discovered that we have a resident tree boa. He is lying curled up near the dustbin and I’m hoping he remains there till we leave. 
Col is also starting to feel unwell. Same symptoms as I have and we’re going on a night walk which we feel obliged to attend. 
After setting off just after dark with our head torches, a mouse lemur is spotted curled up in a tree not long after entering the dense forest. It has the sweetest face, big round red eyes and a tiny brown furry body. At night, the presence of torches light up the eyes of any nocturnal animal and makes for easier viewing. At the local Fishermans camp they’re busy smoking fish over a fire. Racoute finds a spot on the beach where we wait hopefully for an aye-aye to appear. It’s the kind of darkness that settles like a blanket over everything, removing any ability to identify anything without light. I’m sitting on a very damp log and my muscles are aching. The noise from the fishermen is too loud to attract anything other than our wrath. Tim says something is nibbling his feet and my temperature is soaring so that my eyeballs hurt.. After another half an hour of waiting with no sign of an aye-aye, we turn back to camp where I wash my face with cold water and crawl into the tent. Tim and Col bravely stay for supper of fish cooked in coconut. I visit the pit latrine a few times and each time another creature slithers in till there’s quite a collection; a crab, a lizard, a gecko and a giant snail. Ablutions are quick and economical in case the boa decides to join the party.

I feel a modicum better the next morning but still very weak as I climb off the camp bed and brave the dark, dank bug-ridden toilet to wash my face into wakefulness from the cold water bucket provided. Col and Racoute disappear into the forét for a long hike. 
Later we walk the contour path behind the camp and before long we come across a White ruffed brown lemur. Tim spots it midway up a tree. I’ve forgotten my video camera so Col and Tim run back to fetch it but, as is the way with nature, the lemur, figuring he’s about to be photographed, jumps smoothly away into the dense forest. I feel amazingly good today considering my raging temperature the past three days and bound over the rocks with ease. I’m really enjoying the two hour hike.I attach my headlight and set off in search of Aye-ayes again. These long-fingered lemurs, are primates only found in Madagascar with rodent-like teeth that perpetually grow and a special thin middle finger that they can use to catch grubs and larvae out of tree trunks. It is the world's largest nocturnal primate. 
Aye-ayes tap on the trunks and branches of trees at a rate of up to eight times per second, and listen to the echo. It’s this sound we are now trying to listen out for as we puff our way up a really steep incline. At the top of the mossy damp path we find a spot to sit and wait. I’m glowing with heat and find a bit of plastic in my waist bag and fan myself with short whooshing sounds. Col glares at me and hisses to keep quiet. We sit waiting for both darkness and an Aye-aye to approach. Whilst the former arrives like a damp blanket, the latter does not, so we rise and trudge further up the pathway. Racoute earlier informed us that there are special fruits along this path that they enjoy and as we climb he suddenly he bends down and lifts up a piece of fruit skin. He says the aye-aye has sucked the juice within and left the pulp intact. Then we hear it; a tap-tapping sound to our left so continue upwards following the sound of falling fruit crashing through the tall trees. It is very dark by now so I follow the slippery path by the dim yellow light of my head torch. 
Racoute whispers for me to wait while he and Col continue to move fast, back down the path. My torchlight bobs from side to side as I try to follow the sound of falling fruit. Suddenly Racoute appears out of the darkness and asks if he can borrow my torch just as I’m in the process of photographing the sloping pathway with my waist bag open to remove a new memory card. Racoute takes my torch and bounds quickly down the path leaving me clutching his useless torch in one hand. The darkness is immediate on his leaving and I’m left in the precarious position of standing on a 90° slope, one leg raised to keep balance, a wooden stick resting on my knee, a useless torch in one hand and my open camera bag in the other. I stand like this for twenty minutes, not daring to move my carefully elevated leg in case it meets with, well, nothing.

In the distance I make out the faint glow of my torch bobbing up and down the path while I try to maintain my rigid position. Sweat is pouring off my face and nestling in my neck. My knee trembles and suddenly gives way and I lose balance. While trying to grab the stick, my open camera bag falls, spewing batteries and camera cards into the velvet darkness. When I feel something hairy walk across my back, I decide it’s time to reclaim my torch and I screech out Racoute’s name. Col’s voice yells back at me to keep quiet and I fling a few choice words somewhere in his direction. Racoute bounds back to me and with the aid of my torch we locate most of my batteries and memory cards. I’m irritated that the guide who has not thought to bring his own batteries has relied on me. As we are slowly making our way back down to the beach, it starts to bucket down with rain and soon we’re all drenched and I stumble in the semi dark, over wet mossy rocks while Racoute bobs along with my torch. I’m now feeling wet and miserable and slightly hard done by as we trudge tiredly into camp. Supper is prawn kebabs, rice and steak. I’m unsure of the freshness of the food and when Racoute brings the boiled water for tea, I mime great enjoyment then casually ask him when they caught the prawns.

‘Ah couple of days ago. Maybe one week.’  he says, pouring hot water onto my teabag. 

I nod slowly and in one movement surreptitiously slide every prawn off my skewer into my palm. I grab my torch, mumble something about fetching salt and make a detour past the now wakeful boa. I drop the prawns into the bin, keeping my torch firmly fixed on the serpent, then back away to return quickly to my seat. If Racoute is surprised at my sudden clean plate which is also suspiciously devoid of prawn shells, he says nothing. Col takes my torch to go and photograph the emerald and white boa, which is a pity because I was on my way to the long drop. I have to take the mosquito repellent candle, turn it sideways to avoid hot wax dripping on me and walk in a curiously bent position to shield the waning candle from blowing out.  I shuffle along to the loo. Just as I’m lowering my buttocks over the hole for a wee, the candle goes out and I’m left in total darkness. I hear a scuttling sound somewhere near my feet. Without waiting to raise my trousers, I shuffle out, feeling my way to the tent and cursing under my breath. 

Just after breakfast a new group of people arrive in camp by boat. I have just emerged from the long drop, clutching a roll of toilet paper, my hair surely the result of a recent tornado and a matching scowl, to come face to face with a strange man with a video camera. The camera is rolling and he manages to capture my undignified demeanour on celluloid. A young woman with short blonde hair bounds up to me and introduces herself as an American firstly ( the accent helps)  and she follows with the information that they are a film crew from the ‘Yoo Ess’ and they’re doing a documentary on great tourist spots in Madagascar.

She asks where I’m from and I wave the bog roll in the general direction and say, ‘South Africa’. 

‘Oh cool,' she gushes, ‘We’re gonna be filming there too!’

I smile wanly and hope that my small walk-on part will be unrecognisable when the doccie comes out. The other members of the group are all over the place, swarming around like bees in our small camp. I’m captured again on camera lifting my sweaty T-shirt up to sniff it, partially exposing my right breast in the process. At this point I give up. I shall emphatically deny any knowledge of the imposter posing as me when the documentary becomes a viral success. 
Col, Recoute and I take a walk up to the summit of the mountain and come across two Brown lemurs but not much else. The summit is no more than a termite infested log in a leech ridden section of the forest and it’s so dense we are unable to see the sea below as I’d hoped. We're perching on the log trying to catch our breath when suddenly Racoute  leaps up clutching his neck and performs an impromptu dance. I’m just thinking his entertainment skills both unnecessary and lacking in variety when he shouts,

‘Leech! It is the leech!'

It turns out he is terrified of them. I pry the leech off his neck and then he makes the mistake of showing me how it happened by leaning against a moss covered tree and promptly receives another one. Descending is no easier as we have to walk down very damp, slippery moss covered rocks. We pass some ancient tombs of some of the first people on Nosy Mangabe.
Archaeological excavations reveal that the first traces of human presence on the island date back to the 8th century. 
These graveyards belong to the Betsimisaraka people who liked to place stone graves in caves to shelter them, as with the two we have come across.

Back at camp lunch is Chicken stew. Chicken is Clive, the poultry that sailed over in the boat with us. Try as I might, I can’t get the picture of his little red crest bobbing up and down in the raffia basket out of my head and find I can’t eat any of the stew. 
One of the film crew, a girl called Merril tells us that their departure has been delayed due to one of the pirogues capsizing. We’re commiserating the poor man’s fate and I glance over at Col. He’s nodding and murmuring words of sympathy but one side of his hair is sticking up like a Mohican.
The film crew eventually leave and we help to tidy up camp preparatory to leaving tomorrow morning. 

Supper is tasty this evening; large sardines, rice and sautéed vegetables. This is followed by a tot of vanilla rum on the deck. I crack open the well-travelled bottle of wine which I share with the other French man in camp. He’s a large jolly looking chap with a face that resembles the Queen of hearts in Alice in Wonderland. He tells us this is his fifth trip to Madagascar and that he is an amateur photographer. He and Col discuss camera technicalities and I watch the rain falling heavily in camp and over the forest. Monsieur ‘la Reine’ gives us some great tips about where to go next. Unfortunately our last chance to see the Aye-aye is thwarted by the rain. 
We set off soon after the boat arrives early the next morning and arrive back at the original bungalows we stayed in a week before.

MASOALA - East coast

The boat trip to Masoala is one of the worst I’ve experienced, mostly I suppose due to my fear of capsizing and a sizeable mistrust in the boatman who was the same simpleton who joined us on a walk yesterday. Not a full deck of cards. This translates into some hairy moments in the rough sea. I’m seated in the front and spend the entire two hours clutching the safety railing with my eyes closed. At one point I open my mouth to shout something to Tim and receive a mouthful of salt water. 

We eventually arrive at the beach and as the boat is being tied up, I look over at the circle of banana-leaf huts on raised wooden platforms which looks rustic but pleasant and unravel my stiff limbs and unclench my hands from the railings and hop off onto the beige sand. Tim passes me my rucksack and camera gear and we lug everything up a short steep hill to the camp site where the owner, Olivier is waiting to greet us. He wears a typical Gallic visage, with a large nose, dark curly hair and a mouth set in a permanent moué. He shows us to our respective huts and I strip off my wet clothes, have a cold shower and find some dry things to put on. Supper is crayfish and sautéed vegetables with riz. The cook is a tall gangly Madagascan girl with two thick plaits and a welcome smile. Our French friend from Nosy Mangabe suddenly bounds out of the forest looking like a character from Asterix and Obelisk. He tells us in his heavily accented voice that he spent six hours looking for the ‘wed-wuffed’ (Red ruffed) lemur with nary a one in sight. Our new guide, Seraphin, takes us on a hike up the mountain and the short steep incline soon becomes a dense rocky, slippery, multi coloured forest. I spot some beautiful blue feathered Couas  and a Bruchesia chameleon on a low branch.

After breakfast of omelette, toast and tea we set off for another forest walk. Seraphin points out various animals as we pass; a bright green leaf frog, some more blue Coua birds in the canopy and towards the end of the walk, a really gorgeously hued bird, the blue Vanga. It has a bright blue coloured back and head with a startling blue beak. We see Madagascar’s answer to the Hornbill; a red Fody but have no luck with the rare Red Ruffed lemur but Seraphin says they have recently been poached and are now scared and hide in the upper parts of the forest. How horrid is humanity in such cases and so sad for the wildlife.

We have sardines for lunch then I join the boys for a snorkel on the beach. The water is a beautiful tropical blue today. I find a big clam shell and decide to take it home with me and carry it back to camp where I have a short sleep as I’m suffering from an intense hormone related headache. Later I take a walk to the beach with Col and we share a beer while watching the dark sea swallow the sun. The Cheshire Cat smile of moon rises in a cloudless sky as the second act. 

The next morning we are up early for the boat. After slurping my mug of tea I chat to a new guide called, Arnaud. He tells me that there were fifty Red Ruffed Lemurs sighted in February, he returned in July with an American and a group of Military men arrived that afternoon and shots were heard . They didn’t see anything after that and the American apparently contacted the ANGAP and local police but he wasn’t sure if anything was done. All things considered Col is lucky to have seen them yesterday. The sea is calm today so the boat ride isn’t as bad as before for which I’m extremely grateful. 
After walk ing over to the Café Rive for a drink of Coco Rum when suddenly Racoute appears waving his arms in an excited manner,

'Come, come!’ He says, 'I have a Tenrec for you to see.’

We rush outside to find a rusty bucket on the ground. Racoute points excitedly into it and we bend to take a look. A tiny striped Tenrec is resting at the bottom of the bucket. Tenrecs are mammals endemic to Madagascar and look similar, but are not related to hedgehogs. Col wants to photograph the Tenrec in more natural surroundings than a rusty bucket so Racoute tips him out onto the grass and a hilarious five minutes ensues with us chasing the poor thing around the garden with our head torches and cameras. 

MAHAJANGA - Katsepy

While Tim and Racoute disappear for a last quad bike ride, Col and I sit on the verandah trying to photograph the beautiful Fody that is flitting through the nearby forest then leave for the airport.
The ‘Toy’ plane eventually arrives. It’s very small- no more than eight seats. I’m next to the window and watch in amazement as they load a live pig into the underbelly. 
The captain has just pushed the cockpit door open, leaned back and asked for his lunch. I buckle up and cast my eyes over the shabby interior, most of which could do with a make over. We stop briefly in a very dusty, hot and windy place called Mandritsara where a few people and the pig are off-loaded. We land with a huge bump on a runway that is more pot holed than a lunar surface. The air is hot and dry so somewhat bearable. Col hails a taxi to take us to Hotel De La Plage. The hotel is run by a French couple and we check in to a room with plenty of French rolls ( long cylindrical pillows) on the bed which Col immediately flings to the floor before wandering down to the lounge where we sit somewhat dejectedly trying to figure out what to do. The chef comes in and tells us to leave the lounge as it is closed.
Feeling very unwelcome, we take a wander down the road. The area doesn’t have the charm or beauty of the other places we’ve been and instead is dusty, windblown and has a lot of rubbish strewn around the many derelict buildings. We pass a dead dog on the side of the road with its guts spilling all over the pavement which puts us all in a further melancholic mood. After walking back to hotel ‘Grubby’ feeling dehydrated and irritable, we order supper which takes ages to arrive then back to our room to tuck ourselves in the fly spattered mosquito net. Definitely a low point in the holiday.

The next day we are all up early and slowly move our luggage over to the other hotel and check out of the De La Plage. The hotel owner barely looks up from his book when we leave the keys. 
I somewhat uncharitably imagine him choking on a mouldy croissant and feel better.

Our new room is smaller but more tastefully designed. I make us some tea then catch a lift with a construction worker to the ‘pont de bateaux’ or ferry to the uninitiated. The ferry takes us to a small fishing village in Katsepy which is located in the northwest of Majunga. There we find our haven for the day at Madame Bouchards, - a beautifully furnished niche in the otherwise squalid village surroundings. 
Mde. Bouchard’s pretty daughter takes our order and soon we are drinking tea from a gold teapot and munching chocolate croissants. I immediately feel so much better. Tim and Col catch butterflies while I chat with Mde. B the younger, about politics, the war, tourism ( she has a small hotel and restaurant in central Mahajanga). Later I join Col and Tim for a long walk along the beach picking up shells and watch the local fishermen casting their nets wide into the ocean next to piles of drying shrimp on rattan mats. Later we board the ferryand sit on the top deck. It turns very slowly and moves close to the beach to allow nine Zebu to climb aboard. We set off again but it isn’t long before the engine is cut and we stop. It seems someone has fallen off. People immediately lean over to the left to watch and find a figure swimming crazily in circles. Several life buoys are flung at him and eventually he is hoisted up the side by several onlookers. I am feeling very anxious because all 250 people on board have moved to the left and the ferry is listing ominously. I march over to the right side in a futile attempt to balance the weight. I call out,

‘Never mind gawping at the drowning man, come back to your seats!’ In an authoritative voice.

Naturally no one takes any notice of me. The captain restores order and we set off into the choppy sea, tilting and rolling again against the high angry waves. To add to my discomfort, there’s a large petrol tanker on board with neither a driver nor restraint and it moves back and forwards with each wave. 



AMPIJAROA

The heat is intense and the surroundings are dry and denuded of trees. The taxi brings us  the ‘Parc de Nasionale a Ampijarowa more by luck than design. Luckily, ( as we haven’t booked,) there are two rooms for us. We drop off our suitcases and go in search of Guy the guide (pronounced the French way, ’Gee’. Eric is also here and joins us on a night walk through the dry deciduous forest. Along the flat paths we come across the Horned or Rhinoceros  chameleon, a turtle and some blue vangas then suddenly a group of Coquerrelles Sifakas - beautiful rich mahogany, chocolate arms and white backs. They have large round green eyes and look like giant white teddy bears.

The following morning we go looking for the Angonoka tortoise enclosure (also known as Ploughshare tortoise) which are the rarest in the world with unique, high-domed shells. The enclosure is managed by Jersey Wildlife ( Gerald Durrell's zoo in the U.K.) which was established in 1995. 
The Angonoka tortoise is endangered mainly because it’s highly sought after in the international pet trade.
Col and I photograph some Sifakas in the camp area. It’s fun watching their antics and the special side dance they perform on two legs. I notice a man selling bananas, pickled mango, dried fish and small yams at the side of the road and buy some bananas. He wears an old blue cap and his wife who joins him from the bushes, wears a dress with three straw hats on her head.
The next morning we join Guy for a forest walk and see the Madagascan Fish eagle which is quite rare apparently.
He has brought along a tape recorder and presses play and we collapse with laughter. There’s a faint sound of the rare Acity bird but it’s almost completely drowned out by the sound of a car engine. Col says he didn’t know Acity’s could drive. The laugh is on us though because, not half an hour later, we come across the rare Acity, high up a tree but well visible. It’s very exciting.
The road to ‘Tana is crisscrossed with bridges in all manner of size and description. We cross over the many waterfalls of the Betsiboka River then stop again at a horrible dry, dusty, humid village. I’m bursting for the loo, a small smiling woman in a faded yellow dress takes me to a dirty wooden door with more holes than a Swiss cheese, down a rat infested alley. I open the creaky door with trepidation and peer into the gloom. I instantly cover my nose. Flies are swarming around the pit like drones. I notice a plastic jug and a bucket of dirty brown water. I finish quickly and sprint out feeling instantly remorseful they have to live with these conditions on a daily basis.

It becomes really cold. A huge blood red moon hovers in the sky casting pale crimson light over the landscape. The driver lends Tim and I a blanket to share. He entertains us with music from an old car tape and we listen to music who’s genre ranges from the Ink Spots to Reggae and arrive at Hotel Glacier at one in the morning.  The half asleep receptionist tells us there isn’t a room for us. He says, after consulting a tattered register, that we have booked for tomorrow night. Paul, the driver saves the day (or night) again by taking us to a rather more expensive Hotel de la France. At least we have a room for what’s left of the night.

TANA

I bring my powdered milk and tea bags into the five star hotel dining room much to Col’s amusement. One has to be careful with the water and it’s essential I start the day with a good cuppa. Our ground floor room at hotel Glacier faces a very busy thoroughfare and we have more than a couple of Vaasa’s nodding a ‘bonjour’ at us. After a simple lunch we take a taxi to the Tzimbazaza zoo which was founded in 1925. It includes a large lake, a botanical garden and of course, the zoo itself. Whilst we don’t always enjoy zoos, it’s can be an opportunity to see some of the animals we wouldn’t otherwise see.. Col bumps into an old guide from a previous visit and we must have overpaid him judging from the enthusiastic welcome we receive.
By 5pm most visitors have left the park and we meet up with Bruno, our ‘night guide’ whose services we have paid to see the Aye-aye. These interesting creatures are nocturnal so dusk is a good time to see them. The night descends rapidly and one minute we’re following Bruno into some cages and next minute darkness has enveloped us and almost removes vision, so he switches on his hefty torch and shines it directly into a large cage and the first thing we see are huge, round orange eyes, then gradually the entire body is revealed in the dim light like the Cheshire Cat. He’s bigger than I imagined, the size of a small dog with a huge bushy tail. I notice the extra long middle finger and dark brown fur. Aye-aye’s are the world's largest nocturnal primate. This specimen darts around the cage making photography a bit tricky but we manage to get some nice footage of this magnificently weird creature. Back at Hotel Glacier, we find Harianbinana waiting for us so we invite her to join us for supper. In her honour we have a typical Malagasy meal of spinach and rice with Zebu and shredded manioc leaves. We hug farewell as we will not be seeing her again.
A bientǒt Madagascar!

ZIMBABWE 2004


En route Zimbabwe by car, I book us into a small apartment for the night. Early this morning I step into five inches of water in the loo which simultaneously freeze my ankles and removes any traces of sleep. Col says he’d done the same thing last night but as well as freezing his feet, the toilet seat hadn’t remained in place but instead attached itself to his nether regions. I laugh at the image this brings. The border is the usual experience in slow torture and tests my already flimsy patience to the max. We’re ascended upon by the sick, the wounded and the downright desperate after opening the door. Batting away these pests, we make our way to the queue and prepare to wait a long while. A few touts approach us offering their services which includes such mentally challenging processes as filling in forms in English and answering questions such as, ‘ Name, occupation and address’ etc. 
As we feel justifiably qualified to do this ourselves without the assistance of their ‘expert ‘ help we deny assistance until one of them manages to convince us that his assistance will speed up the process. Thus it is that we find ourselves paying for the privilege for them to stand in our personal space for two hours while Col laboriously enters the details of his many cameras and equipment. The South African side has the same people informing us of the procedure for crossing the border. I receive several kicks to the shin from Col when I hear we have to add carbon tax to the already hefty payment. I’d already endeared myself to him from the start by hooting at the Police Stop. My excuse is that my finger slipped. 
Later at the Elephant and Tusker hotel; an aging beauty of erstwhile years, we stop for breakfast. The boma is empty, save one rather dour family who barely speak to each other but bark orders at the hapless waiter who’s wearing a bright green jacket that’s three sizes too small for him. The Bumi river is a distant memory as it’s now hidden in the depths of pale orange sand. I manage to send three sms’s at the border. Two find their target but one is still somewhere in cyber space. We leave Bumi and hare off towards Harare - a six hour drive away. The roads are in a terrible state of disrepair and decay compared to the last time we were here in 1997, with signs as empty of information as the roads are empty of cars. Petrol is hard to come by and it’s not uncommon to find lines of cars a kilometre long outside of some of the petrol stations. Luckily we filled up in Beitbridge across the border where petrol is more easily available. Dry deciduous trees line the dusty roads and Msasa tress are devoid of their usual pretty orange colours until September and the grass is a dry, pale, auburn colour. There are few people walking along the roads, but those who are walking the dusty roadside, look dejected, quiet despair describes their features. These sorry looking people are a product of the tyrannical rule of Mugabe, the president of Zimbabwe. After nearly four decades of (mis)rule he has managed to bring the once-rich nation to economic ruin, by presiding over the forced takeover of white-owned farms at the end of the 1990’s, which in turn, devastated agriculture and led to hyperinflation. There’s the occasional figure waiting at lonely bus stops, not so much for the transport, which is unlikely to arrive, but in hope for something better. 
Our first forced stop goes without incident, despite Col’s misgivings and audible groan when we spot the police presence. This is probably more because he’s not sure of how I'll respond, than the police themselves. I’m not good with abusive authority as you’ll know by now. It’s generally accepted that you suck it in and do what they ask but I find my brain has not received that memo. As we arrive in Harare, having come through a really grotty part of town, we’re astounded at the state of general degradation since we were here last. There are people everywhere, walking the streets, selling, talking, standing in groups, vendors selling oranges from makeshift boxes. In other words a typical African scene.
We’re staying with Cecilia and Andy. Col visits the bank to get some Zim dollars. The notes all have a minimum of five zeros after whatever denomination it is and we have a few ‘trillion dollar’ notes that are practically worthless. Col and I go off to Mukuvisi, a small game park nearby. Our guide is Ben and he takes us on an hour and a half bush walk where we come across giraffe, sable antelope and some zebra. Ben chats non stop as we trample through the golden grass; the town of Harare a purple backdrop in the distance. Ben tells us a little about Bob Mugabe’s wife Grace. She was his secretary first and used to be married until her husband disappeared under mysterious circumstances. She’s known as ‘Gucci Grace’ due to her propensity for jetting off to Europe on shopping sprees, funded by the government. We murmur our surprise at this audacity then return to join the others outside for some wine and a chat. Col, Tim and I head off to Ewanrigg gardens for the morning, which is approximately 38 kilometres from Harare. It is renowned for its extensive collection of aloes and cycads. En route we’re stopped at a police block and asked if we have a car radio. Luckily we don’t. That would have elicited a fine of $10 without the accompanying licence which is also difficult to obtain.. I later join Cecilia for a trip to a supermarket. The shelves are not at all well stocked; most shelves are completely empty. It’s a sad situation. Cecilia tells us that their friends take turns to enter South Africa to do a load of shopping and take orders for each other. She tells me she keeps small denomination notes under the sun visa for police stop bribes.
Journey to Mana Pools

We are up early to prepare the cars for our trip to Mana Pools and get there at 5 p.m. and pay a hefty fee as we are from South Africa. Mana Pools means ‘four pools’ in the local Shona language. It’s a massive wildlife sanctuary that straddles the Zambezi river. It has abundant game and camp sites. Mana is also a proclaimed World Heritage Site. 
We have a campsite and immediately spot some female elephants on the way in. The campsite itself is also well laid out and pleasant with the tall mahogany, fig and acacia trees providing adequate shade. It is full of vervet monkeys scampering all over the place, so we will have to be extra vigilant with our provisions. Just as I am thinking this, a monkey jumps through the open window of the Toyota and steals a toilet roll.
It is getting dark, so we quickly set up our two tents. We make 'spam bol' (our take on spaghetti bolognese) for supper and eat overlooking the Zambezi River from the campsite. Cecilia regales us with tales of hyena antics from the previous trip and Tim says he can see hundreds of eyes peering out of the dark at us. Col turns on the Toyota headlights which reveals a herd of impala staring back at us and goes some way to appeasing him. We crawl into our tents early, but sleep is difficult for me with all the sounds of wildlife; hyenas howling, hippo grunts, monkeys chirruping and, in the wee hours, the guttural growl of a lion. I love it though and it’s these very sounds that I miss most when I’m not in the bush.


First Full Day in Mana

I emerge from our tent with aching bones and wild hair to start a fire for tea. The rest of the family emerge a bit later, grumbling about a lack of sleep and a fear of some of the more alarming sounds in the night. A lone bull hippo is strolling on the periphery of the camp towards the river. Hippos are known for their aggressive and dangerous behaviour and have killed any unfortunate person found to be in their way.
After tea in tin mugs, we batten down the hatches as best we can (these being tents, are canvas flaps and not very secure) and set off for an early game drive.
There’s a variety of animals: monkeys, impala, silver-backed jackals, and waterbuck with their distinctive ‘wet paint toilet seat’ bottoms. We also see elephant—one huge bull with a tusk missing—many hornbills, open-billed storks, saddle-billed storks, more hippos, painted storks, eland, kudu, zebra, crocs, and even a python curled up near the edge of the river. It's a great morning's viewing and we head back to camp for pan-toasted bread and tea, in good spirits. I notice a buffalo lurking not far from our tents and eye him as warily as he eyes us. Buffalo are equally dangerous when confronted. Col gives Tim his old camera, and he stands next to Col, zooming in and out and photographing whatever his dad is photographing, like two peas in a pod. I have a lovely hot bath in the ablutions block before setting off for an afternoon drive around 3 p.m.where we see a lot of what we saw this morning, then head back to camp for sundowners overlooking the Zambezi River with hippos snorting their amusement. 
Steak cooked over the fire with toasted bread for supper, and a glass of still-chilled wine. This time, I decide to join Cecilia in her tent and leave Tim and Col in the other one. The night sounds of hyenas, hippos, nightjars, and rutting impala are soothing, and I drift off early. 
Two buffalo have wandered into camp this morning—one stands just outside the ablutions block, which makes washing a little difficult. I take a really circuitous route and then run in quickly.


Hyenas, Buffalo, and Tsetse Flies

Last night, I heard noises close by and got up to peer out of the tent to find a huge hyena at the camp table. The smell of barbecued meat is the attraction. There are many other nocturnal noises: hyenas giggling, lions roaring, buffalo bellowing, and grunting hippos to name a few. I am woken again a few hours later by some loud rustling sounds and peer from the tent flap at a beautiful male eland wading through the silver, moonlit water. He silently leaves the river and stands close to the boys' tent, munching on leaves. It’s a wonderful sight and I go back to my sleeping bag feeling happy.
I wake up early and try to head for the ablution block, but my favourite buffalo is there guarding the entrance. I have to wait till he moves a little way from the door to dart in and wash.
This morning’s drive is not pleasant, as I am bitten excessively by tsetse flies, and my left hand and arm are swollen and painfully itchy. The tsetse fly is a dangerous insect that can spread trypanosomiasis ( sleeping sickness) if severely bitten.
Breakfast is taken at a pan overlooking the Zambezi, where hippos frolic noisily for our entertainment. Back at camp to find the campsite has been raided by monkeys. They have torn the big tent by dragging the tent peg backwards, leaving it with two huge holes. The smaller tent has a tear along the front flap as well. Cecilia and I repair the damage with black duct tape. Cecilia tells one of the camp cleaners about the monkeys tearing our tents. Unfortunately, he has the IQ of an amoeba and just stares at her open mouthed, so not a lot will be done about it. We have seen several leguaans along the pan today, looking like prehistoric creatures. 
My period has started so, feeling bloated and irritable, I get up around 5 a.m.  feeling like a piece of chum bait  walking across to the ablution block by the light of a pink Barbie torch, whose beam gives the name ‘penlight’ new meaning. My buffalo isn’t here but I keep glancing around at dark shapes and twinkling eyes with some trepidation, waiting for some hungry beast to pounce. We removed anything of value from the tents this morning in case the monkeys or baboons want another go at ferreting for food. After a fairly uneventful drive, we return to camp to find that the disgruntled baboons have crapped their annoyance at finding no food. Faecal matter perches down the top and sides of our tents. A family of ‘pumbas’ are scattered in mounds all over  camp, as well as the ubiquitous, ablution-guarding buffalo. 
Col goes off to try his luck with the bee-eaters again while Cecilia and I clean off the tents and tidy up before preparing supper. I have trouble with my fire-lighting skills, so Col finishes off. We have bacon and scrambled egg for lunch with a three-bean salad and crackers—good camp cuisine. During the bacon-frying process, I have a feeling I am being watched and turn around to find a huge hyena under a fig tree; he’s hunched over, salivating at the possibility of some rashers. I shout loudly in fright and he actually takes flight, skulking off to await his chances from the nearby bushes. We have another sundowner over the river before retiring to our tents for the cacophony of evening sounds. Luckily there’s no sign of the hyena.

The Journey Back
One of the park officials asked if he could get a lift with us to Karoi. We have a bit of game spotting en route to the main gate but don’t see much besides wild piggies, a few elephant, and some zebra. We leave Mana Pools park and travel along the red, dusty murram roads in search of some baobab fruit.
The ‘soda bicarb’ fruit of the baobab tree, also known as ‘monkey fruit’, has a tough outer shell. Inside, kidney-shaped seeds are set in a dry pulp. Baobab powder has a sharp citrus flavour similar to sherbet. We find several pods on the ground and have a taste of the sweet-sour delicacy.
We set off again, swerving over dry rivulets of the dirt road until Col passes us in the other vehicle. This is untimely, as shortly afterwards, we have a blowout and the rear left wheel is in ribbons. Both Cecilia and I have to get out in ‘lion’ country to change the huge tyre. It's not an easy process due to the size and the weight of the Toyota. Her 4x4 is relatively new, so she has no idea how to change the tyre. We consult the guidebook, but it seems to be written in ancient Greek. Luckily, I have some knowledge based on our tyre changes in Saudi so we find the winch to bring down the new tyre from underneath the Land Cruiser's belly. However, it takes us a lot longer to work out how to release the jack from its vice-held grip in the back. I crawl underneath the car, spitting dust, until I see the winch chain, and I’m in the process of guiding Cecilia through the procedure when a succession of 4x4s slow down and ask if we need help. I ask one of the drivers if he would try to catch up with Col and ask him to come back to us. They agree and roar off. Cecilia and I manage to get the tyre off, then I have a nervous wee in front of the back door of the Toyota while Cecilia keeps an eye out for predators. We are just trying to work out where to place the jack when Col arrives in a skitter of stones and dust. He and Amos, the guide, complete the tyre-changing process. I am leaning over in a crouched position, showing Col where the jack should go and place my hand on the ground for balance and encounter a very damp spot and realise too late that it is exactly where I wee’d a few minutes before. After finding some wet wipes for my hands, we continue towards Karoi and stop at a motel for tea and sarmies. And in my case, to wash my hands properly. As we’re munching on sandwiches, Col tells us that Amos, after learning Col is a doctor, began telling him stories of woe regarding his prowess, or rather lack of it, in the bedroom. He declared that he needs some pills for his ‘backbone’ (a euphemism for his replicator applicator, we assume). Col says he gave him some vitamins and told him that would do the trick. We chuckle at this for a while and Tim asks what did he mean by backbone. ‘His willy’ I state simply. ‘Oh’ says Tim and takes another bite of his sandwich.


Towards the Bvumba Mountains

Col packs the car, which is looking distinctly heavier than when we arrived. We bid a fond farewell to all the kids before they leave for school, and then to Andy and Cecilia. It’s only 7:30 a.m. when we set off, but we have a long way to go. My tsetse fly bites are looking really horrible. They have left black and blue bruises all over my body—I jokingly say to Col that I look like a battered wife. As long as I don’t get trypanosomiasis, it will all fade soon enough.
The Leopard Rock hotel is a beautiful spot, so far up the mountain and overlooking the valley below. The hotel has manicured lawns, lots of bright red aloes, huge fig trees, flowering gardens, and vervet monkeys. One of the guards points out a monkey and tells me it is a Samango monkey, so I photograph it on the roof (the monkey, that is; I’m still on terra firma).
Great Zimbabwe and the Journey South

Later we set off towards the Great Zimbabwe Ruins, stopping at Chimanimani en route. The Chimanaimani mountains stand majestically overlooking the small town.
The silver glimmer of Lake Kyle is visible in the distance but we take the wrong turning, as the Zimbabwe authorities in their misguided wisdom, have removed most of the signposts with the intention of replacing them with the new name changes. Unfortunately, they haven’t got to that part yet. 
Earlier I asked Cecilia where the Zimbabwe Ruins are, and she replied,
‘Everywhere!’  
The ruins were built between the 11th and 15th centuries, are the largest stone ruins in sub-Saharan Africa, spanning approximately 80 hectares. The ruins been inhabited in the Iron Age, by a Bantu population called the Shona. 
We climb to the top of the fort via the ‘authentic’ route. The view is great from here. We take some photos then walk to the other part of the ruins. The ruins also feature Cecil John Rhodes' grave, he was the Founder of the southern African territory of Rhodesia, which the company named after him in 1895. In his last will and testament he wrote;
“I admire the grandeur and loneliness of the Matopos in Rhodesia and therefore I desire to be buried in the Matopos on the hill which I used to visit and which I called a 'View of the World.”

We have several police stops en route to the border. At the last stop, they say we have been speeding. 

‘Why are you speeding in a built-up area?' asks the rotund policeman, fingers twitching for a bribe. Col says he can’t see a sign saying it is a 60km zone, which he knows for a fact as there’s an absence of road signs so we’re let go.
Tim helps Col fill up our tank with petrol from our spare containers, as we are not likely to find any until we cross the border and for a while after that. 
Col mutters that’s going to be ‘fun’ at the border, ( with an accusatory glance at me). Actually, apart from the general attitude, which is ‘kraal manure’ at best, we get through customs with minimal fuss. The South African side holds the usual officious, unhelpful, and insolent collection of officialdom, and I long to punch their collective faces or at least poke them in the eyes. We are asked to climb out of the car and stand in a shoebox-sized container of disinfectant, and this apparently is the border ‘Foot and Mouth’ control done with.

An absolutely magnificent sunset spreads it’s vibrant colours over the darkening sky, which sweetens the mood somewhat as we slowly return home.
