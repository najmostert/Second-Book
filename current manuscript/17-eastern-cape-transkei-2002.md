# Chapter 15: Eastern Cape: Transkei 2002

I enjoyed watching the wild landscape change from the rolling green hills of the Transkei to the dry, mountainous West Coast, dotted with beautiful fynbos vegetation and past the occasional flaming gum tree. We had arrived near Port St Johns on the East Coast. The lodge we were staying at was located up a steep hill and looked rather nondescript from the outside. We were warmly met by a woman of around sixty called Thelma, and a rather dour, taciturn man called Clive. Local vendors were selling wild leeches and cooked corn on the cob along the roadside nearby. I wondered idly what the leeches were for. In my mum’s day as a young nurse, medical teams were still using them for a procedure called blood-letting, used to treat various inflammatory ailments. The historical practice was rooted in the ancient belief that illness stemmed from 'imbalances' in the core body fluids, so live leeches were applied to the skin to draw blood from specific areas, thereby theoretically removing the 'poisons'. Perhaps that was the purpose here on the coast- I never found out. After breakfast we dressed in our heavy hiking gear, drove to the turn-off for the river and left our car parked under a tree. Col, Tim and I set off on foot along a muddy road, clambering over fallen tree trunks and balancing precariously over slippery logs to reach the waterfall. We traversed the Umzimvubu River which flows all the way from the Drakensberg Mountains further north, were banks are covered in beautiful coastal forest flowers, such as vibrant flame lilies and continued walking up a steep incline toward the waterfall. After about an hour of trekking we found a shaded spot to rest our legs. A group of tourists from the United Kingdom and the United States were sitting in the shady clearing, eating snacks and sucking on reefers and the mountain air was thick with pungent, sweet smoke. I joked to Col that we shouldn’t breathe in too deeply, then turned to him laughing at the absurdity when I noticed his far-away, intensely focused expression. ‘Col! I said don’t breathe in!’ I admonished him a little unfairly. He turned his head slowly towards me and replied, 'I think I saw a rare swallowtail back there in the thicket so I might try and catch a few using some of my rotten banana as bait.' This, I smiled to myself was perfectly normal conversation for Col. Silaka was a magnificent grassland flanked by dense, indigenous coastal forests. The almost harsh, rugged coastline was softened by rolling grassy plains and the forest floors liberally covered with wild lilies, orchids and thick velvet mosses. Col and Tim went for a swim in the surf while I relaxed and read my book. A small troop of vervet monkeys were swinging about in the nearby forest canopy. Later that evening we enjoyed a drink with Thelma and were thoroughly mortified to discover that the couple we’d been calling ‘Clive and Thelma’ for the duration of our stay were actually named Pam and Cecil! We hadn't the foggiest notion where we’d acquired their incorrect names and they hadn’t once corrected us—it was quite embarrassing really. For dinner we visited Lily's Lodge to enjoy a tasty seafood meal while perched directly over the valley with a panoramic view of the sea. Along the coastal road, there were numerous signs of the festive revelry that was expected later as the old year drew to a close. Neither of us wanted to stay up late or get caught in the inevitable police roadblocks so we headed back to our quarters before 8 p.m. We chatted briefly with Thelma-Clive-Pam-Cecil before retreating to our room to watch a movie and settle down for the night. Suddenly, a colossal wave of sound rent the midnight air. The entire room shook with the booming sound of music, drunken revelry, a thumping bass line and wailing noises that went on all night long: ‘Wa, wa, aaaah, wawawa, waaaah!’ 

Reverse Culture Shock on the Coast 

The noise continued unabated all night long, forcing Col to take his blankets and sleep on the floor. We set off the next morning bleary-eyed and in desperate search of sustenance. We told our multi-named hosts that we had to return to Cape Town but had in fact booked an extra night at Lily’s Lodge just to escape the chaos (we spent so much time over at their restaurant anyway). Col returned from the bush with a few new butterflies before driving down to the beach and checking in at the lodge. We passed streams of hungover, bleary-eyed revellers and noticed commercial buses parked illegally in strict ‘no buses’ zones, along with locals washing their cars in designated ‘no washing of cars’ areas. 
Earlier in the holiday, Col and I met an interesting chap named Ben Decker during one of our many sorties down to the beach. When Col finally returned from his walk, Ben arrived soon after and immediately greeted us with a massive bear hug, lifting me right off the ground. He turned and gave Col an identical hug. Tim and I had a giggle watching Col’s utterly startled face as his feet were lifted a good few inches off the deck. 
When going for breakfast the following morning, I thought that if I had to eat another egg, I would literally start clucking. Aside from fresh crayfish, all I’d consumed the entire holiday was eggs. I later drove Col down to the forest turn-off so he could venture into the undergrowth again to collect more butterflies. He has quite a nice collection, which he originally started when he was only six years old. When we lived in Saudi Arabia, Col had left his extensive collection of common and rare butterflies at a commercial butterfly farm in Cape Town where visitors could gaze in amazement at the variety he had caught all over the world. 

Pepper, Leather, and Old Chickens 

The drive to Hogsback took us two and a half hours, navigating the winding mountainous terrain and passing through the historic town of Alice (yes, we have a town named Alice here in the Eastern Cape). Col pulled up outside the Armin Mountain Lodge where we had stayed once before years ago. The owner remembered us. ‘I only remember the interesting people,’ she beamed at us. We smiled back politely and tried our best to look even more interesting. The fact I was currently wearing a formal garden party dress equipped with a feather in my hair, Col sporting his many-pocketed utility bird jacket with a butterfly net protruding from a rear pocket, and Tim sporting bright orange eyebrows went some way toward attesting to our eccentricity. She gave us two interlinking rooms and Col immediately set off into the bush with his net and a plastic container of foul-smelling rotten bananas for bait. Only two other couples occupied the residents' lounge; a stiff, unsmiling elderly pair and a very young honeymoon couple. I couldn't help but giggle at the thought that the young honeymooners might take one look at the sullen, silent pair across the rug and wonder if they had just made a terrible decision. The male half of the lodge management duo emerged from the woodwork and asked what we wanted to drink. He then walked over to a small wooden cabinet fixed against the wall, featuring three padlocks and carefully unlocked each one in silence. He equally carefully poured the drinks into a tray of glasses, locked all three padlocks again with grim precision and dispersed the beverages to us. He then returned into the shadows, never to be seen again for the rest of the evening. He must have been an absolute ball of fun on New Year’s Eve. At supper the following day, we caught the attention of the dour owner of the drinks cabinet and ordered a bottle of regional red wine, knowing his measures would be tightly controlled. He returned after a twenty-minute battle at the locked cabinet and reluctantly set the bottle on our table. We each took a tentative sip. It was absolutely awful; sour, acidic and left a bitter aftertaste on the tongue. After careful scrutiny of the printed label the vintage claimed to feature ‘undertones of black pepper and burnt leather’. We decided it tasted rather more of old leather than pepper but managed to swallow most of the bottle with the aid of a plate of roast lamb and a dish titled ‘chicken surprise’. The ultimate surprise being the chicken had died of old age long before reaching the pot. 

The Backwards T-Shirt Tour

On arrival at the gates of Addo Elephant National Park we were informed by the rangers that there was absolutely no accommodation or camping space available inside the reserve. This meant we had to take a very quick game drive before the guards closed the main gates for the evening leaving us to reluctantly head into the city of Port Elizabeth where we secured a room at a small seaside hotel. We were the only customers in the dining room which was just as well given Tim was wearing a dirty T-shirt worn backwards, Col’s hair was standing stiff with layers of highway dust and I looked as though I’d just woken from a long, debilitating illness. I mused at how often our appearances let us down when travelling; we always start the journey looking perfectly respectable but it never lasts. An amusing waiter livened up his somewhat dull shift by pretending to serve us completely different dishes to those we’d ordered. Hence, my plate of spaghetti was formally announced as ‘chocolate pudding’, Tim’s fish and chips was delivered as a ‘green garden salad’ and Col’s meal; with ‘Here you are sir, your prime whale steak’. We happily went along with the charade, although privately I figured if he carried the comedy routine for much longer I might have to hit him over the head with my heavy soup spoon. By next morning our dirty clothing scenario was becoming desperate. We were all wearing T-shirts that were badly stained with food and shorts that were heavily mud-spattered from the trails so we decided to skip the hotel breakfast and head straight for Knysna stopping en route at a rural farm café for a meal. A  live wolf was stuck inside a wire cage outside the entrance with a wooden notice board declaring that donations for its upkeep were welcome. Tim generously donated R150 from my purse. In Nature's Valley Col pulled over at a small indigenous forest patch so he could put up his butterfly traps in the canopy. After parking in the grounds of the Tsitsikamma National Park we took a long, beautiful walk through the forest giant trees. Along the path I ran into one of the mothers from Tim's school. She was a very chatty sort and eagerly told us of a nearby river that featured heavy ropes tied to an overhanging tree branch which she felt sure I would enjoy swinging from. She clearly didn't know me well so I let the suggestion pass, but the boys were interested. Soon Col and Tim were enthusiastically flinging themselves over the Coca-Cola-coloured blackwater via a length of thick hemp rope, while I looked on from the bank with absolutely not a single shred of envy.
