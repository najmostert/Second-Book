# Chapter 25: Ethiopia 2010

We were on our way to Ethiopia for a birding trip. Ethiopia is in the Horn of Africa, a rugged, landlocked country split by the Great Rift Valley. It’s also the oldest country in Africa and the second most populated  in Africa, with a population of over 106 million people. Ethiopia is also the only African country that has never been colonised. All these thoughts were swirling around my head as we prepared to leave for this ancient place.

Our flight to Addis Ababa was delayed. Once on board Tim and Col changed seats, which necessitated intricate slow-motion ballet-like movements to stand up while holding several items with one hand, a low crouch and several forward and backward pliés as someone walked past in the narrow passageway. Sod’s law dictated that the moment you needed to move out of your seat, a number of passengers and crew would have a pressing need to walk past your seat and crouched body.

Ethiopian Airways has a good reputation and I took comfort in their recent safety awards. I made sure that this was not sullied or fate tempted in any way by a lack of concentration so watched safety talk intently. I was also trying not to notice the patchwork on the wing or the mismatched cushions and décor — the usual tell-tale signs of neglect.

I watched an action movie with Bruce Willis sporting a healthy head of hair. Some time during the flight our shoes got mixed up. Tim removed his as soon as he boarded and soon after Col and I followed suit. This made for an interesting shoe-shuffle using our respective toes on landing. Col’s feet ventured into my space, his toes mimicking little animated sausages grasping for anything that felt like a shoe. I swung my left foot under Tim’s space and located a shoe with my big toe. Then we tried to squeeze excessively swollen feet into our respective shoes.

For a change we were going on an organised birding trip. Earlier we had met Keith, our tall, dark-haired guide, in Joburg. We reunited in Addis Ababa and he took us to the first of many queues that we needed to stand in before we could even think of leaving the airport. Each official was relaxed and in no particular hurry despite the long queues and late hour. This contrasted sharply with our general state of weariness. Whilst we wanted the entire process over and done with, Mr Bima wasn’t in any hurry. He opened a passport and stared at it for a few minutes. His lips were moving silently so I assumed he was reading the information contained within. He thumbed a few pages then paused, turned to another man in uniform who had emerged from somewhere dark and engaged in a lengthy conversation. He returned to my passport while I shuffled from foot to foot with impatience. He was frowning as if unsure what to do next before closing the passport and reaching sloth-like for an empty pad of duplicate visas. He licked his pencil (yes, he had a short HB pencil in his sloth hand) and wrote laboriously in upper case. A week later we were ushered into the main airport area and stood in yet another queue that snaked all the way to the double doors. I lifted my backpack strap to relieve the pressure from my shoulders and tried not to scream. Weary travellers shuffled slowly forward in ten-minute intervals. Col asked me to leave the line and change some rands to dollars so I took myself reluctantly over to yet another queue and waited my turn.

I later shuffled back to the other queue with a pocket full of birr notes. Col and Tim didn’t appear to have moved. We then queued for our luggage before the passport control queue. The airport was huge and these extra queues were not close by. At last we were outside shaking hands with our tour driver and within ten minutes we were at our hotel. Addis was a blur of colourful activity from the car window but it was too dark to see anything clearly. In the reception area we were introduced to some of the tour group: Ted and Alice, and Frazer; a tall blonde-haired man with a firm handshake and another guide from South Africa.

Despite our exhaustion we headed downstairs to the lounge to meet the rest of the group. Frazer led the introductions. Aside from the aforementioned Ted and Alice from the Yoo Ess Ay, there were Bill and Ruth also from the States, Katie, a young dark-haired girl on her own from Joburg, Roger, a plump middle-aged man from Cape Town and finally John, a sprightly eighty-six-year-old from the US. Without much time to form much of an opinion — we seemed to be the youngest, bar Katie and the guides. Tim wasn’t delighted to have no one his age, poor lad. Frazer told us we would need to be up early for Awasa. After a few hours of light sleep, we walked down at the appointed hour for breakfast.

The hotel halls were quiet and it was still dark. I found a round table set with coffee, tea and milk and poured a brew. Slowly the others started to arrive. Keith bounced in cheerfully and handed us each a bird checklist that covered the duration of the tour. 
We were divided between four Toyota 4x4s. Col sat in front with Gurner, our driver, while Tim and I claimed the back before setting off in convoy towards Lake Awasa.

I watched a pale sun rise like an undercooked egg as we hurtled in the chilly morning towards the lake and the first of many bird sightings. Both Col and I had forgotten to pack our warm jackets so we were in for a cold trip in the mountains. We arrived at Lake Hawassa or Awasa; an endorheic basin (a closed body of water — river system that doesn’t flow to the ocean) located in the Main Ethiopian Rift south of Addis Ababa. As soon as we stepped out of our respective vehicles, there was a rush to the water’s edge and binoculars were immediately raised in unison. Tim and I shared a smile. Whilst we both enjoy looking at colourful or interesting birds, we were not in this category of enthusiastic twitchers. However we had our own binoculars and slowly raised them towards a couple of yellow-billed egrets. There was a slight mist over the dark water and it was quite beautiful in the early morning light.

Our driver headed to the Crater Lake Hotel, a great place for breakfast with its view of the caldera, a large depression created by a volcano’s collapse which fills with water (usually rain). Stone steps led down to a wide verandah overlooking the lake where the birders immediately raised their binoculars again. I explored a small but beautiful garden en route to the loo. There was brightly hued bougainvillea, purple lilies and marigold-lined paths. The group walked around to the other side of the lake to Koka Dam where we spent the next two hours. Wandering down a long tree-lined road that had a crumbling old stone wall down the length of it, we followed Keith and Frazer who were armed with huge telescopes and whenever they spotted something interesting, which was every ten minutes, they stopped and set up their ‘scopes so we could all have a look.

It was a very pleasant walk down to the lake. Land Cruisers drove slowly behind us and parked under a row of trees. Children hung around everywhere, wearing ragged clothes and huge smiles under large pleading eyes. Most of them must be hungry, I thought, smiling down at two who were walking with me. As we walked they held out their hands and said,

‘Give T-shirt!’ or ‘Give pen!’

I had neither to give, unfortunately.

We found a beautiful pygmy kingfisher on the rocks as well as numerous other water birds. The birders were happy and spent a while gazing at them and recording their find. Some local women were washing their clothes in the lake. I was pleased when someone spotted a few grivet monkeys in one of the trees. They are usually found near rivers and lakes and have a large tuft of white sideburns on their grey faces.
They drove from the lake, stopping at intervals to watch greater flamingos, pelicans and hundreds of marabou stork — not the prettiest of the stork family. Wherever we stopped, we found ourselves surrounded by children holding out their hands saying for some reason, ‘You, you, you!’ Perhaps it was the only English word they knew. Some were quite forceful; I  saw Alice had a particularly insistent child following her, poking and prodding her with a grubby finger. It was both sad and annoying. We set off again, this time aiming for the Shibella Hotel which sits some 4,000 feet above sea level on the shores of Lake Awasa. It had a beautiful garden with huge wild fig trees and flower-lined pathways. That afternoon Tim and I walked down to the spa baths situated close to the hotel and sat on a stone step watching a double-toothed barbet darting around a fig tree. The steamy mineral-rich water poured down a huge rock into the pool below.

After supper, the group sat at the large wooden table to call out birds they’d seen before ticking them off on the checklist we were given. I was pleased to add the double-toothed barbet to my checklist.

The Land Cruisers stopped briefly when the driver saw a huge olive baboon squatting near the roadside and later grivet and a few colobus monkeys. These have long shaggy coats of black and white and a long bushy white tail. We were staying at the Wondo Genet Hotel high in the mountains for the evening. It was much colder being so high up.

After dressing warmly, we set off for the summit of the Bale Mountains park, driving steadily up the mountain. Despite it being a national park, there were many locals wandering the side of the road with cattle. Some carried wood on their heads (the locals, that is) and I was wondering where they had chopped the wood as it seemed quite open scrubland. We spotted a few warthogs walking with their short tails sticking straight up then suddenly, on one of the bird stops, Ruth called out,

‘Wolf! Wolf! Look over there!’

We swivelled our heads as one towards a lone Abyssinian red wolf, endemic to the park, characterised by its brownish-red fur, long pointed snout and distinctive white markings on its chin and under its eyes. It is a vulnerable species, with only a few hundred individuals remaining in a few areas. A beautiful animal with its russet pelt and white-stockinged legs. This route led through a lobelia-covered area that bore a resemblance to the foothills of Kilimanjaro. As the drive continued, we had another, much better sighting of the wolf, who strolled past the Land Rover and continued until he disappeared over a hill, at which point we  carried on to a marshy area filled with many small bodies of water. This served as the lunch spot. The drivers set out camp chairs and a collapsible wooden table. Soon everyone was seated in a row facing the dusty road eating egg sandwiches and drinking hot coffee. Due to the biting cold at this altitude, a three-hour walk provided an opportunity to keep warm. A bleached skull of a giant mole-rat, complete with long incisors, lay on the ground, waiting to be discovered. I bent down to pick it up. Col quickly reminded me I couldn’t take it. I’m known for coming home with interesting specimens of roadkill which I display under a large glass-topped table I have at home. Tim and I played a few games of Ethiopian snooker with some locals in the afternoon which involved throwing balls across the table to hit strategically placed magnetic pins.

There was a mild squabble over who got which Toyota to sit in before we were off into the purple dawn and ascended the Bale Mountains onto the Sanetti Plateau. This is the highest part of the mountain as the name implies and apparently the highest road in Africa. We were in Afro-alpine mountain territory now, characterised by huge lobelias set on tussock grasslands. Large pools of water dotted around the vast empty land. The convoy dropped down from the Sanetti to the Harenna Forest where it felt warmer. The views of the mountain and plateau from this vantage point were truly awe-inspiring. Colossal trees dominated the landscape, draped in long, pod-like blossoms that dangled like vibrant ornaments in a palette ranging from sun-drenched yellow to deep scarlet. It was a place of absolute abundance, teeming with birds and butterflies — a living, breathing forest. To stretch our legs, we set off at a leisurely pace down the dusty track. The vehicles trailed slowly behind, a quiet procession in our wake, as the lush woodland eventually thinned out, surrendering to the parched, sun-baked sprawl of the thorny savanna. After stopping at a very dry wadi on the Genale River, we were looking for Prince Ruspoli’s turaco, named after an Italian prince who was trampled by an elephant in the park in the 1890s. keith found one sitting in a tree unaware of its interest to us while cameras whirred or clicked and binoculars were lifted. We headed on to Negele for the night and arrived at Anaa Hotel for what was to become the low part of the tour — for me anyway.

There was not much to see in the dark as we alighted stiff-legged from the various vehicles into a still, humid air. Frazer told us there were only three double rooms and these were immediately snapped up by the two American couples and John. This left single rooms for the rest of us. Mine was a corner room and as I opened the door and peered into the gloom, I sensed rather than saw some wildlife move across the room. Heart beating fast, I fumbled for the light switch and recoiled when the room was revealed. Bright, shiny green paint (the colour that is always sold on special because it’s so awful) peeled across a slimy damp wall. A single bed lay hunched up against the corner wall in an attempt to distance itself from the horrors within. Wall sockets lay propped alongside the wall. The cement floor was uneven and had a multitude of suspiciously sanguine stains. My eyes followed some movement against the wall and I found a collection of large cockroaches scuttling along its length. I let out a muffled scream and dropped my case on the cold cement floor, sending another cockroach scurrying up the shiny wall. I ventured closer to the bed and threw back the single sheet, sending another cockroach scuttling down the bed. The ceiling was black with mould and a strong smell of stale urine led me to the ‘en-suite’ toilet. I walked slowly and with great trepidation towards its doorless interior to peer in and nearly choked when I found a cracked toilet bowl with no seat, crawling with cockroaches and suspicious stains. My eyes travelled to the ‘shower’ and I let out a little yelp. There was a solid turd lying malevolently over the drainage area. A forlorn tap dripped over a crumbling wall thick with green algae.

‘My God, I can’t sleep here!’ I muttered and retreated to Col’s room next door. I found him sitting on his bed cleaning his camera.

‘How’s your room?’ he asked innocently.

I let out a torrent of strong words interspersed with moans. He looked surprised.

‘I know it’s not five-star but it’s OK,’ he said, removing a small cockroach that was crawling across his cheek.

I had no words and went to see how Tim was faring. He was collecting his things and leaving his room as I arrived.

‘I’m not staying here,’ he told me firmly. ‘There were five roaches in my bed. I’ve asked Frazer if I can sleep in the back of the Toyota,’ he continued, hoisting his bag further up his shoulder.

I returned forlornly to my hovel and decided I couldn’t sleep on the dirty sheet so retrieved a thin sleeping-bag liner that I fortuitously brought and climbed into it, placing my jacket over the hard nylon pillow the colour of old vomit. My eyes took in the huge, cracked mirror that was tilted at a dangerous angle above my bed. I tried to sleep but sat up a few hours later with the sound of loud singing then lay down again miserably. I was awake now and it was stiflingly hot in the room. I felt something crawling on the pillow and flung it away. When dawn broke I used half a bottle of drinking water to wash my face and dressed quickly, eyes on the lookout for more of the flat brown, oval-shaped monsters.

The day was a blur of driving over dusty dry areas and spiky thorn bushes and not many birds. I heard we were to spend another night in ‘hotel cockroach’ and felt resigned to just suck it up and make the best of it.

Keith asked us to meet in the ‘dining room’ for supper at a local tavern attached to the other side of the ‘hotel’. Again it was full of peeling paint and a foetid damp atmosphere prevailed. A few locals were drinking what might be their seventh beer. Everyone was sitting on a rickety plastic chair around a wooden table covered with a dirty plastic tablecloth with a tired-looking pineapple motif. Frazer said he’d go in search of a menu. I wasn’t holding my breath so when he returned and said we had a choice of wet or dry tibs I hardly registered surprise. Tibs is a popular dish there; a type of stir-fry meat in a rich spicy sauce. Meat is lamb, beef or chicken cooked dry in small pieces till almost overcooked and served with injera, a sour, fermented bread made with teff flour, with a slightly spongy texture. It’s used as both a plate and a utensil for the stew. I don’t like the texture of injera personally with its sour flavour and spongy consistency. The meat was so overcooked as to appear like biltong (beef jerky). In the end I only ate half a piece of stale bread with a glass of water. The checklist took place and by 9:30 we were all heading off to various forms of torture for the night. I set the small alarm clock that Ruth kindly lent me for 3:30 a.m. and decided to just lie on top of the sleeping-bag liner as it was too hot and stuffy. I had forgotten to switch off the light so jumped off the bed again. It didn’t help because there was a light right outside my window. The sound of drunken revelry emanated from the tavern next door and an hour later I was still staring miserably up at the cracked ceiling trying not to listen to the scurry of cockroaches. Suddenly a face peered in at me from the window and I jumped in fright.

‘Go away!’ I shouted and there was a drunken giggle in response.

The leering face disappeared but throughout the night, more faces appeared in various stages of inebriation. It was only during the early hours of the morning that I dozed off only to be woken by the shrill sound of the alarm what felt like an hour later. I peered groggily at the hands and saw it was 3:30 so reluctantly got up and flung on the light, scattering cockroaches in all directions. I dressed quickly and went to wake the lads. Judging from their faces I’d say they hadn’t slept much either. We chose our vehicle sharing with Keith and Katie this trip.
 The Liben Plains, a large, flat area known for its unique ecosystem and the critically endangered Liben (or Sidamo) lark, was our first stop and it was for this bird that we were in the area. A day of hot, dusty, dry walks through thorn scrub ensued with stifling slowness. The bushes had very sharp thorns and none of us escaped a good snagging on our clothes and arms and legs. A few anubis baboons and a Pringle’s puffback were spotted as we arrived back at the side of the dusty road where the drivers had set up a makeshift dining area to eat sandwiches and drink hot reviving coffee. Tim and I forwent the second round of thorn-bush bashing and waited in the car.

Ruth’s alarm clock awakened me at 3:30 again. I took a quick cold shower in Col’s room — at least it didn’t have a petrified turd in it. The three of us shared a vehicle and as we set off I was thinking that wherever we ended up next it could never be as bad as this place had been. The drive towards the Kenya border was long and then along the Dawa River on a very bumpy potholed road. The drivers moved away from the river to a drier place and we walked along the pale, beige dusty road towards a long caravan of dromedary camels led by three turban-toting, long white-garmented men. I shouted a few ‘Salamaas’ before noticing that all the men had AK-47s slung over their shoulders. Despite their dangerous demeanour they seemed friendly and returned my greeting. As dusk smeared the sky with streaks of pink and yellow, we arrived at our next accommodation in Yabello. I climbed stiffly out of the vehicle and gazed hopefully towards the garden. A huge open verandah decorated with tables, chairs and plants. I was reassured that this wouldn’t be another insect-ridden hell-hole. Rows of flowering bushes edged the cement floor and gorgeous smells rose from the moonflowers. We had a room each and what a difference a soft, clean bug-free bed and shower sans fossilised stool specimens make.

A few donkey-led tuk-tuks rode by and colourfully dressed women carried huge loads on their backs and heads. Ethiopian features are distinct from other African people with their prominent cheekbones, almond-shaped eyes, defined brows, curly hair, with common features that include narrow noses and high foreheads. This is due to their mix of sub-Saharan and Middle Eastern origins.

The birders returned at lunchtime and sat at the long table outside for lunch. Frazer arrived looking frazzled. His canvas bag containing over $1,000 had been stolen. He wasn’t sure when or where but had just discovered it was missing now. He shrugged his shoulders pragmatically saying it was just paper but the mood had changed and people clutched their valuables more tightly to their chests. A policeman arrived to ‘investigate’ the disappearance. He wore a white hat and dark shades and a cellphone was permanently glued to his ear. He didn’t exude confidence.

Later we drove in convoy to an open plain with numerous red termite mounds. It was still fairly hot and humid and dark clouds were gathering. Ruth spotted a yellow butterfly and pointed it out to Col who immediately chased after it and swiped it into his hat. He was pleased with this catch and we didn’t see him again till after four. Tim and I joined Roger for a walk. Tim had perked up and was showing some interest in the birds so I pointed out the beautiful golden-breasted starling to him. It started to rain halfway down the road; fat drops of water splashed down on the dry sandy road soaking us to the skin and cooling us down so no one minded too much. Later we arranged ourselves between two cars. Keith, Tim and myself climbed onto the roof rack with the rest inside then set off into the dusk clinging on tightly, especially when negotiating a deep donga (ditch). The air was fresh after the rain and smelled like wild sage.

 I was shat on by an Abyssinian starling while sitting outside waiting for breakfast. I wiped away the faecal blob and took this as a lucky omen; we were heading off to Lake Langano, situated near Addis Ababa. We’d travelled in a loop over the last few weeks. Our driver was Gurmah. A pleasant fellow with a thin face and curly dark hair and we chatted about all things Ethiopian. The scenery changed from acacia savannah, grassy plains that were so neat and trim one would think them mown by a giant lawnmower, to dark, dense forest.

Gurnah played some local music as well as a few western songs as I watched locals threshing wheat, chopping wood, carrying huge bags of corn and wheat and many thick bundles of palm-covered khat as it’s known locally. Khat is typically chewed like tobacco, then retained in the cheek and chewed intermittently to release the active drug, which produces a stimulant-like effect. We had a short stop in Dila for lunch at a hot, dusty, noisy town in the south. I watched two Gedeo women with beautiful intricately woven hair and delicate features from the car window. The hotel in Langano was set amid vast trees close to the lake. Bushes of colourful bougainvillea brightened up the area and birds flittered around the lake water, the colour of tea.

Tim and I joined the birders for a bird walk in the hotel grounds set against huge granite rocks. A golden jackal lurked under a huge tree before a mystical scene unfolded: two white horses galloped through smoky-coloured mist through the early morning light. I hired one of the paddle boats so Tim and I could pedal furiously to the centre of the lake.

At the end of the garden, the two white horses we’d seen earlier that morning appeared ghostlike into view and I figured we could take them out for a ride, coaxing them into a slow trot around the garden periphery.

 The Gibe River is the largest tributary of the Omo River. The sun made a watery appearance as we stopped for breakfast on the side of the road . Aside from a few bird stops we pressed on until the summit of the gorge. It had been exceedingly hot and dry and the few scrawny thorn trees didn’t provide enough shade. A  narrow river nearby held a few hippo snorting and cavorting in its centre. The ‘short’ walk turned out to be a long, hot, dusty, snake-infested upward climb in 38°C heat before heading afterwards straight off towards the river to watch a small group of people harvesting huge spinach leaves.

From what I could see of the lodge when we arrived in the dark, it was a huge tree-lined building set behind walled security. The dining room was large with oil paintings along the vast walls, depicting local scenery. My lasagne was huge — we counted seventeen layers but dessert was a bit of a fiasco. Four of the group wanted fruit salad with ice cream not cream, Col and I wanted cassata and Tim asked for a crème brûlée. The waiter didn’t understand the ice cream replacement for the cream. He went to ask the chef if this would be OK and returned with the sad news that there was no ice cream or cassata so I changed my order to crème brûlée. He said the others might have one bowl of ice cream and one bowl of fruit salad as they ‘can’t replace the cream’ then he disappeared and returned and said there was no ice cream or cream but there was cassata available so I changed back to cassata again and he said it came as only chocolate or vanilla so I changed back to the brûlée. We were all thoroughly confused by now as to what was available and what was not. When the dessert arrived we craned our necks to see what there was. Two separate bowls of fruit salad and ice cream which they’d said they didn’t have. The crème brûlée was frozen custard and the ice cream, frozen gelato. We tucked in with a bit of this and that and asked for coffee (with some trepidation it must be said). After the bird checklist was done Alice said she was fed up with travelling every day and not seeing anything of the people and towns. I was inclined to agree with her. It’s great to see beautiful birds but to avoid any of the local people and culture seems ludicrous. 
After breakfast I wandered around the grounds with my camera, capturing the antics of the grivet monkeys. There was an abundance of birdlife, monkeys and a large spa pool. It was a pity we couldn’t enjoy any of it as we were leaving straight after breakfast. We set off for Awash by seven. The land became drier and more arid as we drove down the more lush escarpment towards Awash. At a breakfast stop we had huge pyramid-sized sandwiches with spice tea.

After a couple of hours we stopped for another long, hot walkabout and passed more goat herds carrying AK-47s. Col asked if he could take a photo and he refused. It’s never good to argue with a man holding a gun so we carried on. The others were in search of some LBJ so I walked around the lake photographing heron, flamingos and a young saddle-billed stork. After another two hours of travel we arrived at the Awash National Park and set off early for a bird walk along the dusty park roads. We came across scrub hares, a beisa oryx, Soemmerring’s gazelle, lesser kudu and Salt’s dik-dik and a few warthogs paddling in the morning light. 
 Roger was in full flow at supper time with stories on how to attract ‘birds’ (the human variety). Tim was visibly interested. I didnt agree with most of his suggestions but wiely held my tongue. A smell of wood burning from across the room  was quite acrid. The ‘coffee lady’ arrived and seated herself on the floor  setting out her items. She had a small charcoal burner, raw coffee beans, hot water and small glasses arranged on a silver tray. She roasted the beans over the burner in a shallow pan then brought the pan around for everyone to take a sniff of the beans then passed round the coffee glasses and a raffia basket of popcorn. Next water was brought to a boil and the newly roasted beans were added to the hot water. After several minutes she decanted the liquid coffee into a large jug and filled our glasses. The result was strong but aromatic coffee.

We heard that the previous evening’s drive had been cancelled due to a breakdown in relations between the park and herdsmen who drive their cattle through the park every day to graze. Keith told us they were afraid they’d mistake us for game rangers if we drove off the beaten path and most of them were armed as we had seen. Nevertheless a night drive was arranged and Col, Tim, Keith and Dennis piled onto the roof, Alice in the back with Juju, an armed ranger and I in front with Frazer. Keith held onto the huge spotlight as we set off into the velvet darkness. Aside from some spring hare, a nightjar, dik-dik, a Soemmerring’s gazelle and a bat-eared fox, we didn’t see much else.
 Tim and I walked down to the waterfall after breakfast, through dappled shade then back up to the main gates towards a small museum. I phoned the number that was stuck on the door of the museum. Five minutes later a short local man arrived with his young son in tow and opened the door. The museum was more mausoleum with its dusty moth-eaten exhibits some dating from the Italian occupation I suspected. The guide rattled off some information in broken English and we rewarded his efforts with a tip afterwards although we hadn’t understood much.

I was reading my book when a shadow passed over the open door of my room and I looked up to find a large male baboon standing in the doorway watching me with interest and some evil intent in his deep-set eyes. Nervously I got up and shooed him away. He grabbed what turned out to be an empty battery packet and scarpered. As we didn’t have any food in the room he probably wouldn’t return. After supper and the obligatory checklist completed, we repaired to our rooms. Tim told me in passing that he had seen a mouse in my room. Sure enough it was scurrying around the room when I switched on my light. I love mice — in their natural habitat but not anywhere near my bed so I leapt onto the bed and tucked in the mosquito net. Col later coaxed it out and covered the small hole in the door with a pair of underpants.

We left the following day after a quick breakfast of toast and egg. We were in the second vehicle with Bali the ranger and Frazer and stopped for lunch at the Ethiopian Gentleman’s Club which has a beautiful setting overlooking the crater lake. I ordered a ‘hum’ and cheese sandwich but couldn’t eat it as the ‘hum’ was a suspicious burgundy colour. I picked out the slices of cheese and ate that. Our stop for the night was at a small hotel just off the road. Their restaurant menu was amusing and read as follows:

‘Spagati with meet souce’
‘Beef cotelet with French frize’
‘File gulash’
‘Lamp tips’

I opted for the ‘spageti’ with ‘meet souce’, there being no ‘meet’ or ‘frize’ or any number of other items available.

Simien Mountains day meant we'd see the gelada baboons. These primates are restricted to high grassland escarpments in the deep gorges of the central Ethiopian plateau. I scoured the surrounding escarpment when we arrived and thought I could see two in the distance. As we walked down to a flat grassy patch we were rewarded with five gelada sightings; two with magnificent males. They are also known as ‘bleeding-heart’ baboons for their distinctive appearance, including the bright red, hourglass-shaped chest patch and long, flowing golden manes. Geladas are terrestrial primates; they spend most of their time on the ground, grazing on grasses and other vegetation. Lunch was eaten under the trees and consisted of injera with yellow and red lentils and vegetables which tasted delicious. Ten minutes after lunch the ‘mad’ birders set off in 40°C. We continued driving upwards where it became distinctly cooler. Small round grass-roofed huts were surrounded by tall euphorbia. At the summit of Ankober Escarpment I had to leap out of the vehicle suffering from an upset stomach. I grabbed a loo roll and ran as fast as 14,000 feet above sea level would allow (not fast) in search of a bush and squatted behind a large lump of soil. It was so cold I could barely pull up my drawers fast enough with frozen fingers before a truck passed and several pairs of eyes swivelled down to where I was crouching with my trousers round my ankles. I’m going to design an inflatable skirt; one that quickly puffs out when a button is pressed so that these awful toilet stops have a modicum of decorum. Watch this space!

Tim, Col and I walked over to the edge of the vast escarpment with the beautiful valley below. It was breathtaking and we could see as far away as the lake at Awash where we’d been earlier in the week. We spotted a small troop of geladas and sat watching them for a while. Later the hardcore birders joined us and we spent a pleasant hour watching their antics (the geladas not the birders though that’s debatable).

Later we arrived at Hotel Ghiron in Addis and checked in. We had just enough time to wash our faces before meeting in the lobby. Ruth asked if we could do some shopping so Ghurma took us to a real tourist trap where we wandered through overcrowded stalls selling ancient silver Coptic crosses, rings, and rolls of cloth. Late that night we drove to the Dimma Cultural Restaurant for some entertainment. The venue itself was a wide open room with many tables and chairs and a large raised stage against the far wall. A bar lay against the side wall and another smaller room with easy chairs. The meal was a huge buffet with every imaginable style of Ethiopian meal. We piled our plates and sat down to watch some local dancers followed by a set of musicians playing traditional music. The dancers were wearing gorgeously coloured dresses and each dancer held a red umbrella. As the evening drew to a close, Keith handed each of us a plastic bag containing brochures of forthcoming tours, a locally made fringed top, a pen and some roasted coffee beans. On our last morning we woke very early to two different sounds: one was the call to prayer and the other, the guttural sound of lions roaring from the gardens of the presidential residence, formerly Haile Selassie’s residence. My dad met him once at a function in Uganda in the sixties and said he had a firm handshake.  A few hours later we began the tortuous trip back to Cape Town by plane.
