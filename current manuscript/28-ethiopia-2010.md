# Chapter 25: ETHIOPIA 2010

We’re on our way to Ethiopia for a birding trip. Ethiopia is in the Horn of Africa. It’s a rugged, landlocked country split by the Great Rift Valley. It’s also the Oldest in Africa and the second most populated country in Africa with a population of over 106 million people. Ethiopia is also the only African country that has never been colonised. All these thoughts are swirling around my head as we prepare to leave for this ancient place. Our flight to Addis Ababa is delayed. Once on board Tim and Col change seats which necessitates intricate slow-motion ballet-like movements to stand up while holding several items with one hand, a low crouch and several forward and backward pliés as someone walks past in the narrow passageway. Sod dictates that the moment you need to move out of your seat, a number of passengers and crew will decide at that precise moment, a pressing need to walk past your seat and crouched body. Ethiopian Airways has a good reputation and I take comfort in their recent safety awards. I make sure that this is not sullied or fate tempted in any way by a lack in concentration. Hence the safety talk is watched intently by me despite having watched a good thousand of these talks over the years. I always feel, should an unfortunate incident interfere with the crew’s safety talk, I would be able to confidently step up and offer my services. I am also at the same time trying not to notice the patchwork on the wing or the mismatched cushions and decor-the usual tell-tale signs of neglect. I watch an action movie with Bruce Willis sporting a healthy head of hair. Some time during the flight our shoes get mixed up. Tim removed his as soon as he boarded and soon after Col and I follow suit. This makes for an interesting shoe-shuffle using our respective toes on landing. Col’s feet venture into my space and his toes mimic little animated sausages grasping for anything that feels like a shoe. I swing my left foot under Tim’s space and locate a shoe with my big toe. Then we try to squeeze excessively swollen feet into our respective shoes. ...reindeer meat and winter root vegetables. It was highly atmospheric to sit inside a timber building with over six centuries of history, watching the logs crackle in the hearth while our bodies slowly thawed from the day's outdoor excursions. Earlier, we had met Keith, our tall, dark-haired guide, in Joburg. We reunited in Addis Ababa, and he took us to the first of many queues that we had to stand in before we could even think of leaving the airport. Each official was relaxed and in no particular hurry despite the long lines. This contrasted sharply with our general state of weariness. Whilst we wanted the entire process over and done with, Mr Bima wasn’t in any hurry. He opened a passport and stared at it for a few minutes, his lips moving silently, so I assumed he was reading the information contained within. He thumbed a few pages, paused, turned to another man in uniform who had emerged from somewhere dark, and engaged in a lengthy conversation. He eventually returned to my passport while I shuffled from foot to foot with impatience. He was frowning as if unsure what to do next, then closed the booklet and reached sloth-like for an empty pad of duplicate visas. He licked his pencil—yes, he had a short HB pencil in his sloth hand—and wrote laboriously in upper case. What felt like a week later, we were ushered into the main airport area, only to stand in yet another queue that snaked all the way to the double doors. I lifted my backpack strap to relieve the pressure from my shoulders and tried not to scream. Weary travellers shuffled slowly forward in ten-minute intervals. Col asked me to leave the line and change some Rands to Dollars, so I took myself reluctantly over to yet another queue and waited my turn. I later shuffled back to the original line with a pocket full of Birr notes; Col and Tim did not appear to have moved a single inch. We then queued for our luggage, followed by the passport control queue. The airport was huge, and these extra queues were by no means close by. At last, we were outside shaking hands with our tour driver, and within ten minutes, we arrived at our hotel. Addis was a blur of colourful activity from the car window, but it was too dark to see anything clearly. In the reception area, we were introduced to some of the tour group: Ted and Alice, and Frazer—a tall, blonde-haired man with a firm handshake who was another specialist guide from South Africa. Despite our profound exhaustion, we had to walk downstairs to the lounge to meet the rest of the group. Frazer led the introductions. Aside from the aforementioned Ted and Alice from the USA, there were Bill and Ruth, also from the States; Katie, a young, dark-haired girl on her own from Joburg; Roger, a plump, middle-aged man from Cape Town; and finally John, a sprightly eighty-six-year-old from the US. Without much time to form an initial opinion, we seemed to be the youngest, bar Katie and the guides. Tim wasn’t delighted to have no one his age, poor lad. Frazer told us we would need to be up early for Awasa. In our triple-bedded room, we washed and prepared for bed. 

Endorheic Basins and the Crater Lake 

After a few hours of light sleep, we walked down at the appointed hour for breakfast. The hotel halls were quiet and it was still pitch dark. I found a round table set with coffee, tea, and milk, and poured a brew. Slowly, the others started to arrive. Keith bounced in and handed us each a bird checklist that covered the duration of the tour. We were divided between four Toyota 4x4s. Col sat in front with Gurner, our driver, while Tim and I claimed the back seat, and we set off in convoy towards Lake Awasa. I watched a pale sun rise like an undercooked egg as we hurtled in the chilly morning towards the lake and the first of many bird sightings. Both Col and I had forgotten to pack our warm jackets, so we were in for a cold trip in the mountains. We arrived at Lake Hawassa, which is an endorheic basin located in the Main Ethiopian Rift south of Addis Ababa. An endorheic basin is a closed drainage basin that retains water and allows no external outflow to external bodies, meaning its river system does not flow to the ocean but balances entirely through internal evaporation or seepage. As soon as we stepped out of our respective vehicles, there was a mad rush to the water’s edge, and binoculars were immediately raised in unison. Tim and I shared a smile. Whilst we both enjoy looking at colourful or interesting birds, we were definitely not in this category of enthusiastic twitchers. However, we each had our own binoculars and slowly raised them towards a couple of yellow-billed egrets. There was a slight mist over the dark water, looking quite beautiful in the early morning light. Our driver headed to the Crater Lake Hotel, which was a great place for breakfast with its view of the caldera. A caldera is a large, cauldron-like volcanic depression created by the structural collapse of a volcano's magma chamber during an eruption, which subsequently fills with water over centuries from rainfall and local runoff. Stone steps led down to a wide verandah overlooking the lake, where the birders immediately lifted up their binoculars once more. I explored a small but beautiful garden en route to the loo, navigating past brightly hued bougainvillea, purple lilies, and marigold-lined paths. 

Pygmy Kingfishers and Grivet White Sideburns 

The group walked around to the other side of the lake to Koka Dam, where we spent the next two hours looking at various birds. Wandering down a long, tree-lined road that had a crumbling old stone wall down the length of it, we followed Keith and Frazer. Both were armed with huge telescopes on tripods, and whenever they spotted something interesting—which was every ten minutes —they stopped and set up their scopes so we could all have a look. It was a very pleasant walk down to the lake. Our Land Cruisers drove slowly behind us, a quiet procession in our wake, before parking under some trees. I joined the rest of the group on a steep incline. There were local children everywhere, wearing ragged clothes and huge smiles under large, pleading eyes. Most of them must be hungry, I thought, smiling down at two who were walking with me. As we walked, they held out their hands and said, ‘Give T-shirt!’ or ‘Give pen!’ I had neither to give, unfortunately. On the rocks, we found a beautiful African pygmy kingfisher, as well as numerous other water birds. The birders were happy, spending a while gazing at them and recording their find. Some local women were washing their clothes in the lake. I was pleased when someone spotted a few grivet monkeys in one of the trees. Grivet monkeys (Chlorocebus aethiops) are primates usually found near rivers and lakes, easily identified by the large, prominent tufts of white facial sideburns contrasting against their grey faces. Later, we drove to a roadside restaurant for lunch. Afterwards, we continued our journey from the lake, stopping at intervals to watch greater flamingos, pelicans, and hundreds of marabou storks—by no means the prettiest of the stork family. Wherever we stopped, we found ourselves surrounded by children holding out their hands and saying, for some reason, ‘You, you, you!’ Perhaps it was the only English word they knew. Some were quite forceful, and as I turned round, I saw Alice had a particularly insistent child following her, poking and prodding her with a grubby finger. It was both sad and annoying. We set off again, this time aiming for the Shibella Hotel, which sits some 4,000 feet above sea level on the shores of Lake Awasa. It had a beautiful garden with huge wild fig trees and flower-lined pathways. This afternoon, Tim and I walked down to the spa baths situated close to the hotel and sat on a stone step watching a double-toothed barbet darting around a fig tree. Tim wanted to have a swim in the warm spa but waited too long for the shower to empty. The steamy, mineral-rich water poured down a huge rock into the pool below. After supper, the group sat at the large wooden table to call out the birds they had seen, which were then ticked off on our checklist sheets. I was pleased to add the double-toothed barbet to my personal list. 

The Sanetti Plateau and the Red Wolf 

The Land Cruisers stopped briefly when the driver saw a huge olive baboon squatting near the roadside, and later on, we logged more grivets and a few black-and-white colobus monkeys. Colobus monkeys are stunning primates possessing long, shaggy coats of black and white fur, complemented by an incredibly long, bushy white tail plume. We stayed at the Wondo Genet Hotel, high in the mountains for the evening. It was much colder being so high up. After dressing warmly, we set off for the summit of the Bale Mountains National Park, driving steadily up the mountain pass. Despite it being a national park, there were many locals wandering the side of the road with cattle. Some carried wood on their heads, and I wondered where on earth they had chopped it from, as the surrounding terrain seemed to be quite open, sparse scrubland. We spotted a few warthogs walking with their short tails sticking straight up, when suddenly, during one of our regular bird stops, Ruth called out, ‘Wolf! Wolf! Look over there!’ We swivelled our heads as one towards a lone Abyssinian red wolf, endemic to the park. The Abyssinian red wolf (or Ethiopian wolf) is characterised by its beautiful, bright brownish-red fur, a long pointed snout, and distinctive white markings on its chin and under its eyes. It is currently one of the most critically endangered canid species on Earth, with only a few hundred individuals remaining in a few isolated mountain pockets. It was a truly beautiful animal with its russet pelt and white, stockinged legs. Our route led through a lobelia-covered area that bore an incredible resemblance to the foothills of Mount Kilimanjaro. As the drive continued, we enjoyed another, much better sighting of the wolf, who strolled past our Land Cruiser's chassis before disappearing over a hill, at which point our journey carried on to a marshy area filled with many small alpine bodies of water. This served as our lunch spot. The drivers set out camp chairs and a collapsible wooden table, and soon everyone was seated in a long row facing the dusty road, eating egg sandwiches and drinking hot coffee. Due to the biting cold at this altitude, a three-hour walk provided an opportunity to keep warm while favouring our birdwatching. A bleached skull of a giant mole-rat, complete with its long, curved incisors, lay on the dirt waiting to be discovered. I bent down to pick it up, but Col quickly reminded me I couldn't legally take it out of the country. I am notoriously known back home for returning from holidays with interesting specimens of roadkill, which I proudly display under a large glass-topped table designed for that specific purpose. Later that afternoon, Tim and I played a few games of ‘Ethiopian Snooker’ with some local lads at a kiosk, which involved throwing balls across a board to hit strategically placed magnetic pins. There was a mild squabble over who got which Toyota vehicle to sit in before we accelerated off into the purple dawn, ascending the Bale Mountains onto the famous Sanetti Plateau. This is the highest part of the Bale range and represents the highest all-weather road in Africa. We were in true Afro-alpine mountain territory now. The Afro-alpine ecosystem at this high altitude is characteristically dominated by giant lobelia plants rising out of thick tussock grasslands and rocky alpine mosses. There were more of those large pools of water dotted around the vast, empty land. Our convoy eventually dropped down from the heights of the Sanetti into the dense Harenna Forest, where the air felt instantly warmer. The views of the mountain gorges and the plateau from this high vantage point were truly awe-inspiring. Colossal trees dominated the landscape, draped in long, pod-like blossoms that dangled like vibrant ornaments in a natural palette ranging from sun-drenched yellow to deep scarlet. It was a place of absolute abundance, teeming with birds and butterflies—a living, breathing forest. To stretch our stiff limbs, we set off at a leisurely pace down the dusty track on foot. The vehicles trailed slowly behind us, a quiet procession in our wake, as the lush woodland eventually thinned out, surrendering to the parched, sun-baked sprawl of the thorny savanna plains. After stopping at an incredibly dry wadi creek bed on the Genale River, we began searching the branches for Prince Ruspoli’s turaco. The rare bird was historically named after an Italian explorer, Prince Ruspoli, who had tragically been trampled to death by a wild elephant in the region back in the 1890s. One of the birders successfully located a specimen sitting quietly in a tree, entirely unaware of its celebrity status, while cameras whirred and clicked and binoculars were lifted in unison. We headed onwards towards the town of Negele for the night, arriving at the Ana Hotel for what was to become the absolute lowest point of the entire tour—for me anyway. 

The Sanguine Stains of Hotel Hovel 

There was not much to see in the dark as we alighted stiff-legged from the various vehicles into the still, humid night air. Frazer stepped forward to inform the group that the establishment possessed only three double rooms, and these vacancies were immediately snapped up by the two American couples and elderly John. This arrangement left basic single rooms for the remainder of the travellers. Mine was a corner room, and the moment I opened the wooden door and peered into the deep gloom, I sensed, rather than saw, that some form of local wildlife had just moved rapidly across the floorboards. Heart beating fast, I fumbled along the plaster for the light switch and recoiled in absolute horror when the bedroom was revealed. Bright, shiny green paint—the specific awful colour that is always sold on special because no one wants it—was peeling across a slimy, damp wall structure. A single bed lay hunched up against the corner wall, looking as if it were actively attempting to distance itself from the horrors within. Exposed wall electrical sockets lay propped loosely alongside the skirting. The cement floor was uneven and featured a multitude of suspiciously sanguine, blood-coloured stains. My eyes followed a sudden movement against the wall, and I located a massive collection of large cockroaches scuttling along its length. I let out a muffled scream and dropped my suitcase heavily onto the cold cement floor, which sent another cockroach scurrying up the shiny green wall. I ventured closer to the mattress and threw back the single sheet, instantly sending another cockroach scuttling down the bed frame. The ceiling was black with thick mould, and a strong, pungent smell of stale urine led my boots towards the ‘en-suite’ toilet cubicle. I walked slowly and with great trepidation towards its doorless interior to peer inside. I nearly choke when my light found a cracked porcelain toilet bowl with absolutely no seat, crawling with dozens of cockroaches and covered in suspicious stains. My eyes travelled to the floor shower drainage area, and I let out a little yelp. There was a solid, human turd lying malevolently straight over the drainage grid. A forlorn tap dripped slowly over a crumbling wall thick with green algae. ‘My God, I can’t sleep here!’ I muttered aloud, and retreated straight to Col’s room next door. I found him sitting on his bed, calmly cleaning his camera lenses. ‘How’s your room?’ he asked innocently. I let out a torrent of strong words interspersed with groans. He looked thoroughly surprised. ‘I know it’s not five-star, but it’s OK,’ he said, casually removing a small cockroach that was currently crawling across his cheek. I had absolutely no words left for him, and went down the corridor to see how Tim was faring. He was already packing his things and exiting his room as I arrived at his door. ‘I’m not staying here,’ he told me firmly. ‘There were five roaches inside my bedsheets. I’ve just asked Frazer if I can sleep in the back of the Toyota instead,’ he continued, hoisting his canvas bag further up his shoulder. I returned forlornly to my green hovel, deciding I couldn't possibly sleep on that dirty sheet, so I retrieved a thin silk sleeping bag liner that I had fortuitously packed in my luggage and climbed inside it, placing my jacket over the hard nylon pillow—the exact colour of old vomit. My eyes took in a huge, cracked wall mirror that was tilted at a dangerous angle directly above my headboard. I tried my best to sleep, but sat up fast a few hours later when loud singing commenced close by. I lay back down miserably. I was wide awake now, and it was stiflingly hot inside the room. I suddenly felt something crawling across the pillow fabric and frantically flung it away into the dark. When dawn finally broke, I used half a bottle of our mineral drinking water just to wash my face, and dressed as quickly as humanly possible, my eyes on the constant lookout for more of those flat, brown, oval-shaped monsters. The next day was a total blur of driving over dusty, dry plains and navigating spiky thorn bushes, with not many birds logged. I heard from the guides that we were scheduled to spend a secondary night in ‘Hotel Cockroach’, and I felt entirely resigned to just suck it up and make the absolute best of the situation. 

Injera and the Window Leers 

Keith asked the group to meet in the ‘dining room’ for supper— which turned out to be a local tavern attached directly to the far side of the hotel. Again, the room was full of peeling paint and a foetid, damp atmosphere prevailed. Several locals were sitting at the bar, drinking what might well have been their seventh beer of the evening. Our group sat on rickety plastic chairs around a wooden table covered in a dirty plastic tablecloth featuring a tired-looking pineapple motif. Frazer announced he would go in search of a menu. I wasn't holding my breath, so when he returned to report that we had a choice of either ‘wet or dry tibs’, I hardly registered any surprise. Tibs is a highly popular traditional Ethiopian dish consisting of a stir-fry of sliced meat (lamb, beef, or chicken) cooked in a rich, spicy sauce with butter, garlic, and onions. It is traditionally served atop Injera, a sour, fermented flatbread made from ancient teff flour, possessing a unique, spongy texture that allows it to serve as both the platter and the eating utensil for scooping up the stew. Personally, I absolutely do not like the texture of injera with its sour flavour and spongy consistency. The meat pieces delivered to our table were so thoroughly overcooked as to resemble strips of dry biltong jerky. In the end, I only managed to consume half a piece of stale bread washed down with a glass of water. Our checklist session took place at the table, and by 9:30 p.m., we were all heading back to our various forms of nocturnal torture. I set the small travel alarm clock that Ruth had kindly lent me for 3:30 a.m., and decided to just lie flat on top of my sleeping bag liner as it was far too hot and stuffy inside the room. I held my breath, peed directly into a plastic jug inside the shower stall to avoid the toilet, then scuttled quickly back to the hard bed. I had forgotten to switch off our main bedroom light switch, so I jumped off the mattress again. It didn't help matters anyway, because there was a bright municipal security light fixed right outside my window pane. The loud sound of drunken revelry emanated from the tavern next door, and an hour later, I was still staring miserably up at the cracked plaster ceiling, trying my absolute best not to listen to the distinct scurry of cockroaches on the floorboards. Suddenly, a human face appeared, peering directly in at me through the window glass, and I jumped in pure fright. ‘Go away!’ I shouted at the pane, and there was a drunken giggle in response from the dark. The leering face eventually disappeared, but throughout the long night, more faces materialized at the glass in various stages of advanced inebriation. It was only in the early hours of the morning that I finally dozed off, only to be awakened by the shrill sound of the alarm clock what felt like an hour later. I peered groggily at the clock hands, saw it was 3:30 a.m., and reluctantly got out of bed, flinging on the light switch and scattering cockroaches in all directions. I dressed quickly and went to wake the lads. Judging from the expressions on their faces, I’d say they hadn't slept a single wink either. We chose our vehicle for the day; we were sharing the ride with Keith and Katie this trip. Our first official stop was the Liben Plains—a massive, flat expanse known for its unique, fragile ecosystem. The Liben Plains are the critical habitat for the critically endangered Liben lark (or Sidamo lark), and it was specifically to tick this rare bird that our entire tour was in the region at all. A long day of hot, dusty, dry walks through the thick thorn scrub ensued with stifling slowness. The acacia bushes possessed incredibly sharp thorns, and none of our family escaped a good, painful snagging on our clothes, arms, and legs. A few anubis baboons and a Pringle's puffback were successfully spotted before we finally arrived back at the side of the dusty road, where the drivers had set up a makeshift area for us to eat sandwiches and drink mugs of hot, reviving coffee. Tim and I opted to forgo the secondary round of thorn-bush bashing in the heat, choosing instead to sit and wait inside the vehicle. 

The Liben Plains and the AK47 Caravan 

Ruth’s alarm clock awakened me at 3:30 a.m. once more the following morning. I took a quick cold shower inside Col’s room —at least his stall didn't feature a petrified human turd on the tile. The three of us shared a single vehicle for the next leg, and as we accelerated away into the dark, I thought to myself that wherever we ended up next, the accommodation could never possibly be as horrific as this place had been. The drive was incredibly long, heading straight towards the Kenyan border post, and then twisting along the Dawa River channel on a very bumpy, potholed dirt track. That afternoon, the drivers moved away from the river banks to a much drier location, and the group walked along the pale beige, dusty road towards a long caravan of dromedary camels moving through the scrub. The front of the line was led by three turban-wearing men dressed in long, white garments. I shouted a few cheerful calls of ‘Salama!’ to them before suddenly noticing that all three men had black AK47 assault rifles casually slung over their shoulders! Despite their highly dangerous appearance, they seemed perfectly friendly and warmly returned my greeting. As the dusk smeared the desert sky with beautiful streaks of pink and yellow, we finally arrived at our next accommodation infrastructure in Yabello. I climbed stiffly out of the vehicle and gazed hopefully towards the garden. There was a huge, beautiful open verandah fitted with proper tables and chairs, music was playing, and I was instantly reassured that this establishment would not be another insect-ridden hell-hole. There were long rows of flowering bushes, and gorgeous sweet scents wafting from the blooming moonflowers. We were allocated a room each, and what an unbelievable difference a soft, clean, bug-free bed and a hot shower sans fossilised stool specimens make for the human spirit! A few donkey-drawn tuk-tuks ambled past the gates, and colourfully dressed local women carried huge loads of wood on their backs and heads. Ethiopian facial features are distinctly recognizable from other African populations, often characterized by prominent cheekbones, almond-shaped eyes, defined brows, and fine curly hair, with common features including narrow noses and high foreheads. This unique appearance is due to centuries of ancestral mixing between sub-Saharan African and Middle Eastern populations. The birders returned to the lodge at lunchtime and sat down at a long table outside for a meal. Frazer eventually arrived looking thoroughly frazzled. His primary canvas bag, which contained over $1,000 in cash for tour expenses, had just been stolen! He wasn't entirely sure when or where the theft had occurred, but had only just discovered it missing from his gear now. He shrugged his shoulders pragmatically, muttering to the table that it was ‘just paper’, but the general mood of the tour group shifted instantly, and people began clutching their valuables significantly more tightly to their chests. A local policeman eventually arrived at the lobby to officially ‘investigate’ the disappearance. He wore a crisp white hat, dark sunglasses, and a cellphone was permanently glued to his ear. He did not exude a single shred of professional confidence. Later on, we drove in convoy to an open plain dominated by numerous red termite mounds. It was still fairly hot and humid, and dark storm clouds were gathering on the horizon. Ruth spotted a yellow butterfly in the scrub and pointed it out to Col, who immediately gave chase through the grass and managed to successfully swipe the specimen straight into his hat. He was highly pleased with this catch, and we didn't see him again until after four o'clock. Tim and I joined Roger for a walk down the asphalt. Tim had perked up immensely and was showing some genuine interest in the birds; I proudly pointed out a beautiful golden-breasted starling to his eyes. It started to rain heavily halfway down the road; fat, massive drops of water splashed down onto the dry, sandy road, soaking our clothes straight to the skin, but it cooled our bodies down beautifully so no one minded the downpour too much. Later on, we arranged our group between two cars. Keith, Tim, and myself climbed straight up onto the vehicle's roof rack to ride outside, with the remainder of the passengers sitting inside the cabin. We set off into the dusk clinging on tightly to the metal bars, especially when the driver was negotiating a deep donga ditch. The night air felt incredibly fresh after the storm and smelled strongly of wild sage. 

Lake Langano and the Stimulant Khat 

The next morning, I unfortunately got shat on by an Abyssinian starling while sitting outside on the bench waiting for breakfast service. I wiped away the wet faecal blob from my arm and chose to take this as a lucky omen; we were heading off to Lake Langano, which is situated closer to Addis Ababa. We had successfully travelled in a massive loop over the last few weeks. Our driver for this leg was Gurmah; he was a very nice fellow with a thin face and curly dark hair, and we spent the miles chatting about all things Ethiopian. The passing scenery transformed beautifully from dry acacia savannah to neat, trim grassy plains that were so flat one would think they had been mown by a giant lawnmower, before shifting into dark, dense forest. Gurnah played some local traditional music as well as a few Western pop songs on the tape deck, while I watched the local villagers threshing wheat, chopping wood, carrying huge burlap bags of corn, and transporting many thick bundles of palm-covered khat, as it is known locally. Khat (Catha edulis) is a flowering plant native to the Horn of Africa. The leaves are typically chewed like tobacco, retained in the cheek, and macerated intermittently to release the active chemical alkaloid, which produces a mild stimulant-like effect. We enjoyed a short stop in the town of Dila for lunch—a hot, dusty, and incredibly noisy settlement in the south. The local lunch crowd completely filled the restaurant room. Ours was a large table set directly against the concrete wall, and our meals were delivered to us on huge, circular tin trays; the portions were absolutely massive. From the car window, I watched two Gedeo women walking past, sporting beautifully, intricately woven hair designs and delicate facial features. Our hotel in Langano was set amid vast indigenous trees close to the edge of the lake. Long bushes of colourful bougainvillea brightened up the grounds, and various birds flitted around the lake water, which was the distinct colour of black tea. Tim and I joined the twitchers for a bird walk through the hotel property, which was set against massive granite outcrops. A golden jackal lurked under a huge tree trunk, before a truly mystical scene unfolded before our eyes: two snowy white horses came galloping gracefully straight through a smokey-coloured mist in the early morning light. I subsequently hired one of the resort's plastic paddle-boats so Tim and I could pedal furiously out to the absolute centre of the lake. At the far end of the garden later on, the two white horses we had seen earlier appeared ghostlike into view through the trees, and I figured we could take them out for a ride, successfully coaxing the animals into a slow, gentle trot around the garden periphery. 

The Seventeen-Layer Lasagne 

Today, we were heading towards the Gibe Gorge. The Gibe River is the largest tributary of the massive Omo River system. As we pulled over for breakfast on the side of the road, the sun made a watery appearance through the clouds. Aside from a few quick bird stops, we pressed on until our convoy arrived at the summit of the gorge. It had become exceedingly hot and dry outside, and the few scrawny thorn trees along the ridge didn't provide enough shade for our heads. There was a narrow river flowing nearby, with a few hippos snorting and cavorting in its center. Our scheduled ‘short’ walk turned out to be a long, intensely hot, dusty, and snake-infested upward climb in 38°C heat, before heading afterwards straight off towards the riverbank to watch a small group of local people harvesting massive green spinach leaves. From what I could see of our wilderness lodge when we finally arrived in the dark, it was a massive, tree-lined building set behind high security walls. The dining room was large, with beautiful oil paintings of local landscapes dotted along the vast concrete walls. The portion of lasagne I ordered was absolutely monumental; Tim and I actively counted seventeen individual layers of pasta! Dessert, however, turned out to be a complete logistical fiasco. Four members of our group wanted fruit salad served with ice cream instead of the listed cream; Col and I wanted cassata, and Tim asked the waiter for a crème brûlée. The waiter completely failed to understand the concept of replacing the cream with ice cream. He announced he must go to the kitchen to ask the chef if this substitution would be OK, and returned to our table with the sad news that there was absolutely no ice cream or cassata available in the kitchen. I promptly changed my order to the crème brûlée. He then announced to the table that the others may have one bowl of ice cream and one bowl of fruit salad as they ‘can’t replace the cream’, then he disappeared back behind the doors. He returned minutes later to say there was actually no ice cream or cream available at all, but claimed there was cassata available after all, so I quickly changed my order back to the cassata. He then stated it was only available in plain chocolate or vanilla, so I sighed and changed back to the crème brûlée once more. We were all thoroughly confused by now as to what was physically available inside the kitchen fridge and what was not. When our desserts were finally delivered to the table, we craned our necks to see what had actually arrived. There sat two separate bowls of fruit salad and ice cream—the exact items they had explicitly stated they didn't possess! The crème brûlée turned out to be frozen custard, and the ice cream was frozen gelato. We all tucked in with a bit of this and that, and ordered hot coffees with a fair degree of trepidation, it must be said. After our bird checklist session was completed for the evening, Alice announced crossly to the table that she was thoroughly fed up with travelling every single day and never seeing anything of the local people and historic towns. I was highly inclined to agree with her sentiments; it is truly fantastic to see all these beautiful endemic birds, but to entirely avoid any contact with the local people and cultural history seems completely ludicrous to my mind. The next morning, I had cereal, toast, and tea for breakfast, then wandered around the lodge grounds with my camera, capturing the playful antics of the grivet monkeys. There was an abundance of birdlife, monkeys, and a large spa pool on the property. It was a great pity that our family couldn't actually stay to enjoy any of it, as we were scheduled to depart straight after breakfast. 

Armed Herdsmen and Roasted Beans 

We set off for Awash by seven o'clock. The landscape became noticeably drier and more arid as our vehicle drove down the lush escarpment towards the plains. At a breakfast stop along the route, we enjoyed huge, pyramid-sized sandwiches served with hot spiced tea. After a couple of hours of driving, we stopped for another long, hot walkabout, passing more local goat herdsmen who were casually carrying AK47 assault rifles over their shoulders. Col asked one of the men if he could take a photograph of his rifle, and the man flatly refused. It is never a good idea to argue with a man holding a loaded firearm, so we simply carried on down the track. The twitchers were in search of some rare Little Brown Job (LBJ) bird, so I walked around the edge of a local lake, successfully photographing herons, flamingos, and a beautiful young saddle-billed stork. After another two hours of hot travel, our convoy finally arrived at the Awash National Park gates. This morning, we set off early for a bird walk along the dusty park tracks, coming right across scrub hares, a beautiful beisa oryx, Soemmerring's gazelles, lesser kudu, Salt's dik-diks, and a few warthogs paddling in the morning mud light. During our dinner that evening, Roger was in full flow at the table, delivering elaborate stories on exactly how to attract ‘birds’—meaning the human female variety. Tim was visibly, highly interested in his advice. There was a lovely smell of wood burning from across the room, which was quite acrid. The traditional ‘Coffee Lady’ arrived in the dining room, seated herself flat on the floorboards, and set out her cultural items. She possessed a small charcoal burner, raw green coffee beans, hot water, and small glass cups arranged neatly on a silver tray. The traditional Ethiopian Coffee Ceremony is a core cultural ritual of hospitality. The beans are systematically roasted by hand in a shallow pan over hot coals, and the aromatic smoke is carried around the room for guests to inhale the fragrance before the coffee is brewed inside a clay pot called a jebena. She roasted the beans over the burner in her shallow pan, then brought the pan around the tables so everyone could take a deep sniff of the rich aroma, passing round the coffee glasses along with a small raffia basket of fresh popcorn. Next, the water was brought to a boil and the newly ground, roasted beans were added to the hot water. After several minutes of brewing, she decanted the liquid coffee into a large jug and filled our glasses. The result was an incredibly strong, thick, but wonderfully aromatic coffee. We subsequently heard that our scheduled previous evening's night safari drive had been abruptly cancelled due to a severe breakdown in relations between the park authorities and the local herdsmen, who drive their cattle through the park boundaries every single day to graze the grass. Keith explained to our room that the rangers were terrified the armed herdsmen would mistake our tourist vehicles for game management rangers if we drove off the beaten paths at night, and most of them are heavily armed, as we had seen. Nevertheless, a night drive was eventually arranged for our group. Col, Tim, Keith, and Dennis piled straight onto the vehicle's roof rack outside, while Alice sat inside the cabin alongside Juju—an armed park ranger—and I sat in the front passenger seat next to Frazer. Keith held onto a massive spotlight beam, and we set off into the velvet darkness of the bush. Aside from a few springhares, a nightjar, some dik-diks, a Soemmerring's gazelle, and a beautiful bat-eared fox, we didn't spot much else in the dark. Tim and I walked down to the park waterfall after breakfast, navigating through beautiful dappled shade, then headed back up to the main gates towards a small museum building. I phoned the mobile number that had been taped to the wooden door of the museum. Five minutes later, a short local man arrived with his young son in tow and unlocked the door for us. The museum was more of a dusty mausoleum than an educational center, packed with moth-eaten taxidermy exhibits, some of which I suspect dated all the way back to the historic Italian occupation of the country. The guide rattled off some local history in broken English, and we rewarded his efforts with a cash tip afterwards, although truth be told, we hadn't understood a single word he had uttered. Later after lunch, I was quietly reading my book inside our hotel room, when a heavy shadow suddenly passed over the open doorway. I looked up from the pages to discover a massive male baboon standing directly in the doorway, watching me with intense interest and some clear evil intent in his deep-set eyes! Nervously, I stood up from the bed and shooed him away with my arms. He reached down, grabbed what turned out to be an empty plastic battery packet from our counter, and scarpered off into the garden. As we didn't store any food inside our room, he luckily didn't return. After supper and our obligatory bird checklist session were completed, we repaired to our rooms. Tim mentioned to me in passing that he had just spotted a mouse inside my bedroom. Sure enough, the rodent was scurrying rapidly around the skirting boards the moment I switched on my light switch. I absolutely love mice in their natural wilderness habitat, but I flatly do not want them anywhere near my bedsheets, so I leaped straight onto the mattress and tucked the mosquito net tightly under the base. Col later managed to coax the creature out of the room, and covered the small gap at the bottom of the door utilizing a spare pair of underpants. We departed the next morning after a quick breakfast of toast and eggs. We were allocated seats in the secondary vehicle alongside Bali the park ranger and Frazer, and pulled over for lunch at the Ethiopian Gentleman’s Club, which featured a beautiful architectural setting overlooking a massive crater lake. I ordered a ‘hum’ and cheese sandwich from the waiter, but simply couldn't bring myself to eat it as the sliced ham meat was a highly suspicious, dark burgundy colour. I carefully picked out the slices of processed cheese and ate those instead. Our stop for the night was at a small roadside hotel just off the asphalt. Their printed restaurant menu was highly amusing and read exactly as follows: • ‘Spagati with meet souce’ • ‘Beef cotelet with French frize’ • ‘File gulash’ • ‘Lamp tips’ I wisely opted for the ‘spageti’ with ‘meet souce’, there being no actual beef cotelet or frize or any number of other listed items available in the kitchen. 

Geladas and the Inflatable Skirt 

Today was our highly anticipated Simien Mountains day to observe the famous gelada baboons (Theropithecus gelada). Geladas are unique, grass-eating Old World monkeys entirely restricted to the high alpine grassland escarpments and deep gorges of the central Ethiopian plateau. I scoured the surrounding rocky escarpment the moment our vehicle arrived, and thought I could pick out two shapes in the far distance. As we walked down to a flat, grassy patch on the ridge, we were rewarded with five spectacular gelada sightings, including two magnificent adult males. They are commonly known to researchers as the ‘bleeding-heart baboons’ due to the distinctive, bright red, hairless hourglass-shaped patch of skin on their chests, complemented by long, flowing golden-brown capes of hair. Geladas are strictly terrestrial primates, spending almost their entire lives on the ground grazing on grasses, blades, and roots. Our lunch was eaten under the shade of the trees and consisted of traditional injera served with yellow and red lentils and mixed vegetables, which actually tasted absolutely delicious. Ten minutes after lunch concluded, the hardcore birders set off on foot into the blistering 40°C heat. Later on, our convoy continued driving upwards into the peaks, where the mountain air became distinctly cooler. There were rows of small, round, grass-roofed huts surrounded by tall Euphorbia candelabra trees. At the absolute summit of the Ankober Escarpment, I suddenly had to leap out of the moving vehicle, suffering from a catastrophic upset stomach. I grabbed a roll of loo paper and ran as fast as a human standing 14,000 feet above sea level can possibly manage —which is not fast at all—in a desperate search for a bush. I was forced to squat behind a large lump of soil to allow my bowels to violently explode. It was so bitterly cold on the ridge that I could barely pull up my drawers fast enough with my frozen fingers, before a commercial truck rumbled past and several pairs of local eyes swivelled straight down to where I was crouching on the dirt with my trousers around my ankles! I swear I am going to design an inflatable skirt; one that quickly puffs out like a balloon when a button is pressed, so that these awful roadside toilet stops can maintain a modicum of human decorum. Watch this space! Tim, Col, and I walked over to the absolute edge of the vast escarpment, looking down at the beautiful valley unfolding miles below. It was absolutely breathtaking, and we could see as far away as the lake at Awash where we had been earlier in the week. We spotted a small troop of geladas resting on the rocks and sat watching their interactions for a while. Later on, the hardcore birders joined our position, and we spent a very pleasant hour watching their social antics—the geladas' antics, that is, not the birders', though that remains highly debatable. Later that evening, we arrived at the Hotel Ghion in Addis Ababa and checked into our rooms. We had just enough time to wash our faces before meeting back in the lobby. Ruth asked if we can do some souvenir shopping, so Ghurma took our group to a real tourist trap of a market, where we wandered through overcrowded stalls selling ancient silver Coptic crosses, rings, and massive rolls of woven cloth. Tonight, we were driven to the Dimma Cultural Restaurant for our farewell dinner and entertainment. The venue itself was a wide, open-plan room packed with dining tables and a large, raised stage against the far wall. A long bar lay against the side wall, next to a smaller lounge room fitted with easy chairs. The farewell meal was a massive, traditional buffet featuring every imaginable style of Ethiopian culinary dish. We piled our plates high and sat down to watch a troupe of local dancers performing on stage, followed by a set of musicians playing traditional instruments. The dancers were dressed in gorgeously coloured traditional dresses, and each dancer held a bright red umbrella aloft. As the evening drew to a close, Keith handed each of us a plastic bag containing informational brochures for their forthcoming birding tours, a locally made spinning top complete with a fringe, a pen, and some freshly roasted Ethiopian coffee beans. On our very last morning in the city, we awoke exceptionally early to two entirely distinct sounds: one was the beautiful, echoing morning call to prayer, and the other was the deep, guttural sound of wild lions roaring loudly from the gardens of the presidential residence —which had formerly served as the palace of Emperor Haile Selassie. My father had actually met the Emperor once at an official diplomatic function in Uganda back in the 1960s, and always reported that he possessed an incredibly firm handshake. With those roaring lions in our ears, we began the tortuous trip back to Cape Town by plane.
