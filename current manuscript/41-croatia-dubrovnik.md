# Chapter 38: Croatia: Dubrovnik
 Billy Banana (not his real name, but it sounded similar), picked us up from the station. Our room was quite close to the Dubrovnik Fortress with its white-painted turrets and we had a great view from the window, except that it was overcast and rainy. Tim and I explored nearby streets and shop windows then found a small café for a drink and  supper. Our meal was served by a ‘dominatrix’ in squeaky black leather. I could imagine her thrashing the sous chef for providing a salty sauce.
Sunshine was streaming through the open window as I woke. The hostel was perched on a hill along an ancient walkway with a low stone wall which led to the older walls of Dubrovnik itself. Some of the lanes were very narrow and became quite congested with locals and tourists alike.

We came to a small square building that was St Blaise’s Baroque-style church. St Blaise was the patron saint of Dubrovnik. Following this we approached the Franciscan Church and arrived at the seafront to walk along the promenade near the Old Town. The sea that morning was less navy blue and more a deep cerulian colour broken with lacy waves. Narrow alleyways between buildings had paving stones rubbed smooth over the years.

Lokrum, the destination for the day, sits in the Adriatic Sea barely half a kilometre from Dubrovnik. Visitors are drawn to the salt lake’s natural swimming pools and the fact that the popular series Game of Thrones was filmed there. Exploring the island revealed a botanical garden, numerous walking paths and the old Benedictine monastery. 

After passing a small cave, we met a girl from the USA and a discussion about the island’s best spots ensued; she shared information about a popular nudist beach which I immediately filed under ‘never going to happen’. Peacocks, for which the island was famous, were found congregating at one of the rock pools with a few of the males spreading their beautiful fan of feathers to several unimpressed peahens. We chose lunch at an outdoor seafood restaurant and enjoyed calamari—delicious, tender and full of flavour.

The Benedictine Monastery housed one of the thrones from the TV series and we did the tourist thing and photographed each other perched on its massive seat.  Stone wall ruins could be viewed at Posebni Reserve and we met a couple there from Venice who offered to take some photos of us. After taking the ferry back to the mainland we walked around the old town listening to live music.

We were later heading for Bari which lay in the ‘heel’ of Southern Italy. A middle aged English couple stood on the ferry deck watching the activity below and we chatted to them for a while. Conversation soon turned to religion so we didn’t overstay our welcome when they attempted to have us ‘tread the path of righteousness’ and quickly continued our exploration lest there’d be a laying of hands. 

## Matera

A loud banging on the door summonsed us to breakfast and we slow-danced our way around the cramped cabin to get dressed then ambled down to the breakfast galley for boiled eggs, toast and coffee. I watched with gritty, red eyes as the ferry slowly approached Bari harbour. We were now in Italy and I had gained another year in age. One of these was great, the other, not so much.

After a quick trot through customs (with gun-toting soldiers), we wheeled our suitcases towards the bus terminus. 
Bari was very 'European' looking after Croatia. 
A pavement café in Palo del Colle took our order for cappuccinos. With no euros on our person and a request for cash payment, Tim headed off in search of an ATM. I watched several large lorries try to negotiate the small, cobbled street of the Palazzo di Cotta, almost knocking over a table and two local women in the process. I had many online birthday wishes, many of whom were querying why I had suddenly become ‘Dora D’Oraborous’ on Facebook. I thought it would be fun to change my name for my big birthday and change it back the next day. This proved difficult; Facebook said sternly that I couldn’t keep changing my name willy-nilly, so I was stuck with being Dora for 30 days.

Some interesting architecture and more old churches came into view as we passed time until midday when the bus was due to arrive. Close to an ornate fountain at Piazza Aldo we found a restaurant, Basic di Latti, for a coffee. A local musician was playing Steve Wilson on an electric guitar. I left him a few euros as he was really good.

 We walked up the Strada Sagges and came across the Giardini Isabella D’Aragona Church. A large, open, glass-walled ‘coffin’ on a raised dais contained what I thought was the wax replica of a woman captured in languishing mode and wearing a lavish white dress. She  inexplicably held a black feather in her wax-like hands. I later discovered that it was the exceptionally well-preserved natural mummy of Isabella of Aragon, Duchess of Milan and Bari (1470–1524). The mummification process, the high-quality, specialised burial clothing and optimal conditions of the crypt had led to the lifelike appearance.
  A very Italian-looking driver; short, stocky with curly dark hair and a Roman nose hopped out of the bus and loaded our cases. After finding some comfortable seats we set off for Matera—the City of Caves; a place we had only heard existed a few days before. 

Giuseppe met us in Matera but spoke very little English so we drove in silence to the caves. The difference in the landscape was amazing. Hundreds of little caves were set in the mountainside above a large valley where some 1,500 dwellings had been carved out of the steep ravine. This small network of caves was occupied in the Palaeolithic Age and now re-inhabited by local Italians. Matera’s ancient warren, once thought of as something shameful for its extreme poverty, now became an exotic experience for locals and tourists alike. 

Fernando Ponte was one of the first to take advantage of the empty real estate. In 1990, he started renovating five cave rooms around a courtyard. He installed plumbing, electricity, heating and ventilation systems against the prevailing humidity. Carved stone stairways still connected arches, attics and balconies. On the opposite side of the ravine, on a plateau called the Murgia, more mysterious caves stared back at us like vacant eyes. Giuseppe showed us to our ‘cave’ room which, in reality, was anything but. It was a large, airy room with a queen-size bed on the bottom floor and two more upstairs. Our room overlooked the ravine on one side and formed part of the grotto on the other. It was quite enchanting.

We were staying in the subterranean part of the old city. Pink and yellow wild flowers sprouted from ancient crevices. I loved it! We entered some of the old Byzantine churches with embossed ceilings, frescoes and stained-glass windows and came across a couple of Rupestrian churches.

After a few hours of wandering around we returned to Hotel Basiliani for a shower and I dressed for my birthday supper. ‘The Soul Kitchen’ wasn't easy to find and we took a few wrong turns along the narrow, twisty, cobbled paths. Our friendly waiter spoke four languages and explained the meal options to us in impeccable English.

The next day was warm and bright and the birds were singing in noisy harmony. We set off after a hearty breakfast to explore more of this fascinating place. I noticed as many stray cats here as there had been in Croatia, all seemed well fed thankfully. Tim and I nipped into a small shop that sold paintings depicting the old city of a few hundred years ago. We passed an underwater scheme at Sasso Barisano, a church in Via San Biagio that had gorgeous stained-glass windows and friezes called ‘The Chiesa San Giovanni Battista’ in the town square. 

Afterwards we walked back along the stone wall overlooking the ravine (Parco della Murgia) and stopped briefly at either a very new and unfinished or very old and unrestored restaurant (it was hard to tell which) for a mojito, drunk with olives and biscotti. A poster hung on a crumbling wall advertising 'The Passion of the Christ'; a Mel Gibson movie which was filmed there.

Later we set off again to look for a suitable place to sit and relax and found an outdoor café with rickety chairs called the ‘Pane e Pomodoro’. It was ideal because it had wooden tables and a good view of all the comings and goings of Matera. I borrowed a pen from the waitress so I could catch up with my journal then watched the colourful tuk-tuks carrying overweight tourists in the back.  Chinese tourists wandered by with their tour leader rattling off interesting snippets to his impassive faced group. A red-faced, bearded man sold wooden carvings from his stall on the roadside. He had a dog with a deformed leg; it nearly lost the other one several times trying to cross the busy road. The driving was atrocious; fast and furious. Later we walked along the outer walls to see the sunset and came to the new part of town, which looked to me like any other European city: fashion shops, coffee shops, restaurants and haberdasheries. On the way back we  stopped for supper at a modern restaurant on Via D’Addozio. A pleasant faced waiter served us a local red wine called ‘Cenacolo degli Artisti’ with starters of barley and lemon; I had a tasty ‘loin’ soufflé (I forgot to ask whose loin it was).

This time, we walked from the left and up the mountain till we came to a huge, disused cave, then a little further to where an old man still lived in one of the smaller caves. The following day we took a bus to Venice in the north.
