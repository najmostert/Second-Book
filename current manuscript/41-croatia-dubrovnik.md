# Chapter 38: Croatia: Dubrovnik

Our flight to Dubrovnik went quickly and we took the airport bus to the terminal where we were picked up by Billy Banana (not his real name, but it sounded similar). Our room was very basic, cheap and quite close to the Dubrovnik Fortress with its white-painted turrets and we had a great view from the window, except that it was overcast and rainy that day. Tim and I explored nearby streets and shop windows then found a small café down a side street where we had a drink and some supper. Our meal was served by a ‘dominatrix’ in squeaky black leather. I imagined her thrashing the sous chef for providing a salty sauce.

I awoke to sunshine streaming through the window, got up and went to investigate the communal kitchen at Villa Banana. Our place was on a hill with steep stone steps so we had a long walk along an ancient walkway with a low stone wall leading to the ancient stone walls of Dubrovnik itself. Some of the lanes were very narrow and became quite congested with locals and tourists alike.

We came to a small square building that was St Blaise’s Baroque-style church. St Blaise was the patron saint of Dubrovnik. Following this we approached the Franciscan Church and arrived at the seafront to walk along the promenade near the Old Town. The sea was less navy blue and more a deep green-blue colour that day. Narrow alleyways between the buildings had paving stones rubbed smooth over the years.

Lokrum was the destination for the day, immediately following breakfast which that morning was cheese omelettes prepared by yours truly in the small kitchen and enjoyed on the balcony. Meanwhile, Tim was busy finalising hotel bookings for the next leg of the journey—the Croatian coast and then Italy. Later, the two of us walked down to the ferry for the small island.

Lokrum sat in the Adriatic Sea, barely half a kilometre from Dubrovnik. Visitors were drawn to the salt lake’s natural swimming pools and the fact that the popular series Game of Thrones was filmed there. Exploring the island revealed a botanical garden, numerous walking paths, and the old Benedictine monastery. So many stray cats were everywhere!

After passing a pigeon cave, we met a girl from the US and a discussion about the island’s best spots ensued; she shared information about a popular nudist beach which I immediately filed under ‘never going to happen’. Peacocks, for which the island was famous, were found congregating at one of the rock pools with a few of the males spreading their beautiful fan of feathers to several unimpressed peahens. We chose to have lunch at an outdoor restaurant and enjoyed an order of fried calamari—delicious, tender, and full of flavour.

The Benedictine Monastery housed one of the famous Game of Thrones Iron Thrones from the TV series and we did the tourist thing and photographed each other perched on its massive seat. There were some other interesting bits and pieces in the museum including a sloping floor that ‘mimicked’ waves in a rough sea. Stone wall ruins could be viewed at Posebni Reserve and we met a couple there from Venice and we had a chat and they offered to take some photos of us. After taking the ferry back to the mainland we walked around the old town listening to some live music and looking at old churches, and afterwards found a sushi place. The waiter was a bit full of himself and his lip curled at our meagre choices—it was very expensive.

We arrived at the harbour and bought two tickets to Bari, which lay in the ‘heel’ of Southern Italy. An English couple stood on the ferry deck watching the activity below and we chatted to them for a while. They turned out to be an over-zealous religious couple so we didn’t overstay our welcome when they attempted to have us ‘tread the path of righteousness’ and quickly continued our exploration for fear there’d be a laying of hands. A large lounge had comfortable chairs, a bar, and a small restaurant where we would have supper. We watched the sunset slip into the dark sea then went for supper.

## Matera

We were woken by a loud banging on the door, summoning us to breakfast and we slow-danced our way around the cramped cabin to get dressed then ambled down to the breakfast galley consisting of boiled eggs, toast, and coffee. I watched with gritty, red eyes as the ferry slowly approached Bari harbour. We were now in Italy and I had gained another year in age. One of these was great, the other, not so much.

After a quick trot through customs (with gun-toting soldiers), we wheeled our suitcases towards the bus terminus and found a luggage-hold and gratefully shoved our cases into it. I needed a wee but was not prepared to pay €1 (R17) for the privilege. A sweet old couple tried to intervene on my behalf and pleaded with the ‘toilet security’ to let me in but he was having none of it. So, I hobbled down the busy road crossing heavy traffic, looking for a café and with it a toilet that did not require parting with any coins.

Bari seemed very European to me after Croatia. The Basilica Pontificia church was closed (quite strange for a church to be closed, but it was still quite early). We passed another church—the Piazza San Nicola—which was open so popped in to look at the beautifully painted ceiling, almost as opulent as the Sistine Chapel with its use of thick gold paint and intricate scenes in oil paint.

A pavement café in Palo del Colle took our order for cappuccinos. With no euros on our person and a request for cash payment, Tim headed off in search of an ATM. I watched several large lorries try to negotiate the small, cobbled street of the Palazzo di Cotta, almost knocking over a table and two local women in the process. I had many online birthday wishes, many of whom were querying why I had suddenly become ‘Dora D’Oraborous’ on Facebook. I thought it would be fun to change my name for my birthday and change it back the next day. This proved difficult, as Facebook said that I couldn’t keep changing my name willy-nilly (a fair comment), so I was stuck with being Dora for 30 days.

Some interesting architecture and more old churches came into view as we passed time until midday when the bus was due to arrive. Close to an ornate fountain at Piazza Aldo we found a restaurant, Basic di Latti, for a coffee. A local musician was playing Steve Wilson on an electric guitar. I gave him a few euros as he was really good.

A small group of women were protesting for women’s rights and against abortion across the road and a group of youngsters dressed in cultural attire provided a dance as part of the Cultural Arts Festival in Italy, which was on at the time. We walked up the Strada Sagges and came across the Giardini Isabella D’Aragona Church. A large, open, glass-walled ‘coffin’ on a raised dais which contained what I was sure was the body of a woman who had been captured in wax wearing a lavish white dress, and inexplicably holding a black feather in her wax-like hands. I later discovered that it was the exceptionally well-preserved natural mummy of Isabella of Aragon, Duchess of Milan and Bari (1470–1524). Due to the mummification process, the high-quality, specialised burial clothing, and the conditions of the crypt, her body was remarkably well preserved, leading to a lifelike appearance.

I had another coffee at San Nicola, where a pretty Italian waitress asked us where we were from. She said she once stayed with a girl from Durban, which we explained was a few days’ drive from Cape Town. Tim asked an elderly couple where to stand for the green FlixBus. It arrived, and a very Italian-looking driver hopped out. He was short, stocky, and had curly dark hair and a Roman nose. After finding some comfortable seats we set off not long after for Matera—the City of Caves; a place we had only heard existed a few days before thanks to TripAdvisor. I messaged Giuseppe who fetched us once we got to Matera then watched the passing landscape finding it to be flat and bland, bar the hundreds of poppies that grew everywhere and brightened up the otherwise dull surrounds.

Giuseppe spoke very little English so we drove in silence to the caves. The difference in the landscape was amazing. There were hundreds of little caves set in the mountainside above a large valley where some 1,500 cave dwellings had been carved out of the steep ravine. This small network of caves had been occupied in the Palaeolithic Age. These underground residences were being re-inhabited by local Italians and living there had become an exotic experience for locals and tourists alike. Matera’s ancient warren was once thought of as something shameful for its extreme poverty.

Fernando Ponte was one of the first to take advantage of this then-empty real estate. In 1990, he started renovating five cave rooms and a dining hall around a courtyard. He installed plumbing, electricity, heating, and ventilation systems against the prevailing humidity. Carved stone stairways still connected arches, attics, belfries, and balconies. There were churches carved out of stone, created by Byzantine monks, with frescoed interiors. On the opposite side of the ravine, on a plateau called the Murgia, more mysterious caves stared back like vacant eyes. Giuseppe showed us to our ‘cave’ room which, in reality, was anything but. It was a large, airy room with a queen-size bed on the bottom floor with two more beds upstairs. Our room overlooked the ravine on one side and formed part of the grotto on the other. It was quite enchanting.

We were staying in the subterranean part of the old city. Aside from all the caves there were pink and yellow wild flowers sprouting from different crevices. I loved it! We entered some of the old Byzantine churches with beautiful ceilings, frescoes, and stained-glass windows and came across a few Rupestrian churches, such as Chiese Rupestri. Rupestrian, I later learnt, meant ‘cut out of rock’.

After a few hours of wandering around, we returned to Hotel Basiliani for a shower, and I got ready for my birthday supper and set off in search of ‘The Soul Kitchen’, taking a few wrong turns along the narrow, twisty, cobbled paths. The friendly black waiter spoke four languages and explained the meal options to us in impeccable English.

The choice was three warm and three cold starters: meats, cheeses, olives, burnt onions, and crusty bread. I had a local red wine, Vino Rosso Calice, which was sweetish but light-bodied and we followed the starters with a salty pasta. We were both drooping with exhaustion by then, so we forwent the dessert, pay, and left for a dark walk back along the winding streets to the hotel. I thanked Tim for all his organising and travel plans; he had managed to more than make up for Cuba.

The day was warm and bright and the birds were singing away in noisy harmony. We set off after a hearty breakfast to explore more of this fascinating place. There were as many stray cats here as there had been in Croatia. Tim and I nipped into a small shop that sold paintings depicting the old city a few hundred years ago and I took a lot of photos of old buildings with wild flowers. There was an underwater scheme at Sasso Barisano, a church in Via San Biagio that had gorgeous stained-glass windows and in the large town square were friezes called ‘The Chiesa San Giovanni Battista’. Another stone church in Via D’Addozio with several huge friezes of various scenes on the walls.

Afterwards we walked back along the stone wall overlooking the ravine (Parco della Murgia) and stopped briefly at either a very new and unfinished or very old and unrestored restaurant—it was hard to tell which—for a mojito, which we drank with olives and biscotti. There was a poster on a crumbling wall advertising the filming of The Passion of the Christ; a Mel Gibson movie, which was filmed there.

Later we set off again to look for a suitable place to sit and relax and found an outdoor café with rickety chairs called the ‘Pane e Pomodoro’. It was ideal because it had wooden tables and a good view of all the comings and goings of Matera. I borrowed a pen from the waitress so I could catch up with my journal then watched the colourful tuk-tuks driving up and down with overweight tourists in the back. A few Chinese walking tours wandered by, with the tour leader rattling off interesting snippets to a bored group. A red-faced, bearded man sold carvings in a stall on the roadside. He had a dog with a deformed leg; it nearly lost the other one several times trying to cross the busy road. The driving was atrocious there—fast and furious. I drew the outline of the caves on my coffee for my Instagram feed. Later we walked along the outer walls to see the sunset and came to the new part of town, which looked to me like any other European city: fashion shops, coffee shops, restaurants, and haberdasheries. On the way back we decided to stop somewhere for supper and found a modern restaurant on Via D’Addozio. A pleasant waiter served us a local red wine called ‘Cenacolo degli Artisti’ and we had starters of barley and lemon; I had a tasty ‘loin’ soufflé (I forgot to ask whose loin it was).

This time, we walked from the left and up the mountain till we came to a huge, disused cave, then a little further to where an old man still lived in one of the smaller caves. The following day we were taking a bus to Venice in the north.
