# Chapter 38: Croatia: Dubrovnik

Our flight to Dubrovnik was quick, and we were soon picked up by our driver, whom we dubbed ‘Billy Banana’. Our room was basic and quite close to the Dubrovnik Fortress, with its white-painted turrets; it offered a great view, though the weather was overcast and rainy. We dined at a small café served by a ‘dominatrix’ in squeaky black leather—I imagined her thrashing the sous-chef for making the sauce too salty! The next day, the sunshine streamed through the window. We walked the ancient stone walkway leading to the walls of Dubrovnik. We visited the Baroque-style St Blaise’s church, then arrived at the seafront promenade near the Old Town, where the sea was a deep, green-blue. We took the ferry to Lokrum, an island barely half a kilometre from the city. Visitors flock there for the natural swimming pools and the monastery where Game of Thrones was filmed. We explored the botanical gardens and the old Benedictine monastery, where we sat on the famous Iron Throne. Peacocks were everywhere, the males spreading their fans of feathers for unimpressed peahens. We enjoyed fried calamari at an outdoor restaurant before returning to the mainland to hunt for sushi. We eventually made our way to the harbour to buy two tickets to Bari, Italy. On the ferry deck, we encountered an overzealous religious couple who attempted to make us ‘tread the path of righteousness’; fearing an unsolicited laying on of hands, we quickly retreated to the large lounge. As we watched the sunset slip into the dark sea, I couldn't help but feel that despite the travel hiccups, we had been incredibly lucky to land in such beautiful corners of the world. We were woken by a loud banging on our cabin door, summoning us to breakfast. We slow-danced our way around the cramped cabin to get dressed and ambled down to the galley for boiled eggs, toast, and coffee. I watched with gritty, red eyes as the ferry slowly approached Bari harbour. I had gained another year in age; one of these is great, the other, not so much. After a quick trot through customs, past gun-toting soldiers, we wheeled our suitcases toward the bus terminus. I needed a ‘wee’, but after being refused entry to the paid facilities by a stubborn security guard, I hobbled down the busy road in search of a café with a free toilet. Bari felt very European after Croatia. The Basilica Pontificia was closed, but the Piazza San Nicola was open; we popped in to look at the ceiling, which was almost as opulent as the Sistine Chapel, dripping with thick gold paint and intricate oil scenes. In Palo del Colle, Tim headed off to an ATM while I sat at a pavement café. I watched several large lorries try to negotiate the small, cobbled street, almost knocking over a table and two local women in the process. I checked my Facebook, where friends were querying why I had become ‘Dora D’Oraborous’ for the day. Changing my name had seemed like a fun birthday prank, but Facebook’s 30-day rule meant I was stuck with being Dora for a month! Around midday, we found a restaurant near a fountain at Piazza Aldo for coffee, where a musician was playing Steve Wilson on an electric guitar. Across the road, women were protesting for women’s rights, while a group of youngsters in cultural attire provided a dance as part of an arts festival. Inside the Giardini Isabella D’Aragona church, I saw an exceptionally well-preserved natural mummy—Isabella of Aragon, Duchess of Milan and Bari (1470–1524). Her body, mummified in her high-quality burial clothing, appeared remarkably lifelike. 

Matera: The City of Caves 

We boarded a green FlixBus for Matera. Our driver was a short, stocky Italian with curly hair and a Roman nose. After a drive through flat, bland landscapes brightened only by fields of poppies, the terrain transformed. We reached the ‘City of Caves,’ where some 1,500 dwellings have been carved out of the steep ravine. Occupied since the Palaeolithic Age, this ancient warren was once considered a shameful symbol of extreme poverty, but today it is being re-inhabited, becoming an exotic experience for locals and tourists alike. Fernando Ponte was one of the first to renovate these ruins in 1990. Today, carved stone stairways connect arches, attics, belfries, and balconies. Our ‘cave’ room was anything but primitive; it was a large, airy suite with a queen-size bed downstairs and two more upstairs, overlooking the ravine on one side and forming part of the grotto on the other. We wandered through old Byzantine churches with frescoed interiors and chiese rupestri—churches literally cut out of the rock. That evening, I set off in search of ‘The Soul Kitchen’ for my birthday supper, taking a few wrong turns along the narrow, twisty paths. The friendly waiter explained the options in impeccable English. We shared a mezze of meats, cheeses, olives, burnt onions, and crusty bread, followed by a salty pasta and a light-bodied local red, Vino Rosso Calice. Exhausted, we paid and left for the dark, winding walk back to the hotel. I thanked Tim for all his organising; he had more than made up for our failed Cuba trip. The next day was warm and bright. We explored more of this fascinating place, popping into a shop to see paintings of the old city from centuries ago. I took photos of buildings bursting with wildflowers, visited an underwater scheme at Sasso Barisano, and marvelled at the intricate friezes in the large town square. Matera is truly enchanting—a place where history is carved into the very earth. the Chiesa San Giovanni Battista. Another stone church on Via d'Addozio features several huge friezes depicting various scenes along its walls. Afterwards, we walked back along the stone wall overlooking the Parco della Murgia ravine and stopped briefly at a restaurant that was either very new and unfinished, or very old and unrestored—it was hard to tell which—for a mojito, which we drank with olives and biscotti. There was a poster on a crumbling wall advertising the filming of Mel Gibson’s The Passion of the Christ, which was shot right here. Later, we looked for a suitable place to relax and found an outdoor café with rickety chairs called Pane e Pomodoro. It was ideal; the wooden tables offered a perfect view of the comings and goings of Matera. I borrowed a pen from the waitress to catch up on my journal, watching the colourful tuk-tuks drive up and down with overweight tourists in the back. A few Chinese walking tours wandered by, with the leader rattling off snippets to a bored-looking group. Nearby, a red-faced, bearded man sold carvings from a roadside stall; his dog, which had a deformed leg, nearly lost another one several times trying to cross the busy, ‘fast and furious’ road. I amused myself by drawing the outline of the caves onto the foam of my coffee for my Instagram feed. As the sun set, we walked along the outer walls into the new part of town, which looked like any other European city with its fashion boutiques and haberdasheries. We stopped at a modern restaurant on Via d'Addozio, where a pleasant waiter served us a local red wine called Cenacolo degli Artisti. We started with barley and lemon, followed by a tasty ‘loin’ soufflé (I forgot to ask whose loin it was!). The next day, we trekked up the mountain to a huge, disused cave and visited an old man who still lives in one of the smaller, traditional dwellings. The following morning, we packed up to catch a bus north to Venice.
