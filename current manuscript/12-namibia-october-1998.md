# Chapter 10: Namibia: October 1998

We’re off to Namibia tomorrow—called South West Africa when I lived there. The name ‘Namibia’ comes from the Nama word Namib, meaning a ‘vast place’ and it certainly lives up to that description. After starting supper, I ran a bath and returned to the kitchen to finish off the cooking completely forgetting about the running taps until I spotted a dark, seeping stain of water snaking out from under the bathroom door and through the hallway. It paused momentarily to leer at me before spreading rapidly towards the lounge carpet. I took a deep, shuddering breath, aimed an angry kick at some scattered toys on the floor and set about the exhausting task of mopping it up, which took ages. Shortly afterwards, Col dropped a few raw eggs onto the kitchen floor so as not to make me feel left out and we alternately mopped, slithered around and dried the floors until we were both thoroughly exhausted. Tim had long since given up trying to dodge out of our way and had wisely taken himself off to bed, where he had climbed under the sheets with all his clothes on. I had just finished packing the car this morning—which now looked as though adding actual people to the towering mound of luggage was bordering on the overly optimistic—when we spotted a bergie ( homeless person) swaying casually up our driveway to peer drunkenly into the depths of our dustbin. I was so annoyed that he’d witnessed our imminent departure that I threw a string of expletives at him, which naturally elicited a matching string of expletives from the depths of the gap-toothed hobo that would make the average man’s toes curl. The content of his colourful invective involved an undue amount of calling my ancestry into question, while making several explicit references to felines. In the end, I just gave him a ham sandwich and watched until he was safely out of sight. Col immediately slipped into his signature role as the ‘pessimistic predictor of coming events’, and growled that the untimely arrival of the hobo guaranteed we would not return to find a full house. We squeezed into the full car, almost forgetting Tim in the process; he’d been patting Jonny-fone the cat and instructing him to look after the property because ‘Daddy will be very cross’ if he didn’t. 

The Road to Namibia
The car was not performing well despite its diagnostic sortie to the garage the previous day. It felt incredibly jerky and was severely lacking power, meaning we would have to get it looked at further down the line. 
Still, we were on our way at last. We both knew that back at the laboratory, people would be frantically looking for Col, desperate to keep him from his hard-earned holiday for just a fraction longer. As the locals would say, ‘fok hulle’—roughly translated as an invitation to engage in a spot of self-fornication. I pulled over at a roadside farm stall to use the toilet. Everything and I mean everything in the ladies' loo was covered in frilly, crocheted and embroidered pink and green yarn. The window ledge, the tables, the chairs, the spare toilet paper rolls, the mirrors, and even the ceramic toilet cistern itself was heavily bedecked in a dense froth of knitwear. It was so overwhelming I had to fight my way through mounds of ice-cream-coloured frills just to locate the flush handle. 
Our next stop was the town of Springbok, where we purchased a bucket of KFC—or ‘Kentucky Fried Buzzard’, as Col preferred to call it—for lunch. From there we carried on towards the Vioolsdrif border post. The South African side of the border was surprisingly quick to clear, but the Namibian side was pure, unadulterated bureaucracy—an endless sea of paper forms and rigid officialdom. The specific border official who attended to us possessed that slow, sullen manner that is so irritating at international boundary lines. He left us in absolutely no doubt that he was the man in charge and that he alone would decide how easily we would be permitted entry. His laconic form-filling ceased abruptly however, the moment he spotted Col’s large telephoto camera lens lying on the cracked Formica counter. His interest instantly piqued. He picked up the heavy metal housing in his massive hands, slowly turning it over in his meaty paws as though the architectural secret to its function lay hidden somewhere on its shiny surface. ‘What is dis ting?’ he barked. Realising the lens wasn't about to verbally volunteer its purpose, he ordered Col to demonstrate exactly how it worked in action. Col put the equipment through its paces with an amazing, albeit strained, show of patience—especially, I thought irritably, since I wasn't even allowed to breathe on those lenses back home. He did however, twitch visibly when the official stabbed a thick finger towards the lens and demanded to know what type of ‘wepon’ it was. Colin informed him that they were delicate camera parts designed for taking long-distance pictures. He placed a subtle soupçon of stress on the word delicate. Undeterred, the official raised a skeptical eyebrow, picking up a second lens in his rough hands, casually pawed the glass element with a greasy finger before holding it up to his eye. I could see a muscle jumping 
menacingly in Col’s jawline, but he managed to hold his tongue. I, on the other hand, held no such restraint and muttered loudly about the sheer rudeness of people handling expensive, specialised equipment without a clue as to what they were doing. My shin received a sharp, cautionary kick from Col under the counter and I wisely turned and left the room. I absolutely detest border posts and Col thoroughly detests me at border posts. A short while later, a khaki-uniformed female official with a large derriere that threatened to burst right through the seams of her trousers sauntered nonchalantly towards our vehicle and glanced at the windscreen. Her general demeanour said it all; she was fully prepared to inspect every single nut and bolt on the chassis even if it took her all afternoon. It didn't luckily, but our tempers were both reaching boiling point by the time she waved us through. The moment we cleared the gates Col and I entered full bickering mode. I was peering down at the map book, which caused a bright white reflection against the glass of the windscreen. Col snapped that he couldn't concentrate and was going to drive us straight into a roadside ditch because the map was glaring in his eyes. I retorted sharply that we would just have to get completely lost then, because I had no other physical way of reading the route unless I sat on the floorboards. Col grumbled that I should find something else to occupy my time, to which I replied that he should hand over the keys and let me drive instead. This inane vehicular bickering continued for a few more kilometres before dying a natural death. Having successfully burned off our accumulated border frustrations we continued the journey in  companionable silence. We were still some 600 kilometres away from Windhoek and thoroughly exhausted, so decided to push on as far as Mariental to look for a room for the night. 

I woke up for a wee at 3 a.m., scrabbling clumsily over Col’s sleeping form in the dark and asked him if it was morning. His reply was short and to the point. The surrounding landscape to Windhoek was beautifully stark and bare, devoid of trees and bushes in most directions as befitted a true semi-desert. There were a great number of Combretum bushes lining the route; their striking pinkish-purple seasonal flowers were actually the plant's unique seed pods. Arriving in Windhoek is always a somewhat surreal experience, especially if you are transitioning from the lush greenery of other African regions. The city doesn't simply appear on the horizon; it reveals itself dynamically, tucked away securely amongst the rolling, sun-bleached hills of the Khomas Hochland plateau. I found I hardly recognised a single thing, which wasn't surprising given I hadn't set foot in the city for almost twenty years. We located the main thoroughfare, which had since been renamed Independence Avenue (formerly known as Kaiserstrasse during the colonial era). The historic old German Lutheran church was still standing proud on the corner of the old Leutweinstrasse where we used to live. That hot, dry desert wind that I remembered so vividly from my youth started blowing across the tarmac and I recalled that I didn't like it then any more than now. We walked into the Parks Board information office to receive a typically laconic response from the clerk behind the counter window. She could barely summon enough enthusiasm or energy to answer even the simplest of travel enquiries such as, ‘Do you work here?’ Our queries regarding accommodation vacancies in Etosha National Park were answered in purely monosyllabic grunts—which is an incredibly difficult feat when attempting to describe the layout of a chalet. After securing two nights at Namutoni camp, one night at Halali and two final nights at Okaukuejo we left her to her phone. I wanted to see if I could locate our old family home; it used to be the oldest standing house in Windhoek and by all rights it still was. Navigating the town was difficult as the historical name changes were exacerbated by a massive three-lane highway that certainly hadn't existed during my childhood. I eventually found our old house; it was now the local Ombudsman’s office. The distinctive red corrugated roof and steep stone steps leading to the front entrance were instantly recognisable, though not much else. I knocked on the side door and one of the office ladies kindly allowed me entry after I explained my attachment to the building. We wandered through what had once been John’s old bedroom—now a sterile office space—and into the old lounge, which was still recognisable by the fireplace. I videoed as much of the interior as I possibly could while we walked through what used to be our home. The resident staff were all remarkably good-natured about having a complete stranger wander in off the street with a video camera, murmuring things like, ‘And this exact spot is where my dad shot bullet holes straight into the fireplace.’ It felt strange to stand there surrounded by modern office equipment, whirring computers, and busy typists while vivid ghosts of the past floated silently in and out of the rooms. I thanked the occupants for their hospitality and stepped back onto the mica-dusted street. 

The Road to Etosha

Col booked us a room for the night at a pension hotel in Otjiwarongo. The garage mechanics informed us that the land cruiser wouldn't be ready until late. After enjoying a cold drink on the shady verandah of a local bar, we walked back to the workshop, only to be told that the car still wasn't ready, even though it was now 5 p.m. We were forced to follow the mechanic to another workshop down an alleyway to find what I suspected was a relative of his; the identical square, ectomorph, matching ear structure, thick thatch of black hair paired with a casual reference to ‘Pa’, convinced me of the family connection. They had a cursory look over the engine block and swapped the silencer. A sizeable mountain of Namibian Dollars changed hands and we finally set off for Otjiwarongo. Darkness fell rapidly across the plains as  I drove the final leg of the journey. Driving at night was a thoroughly hairy experience as the road was nothing more than a rough gravel pit of swirling, pale dust, but I managed to keep the tyres more or less in the direction of ‘ahead’. Col later remarked dryly that it was the worst seventy kilometres he had ever sat through without actually driving. My hands remained locked in a tight, clenched position for a good hour after we finally arrived at the hotel. 
On our way out of town the following morning, we passed a car accident. Col threw the car into reverse because he thought he had spotted someone still sitting inside the wreckage. He was right. The vehicle had quite obviously rolled several times before coming to a rest in the scrub at the side of the road, but the bizarre thing was that a man was sitting quietly in the passenger seat, already wearing a rigid medical neck brace. He was fully conscious and told us what had happened. He claimed the accident had occurred at around 9 p.m. the previous evening after which the local emergency services had arrived, taken him to the hospital, fitted the neck brace around his cervical spine and then driven him straight back out to the crash site where he was left all night. Col checked his pulse which was slow but steady and the man seemed entirely lucid. We flagged down a passing vehicle whose driver possessed a working cellphone to alert the police, left the stranded man with our only bottle of drinking water and drove on. 
The sky was heavily overcast as we drove across the dry, flat landscape, but the temperature was climbing rapidly. The surrounding countryside was a beautiful combination of rich red earth, Combretum bushes and iconic thorn trees that featured a stark, black bark that looked as though it had been scorched by an intense bushfire. A tiny Kirk's dik-dik scuttled nervously around the base of a thorn bush and a family of warthogs and flock of guinea fowl leaped into the thick undergrowth as our vehicle approached. It boded well for wildlife viewing inside Etosha. About thirty kilometres away from the Namutoni gate, Col noticed that the car’s temperature gauge had shot way over into the red zone. The radiator water was bubbling and boiling away furiously like an old, rusty kettle. Because we had given our only bottle of water to the accident victim, a cloud of despair descended upon the cabin for a minute or two until another vehicle came into sight. We flagged them down and the driver kindly gifted us a bottle of water. The temperature indicator stayed down but I noticed a fresh fluid leak springing from somewhere deep beneath the dashboard which proceeded to drip warm water intermittently onto my left foot for the remainder of the drive. When we arrived at Namutoni and climbed out of the cabin to stretch our legs, I glanced down at my feet and noticed that I possessed one rusty, bright red foot and one clean white one, which looked more than a little eccentric. 

Etosha National Park: A Sanctuary of Giants

Namutoni was originally constructed as a German imperial military fort and Tim was absolutely chuffed that we were sleeping inside a real fort. The designated room was tiny; if all three of us wanted to stand up at the same time, two were forced to sit on the bed. The camp featured a wonderful floodlit waterhole located right along the perimeter fence, allowing guests to view wildlife as they came down to drink without frightening them away—and without having to leave the safety of the camp grounds. A couple of gemsbok and a small herd of plains zebras were standing around the water's edge, lazily swishing their tails to ward off the persistent flies. We returned hot, dusty, and thoroughly satisfied from our first official evening game drive. A buffet was laid out for dinner in the dining hall, boasting an excellent variety of food, including traditional puddings, tea, and filter coffee. I sat down and drank a litre of fruit juice without drawing breath. After an excellent repast we set off for the local viewing hide to await the arrival of nocturnal animals at the waterhole. There were about fifteen other tourists already quietly seated on the long wooden benches, peering out expectantly into the floodlit clearing. The wilderness show that evening had apparently suffered a major script change, as it proved to be the local frogs' night for entertainment. They croaked and counter-croaked in a deafening, rhythmic chorus for at least an hour against the spectacular backdrop of a fire-red desert sunset. The frogs, with a fresh burst of staccato croaking, tried their best to hold our attention but we had heard quite enough of the amphibian symphony and slowly made our way back to our air-conditioned cell for some much-needed sleep; the loud chanting of the frogs still ringing loudly in our collective ears. 
At the Kalkheuwel waterhole the following morning, we struck gold in the form of lions. They had clearly just finished consuming a fresh kill as they were round-bellied with muzzles stained a deep, vivid red with fresh blood. There were four of them in total walking lazily down towards the water's edge to drink. A massive gathering of eland, impala, plains zebras and greater kudu were waiting anxiously from the safety of the surrounding bushes, their tails twitching nervously. They were obviously dying for a drink, but knew better than to join the apex predators at the water's edge, even if the cats were visibly gorged. We sat in a state of tense excitement, watching the unfolding wildlife drama from our car window through binoculars. Suddenly a lone bull elephant wandered majestically onto the stage and we all held our breath, hardly daring to move at this unexpected arrival. It was the lions' turn to be wary; they gave the massive elephant  wide berth, circling cautiously around the great bull before heading slowly offstage into the yellow scrub. 
We returned to camp for a midday nap and awoke to the waning afternoon heat, which stripped the sky of its colour. Our evening drive brought out several more elephants, greater kudu sightings and a magnificent kori bustard made a guest appearance by the track. We left Namutoni behind and drove the dusty gravel roads towards the Halali rest camp, aiming to arrive by 7 p.m. whist conducting a game drive en route. The bush was quiet at first but then we spotted three lions approaching our vehicle from a game path across the way. They were heading determinedly towards a specific target straight ahead and paid our car scant attention. One magnificent male with a dark, sweeping mane of hair passed right alongside the passenger door and Col frantically hissed at me to wind my window up. I frantically grappled with the manual window lever in a panic. In my haste, I accidentally wound a large chunk of my hair straight into the window mechanism and wasted a few precious, eye-watering moments in absolute agony while I desperately extricated the strands of hair from the window frame and replaced them back on my scalp. The lions were heading directly towards a basic lean-to public toilet block that we had utilised just minutes previously, both thankful we hadn't lingered there any longer than necessary. Closer to the Halali camp, we encountered a colossal elephant carcass rotting in the sun; a wake of white-backed vultures circled lazily overhead, beadily eyeing out opportunity for a massive feast. We also spotted black-faced impala and something entirely new to our list—a herd of red hartebeest nibbling on the dry grass. As their name implies, they were a rich reddish-brown colour with short, intricately curved horns. They’re fast runners, capable of reaching speeds of up to 65 km/hr. Red hartebeest are pure grazers and possess the evolutionary ability to survive entirely without water, provided they can extract their required daily moisture content directly from plants. 
Our new base at Halali was a bit institutional and barracks-like in its architectural layout. We hastily shoved the fast-defrosting meat we had purchased back in Windhoek into the freezer compartment of the small fridge. The camp was bristling with international birdwatchers, all sporting enormous telephoto lenses, heavy tripods, and specialised, many-pocketed utility jackets that bulged with the high-tech tools of their trade. Grey hornbills and an apparently migratory pied flycatcher were hopping around casually in the white dust surrounding our chalet. I absolutely loved the scenery in this section of the park, stretching from the vast, open salt pans tinged with a delicate lime green to the bare, lime-washed scrub that looked paradoxically wintry in the middle of the shimmering, white-hot desert heat. We logged many of the same animal species as the previous day, photographed a few more red hartebeest before setting off for our final port of call— the Rietfontein waterhole. The coup de grâce of the day occurred in the beautiful evening light under a typically dramatic, slate-grey sky: a massive breeding herd of elephants came trumpeting down to drink at the blue-tinged pan, which featured a central island of green vegetation. They provided a spectacular show—drinking, playing and splashing water over their calves before clambering out onto the bank to enjoy a dust bath en masse. It was a magnificent experience to watch such a display of gay abandon from these great, lumbering beasts. 
Back at camp, I finished cooking our supper—a meal of lamb, peas, potatoes, and cucumber—followed by yoghurt and sweet custard for pudding. Col kept sniffing the meat suspiciously, asking if I had cooked it long enough to kill any bacteria. I assured him dryly that I had absolutely no wish to poison my family nor myself and he tucked in happily. We ate on our verandah, with the surrounding African bush providing a beautiful evening soundtrack of frogs and crickets, punctuated by the occasional eerie hyena whoop echoing in the near distance. It was pitch dark and a bit scary walking down to the Halali lookout area, while navigating the path through the thick bushes from the car park. The lookout platform was constructed directly onto natural rock, utilising the incline to allow tourists to sit comfortably and observe the night activity. We secured a wooden bench and soon after our arrival a lone black rhino emerged centre stage from the sable shroud of darkness, lumbering up to drink from the illuminated pool. A black-backed jackal made its appearance, followed ten minutes later by a second massive black rhino from the inky darkness, who politely introduced itself to the first. A single spotlight shone strategically over the waterhole, but it was still far too dark to tell if I was capturing the event sufficiently on my video. Black rhinos are specialised browsers. They utilise their pointed, prehensile hooked upper lip to feed on leaves, twigs, branches and wild fruit from bushes and trees. This specialised lip allows them to expertly grab and pull dense vegetation into their mouths, shearing it off with their sharp molars. White rhinos, by contrast, are grazers, possessing wide, flat mouths designed for vacuuming up grasses and low-lying ground plants. Later on, a bold honey badger arrived on the scene, scooting quickly around the water's edge. He stumbled suddenly upon the massive rhino, who took such a fright at the tiny creature that it immediately rushed offstage into the bush (the rhino that is, not the honey badger). The next morning, we set off towards the Goas waterhole but accidentally missed the turn-off, which turned out to be a stroke of luck because we came across a mother black rhino walking with her young calf. Col cut the engine and we sat watching them in silence for a while before continuing our drive. We passed the rotting elephant carcass once more, and the stench was horrific in the midday heat. A trio of black-faced impala, a few greater kudu and several gemsbok were browsing nearby, seemingly unbothered by the odour. 
After a session of frantic packing, we were ready for the drive to our final camp; Okaukuejo. This was the largest and oldest established camp in the reserve, dating back to the era when the legendary Bernabé de la Bat was the first chief game warden. He had famously overseen the installation of the massive 2.6-metre, game-proof boundary fence along the park's entire perimeter back in 1973. We called in at the Gemsbokvlakte pan along the way and found a magnificent multi-species gathering all drinking peacefully together: impala, eland, springbok, gemsbok, and kudu stood shoulder-to-shoulder with a confusion of zebras and guinea fowl. Our chalet at Okaukuejo was the largest yet, and Tim finally had a proper bed of his own to sleep in. We visited the main hide on our way back to camp later that afternoon, which was wonderful as a large herd of elephants were actively drinking at the waterhole. The sun was setting—a beautiful, glowing ball of orange fire framed against a background of shimmering pink dust. The elephants were taking massive trunkfuls of the red earth and throwing it over their backs. It was magical to watch. There were several large bulls in the group and they all possessed a characteristically pale, chalky-white coating from the local calcrete dust, which looked exceptionally photogenic in the sunset. 
Back at the camp grounds we found the viewing area packed to capacity with international tourists, all staring out at the nearby waterhole, including a tall, sinewy woman dressed in a long flowing white robe, strappy gold sandals, and heavy make-up. She must have been at least eighty years old. She caught my gaze and revealed a tombstone smile which I returned with some embarrassment. After supper, we prepared for our final evening of viewing at the floodlit waterhole; a beautiful setup with powerful spotlights illuminating animals as they drank. We were in for a spectacular evening of game drama. First, we watched the tender interaction between a massive bull, a small calf and a protective mother elephant. Then a black rhino ambled casually onto the stage and we waited with bated breath to see how the elephants would react to his intrusion. They completely ignored him. A lone giraffe appeared on the perimeter and gracefully bent its long, elegant neck to drink from the pool; a beautiful jigsaw ballerina. There were several black-backed jackals skulking around the edges when suddenly, a pride of four or five lions arrived on the scene. They padded softly into the illuminated perimeter, clearly not too keen on joining the elephants at the main pool. A baby elephant calf walked jerkily up to the massive rhino, boldly attempting to show off his dominance. The rhino ignored the youngster for the most part with only a twitching tail indicating his awareness of the cheeky little pachyderm. Another adult black rhino joined the busy stage, which apparently proved a step too far for the resident bull elephant. A sudden, dramatic altercation erupted between them, resulting in a tense thrust-and-parry of heavy movements. One would charge forward while the other backed off and vice versa. It was thrilling stuff, except for the fact that I ran out of film and battery power at that exact moment! It was incredibly annoying because I could see the lions actively inching closer to the water and felt certain there was about to be some major predatory excitement. Just then, Tim declared loudly to the entire viewing deck his firm intention of going to sleep... in his own bed. I rushed back to our chalet, tucked Tim securely into bed, grabbed a fresh roll of film and plugged the battery into the charger for a quick ten minutes, ready to race back to the hide for the footage of a lifetime. Naturally, the universe had other plans. Instead of a quick exit, I had to gabble a frantic, ten-minute bedtime story to Tim, ensuring the rapid demise of the story's hero to save time, hunt for the spare charger unit, locate a suitable international plug adapter, waggle the wire to keep the temperamental charging light active, have a quick wee, and sprint back across the camp to Col at the lookout area. Needless to say, during the frantic twenty minutes of my domestic ministrations, a lioness had entered the clearing and attacked the drinking giraffe. She hadn't managed to kill it, but Col explained that there had been a magnificent scuffle before the lioness decided the giraffe was simply too large to bother with and had slunk off into the dark. I arrived back at the bench just at the anticlimactic point where all the animals were calmly drinking water or waiting quietly in the wings. Hardly mind-blowing stuff. We waited a further hour but nothing else occurred except for a build-up of nervous gas in my tummy. Such are the joys and tribulations of wildlife photography. 

Beyond the Wild: Omaruru to Swakopmund

By the following morning, my hair was standing up in stiff, dust-encrusted tufts, and I sported that puffy visage that inevitably follows a severe deficit of sleep. We finally left Etosha after 9 a.m., sad to see the end of our time there as we both rated it as the best game park experience we’d had. The previous night, I had bashed open the fresh coconut I’d brought along and we’d enjoyed pieces of coconut flesh with our supper. Col had warned me that coconut was an effective natural laxative—and he was proved correct the next morning with the family frantically fighting for a perch on the only loo. After stopping for lunch, we ended up staying the night at the Omaruru Rest Resort. The owner, a friendly man named Errol McCullough, informed us he’d just purchased the property after renting it from the local council for three years. The resort bar was constructed from cane, and featured a large pool table in the centre of the room flanked by tables and chairs. Gemsbok steak and chips was the culinary fare of the day. We were shown to our room, which contained a couple of bunk beds and an old air-conditioning unit that stubbornly warmed the air up rather than cooling it down. Tim and Col took a long walk along the dry, sandy riverbed later that afternoon to look for birds, while I enjoyed a hot shower and washed my hair—which still stood out in stiff, clean, wavy ripples afterwards. I took a short walk, collecting interesting seed pods and a spot of birdwatching, successfully locating a crimson-breasted shrike, a bearded woodpecker, and a mousebird. I enjoyed a long chat with Errol and his bird-like assistant, Sigi. She kindly offered to let us rent her private apartment in Swakopmund the following night for a few hundred Rands, which sounded far too good a deal to pass up. She explained it was located close to the beach and the tourist information centre. We met a pleasant Canadian couple at the bar—only the husband was German and the wife was American, which sounds Irish. I mean to say they currently resided in Canada. The wife was a passionate birdwatcher and we discussed the possibilities of spotting a red-billed francolin, indigenous to the region. We joined them and Errol for an evening stroll along the dry riverbed just outside the town boundary. It felt a bit scary on foot after Col spotted fresh leopard tracks stamped into the soft sand. 
After consuming a typically high-cholesterol breakfast—the kind so popular with country folk—Col and I chatted with the Canadian couple and then set off for Swakopmund, after bidding farewell to Errol. The dramatic change in the scenery was becoming increasingly noticeable as we drove; it was significantly drier. Massive mountains of shifting orange sand dunes rose up on either side of the highway, reaching heights of nearly 7,000 feet. After locating the local ‘Academy of Learning’; a thin woman wearing a threadbare cardigan and sagging jowls showed us the way to Sigi’s flat. We entered the property after a prolonged series of unlocking heavy deadbolts and undoing thick security chains, which did nothing to reassure us of the general safety of the neighbourhood. Noting our worried expressions, the woman quickly reassured us that the locks were merely precautionary. The flat was small but adequate for our needs. Tim was relegated to sleeping on the lounge couch. The television set was an ancient black-and-white model that appeared to be permanently tuned to a fuzzy, static area located somewhere north of Jupiter. Swakopmund is a town of startling contrasts, where the scorched orange dunes of the Namib Desert collide abruptly with the icy, slate-grey waters of the Atlantic Ocean. It often felt less like an African coastal town and more like a traditional Bavarian village that had been whimsically transplanted onto the Skeleton Coast, remaining permanently blanketed in a thick shroud of sea fog. It was a highly popular beach resort, characterised by beautiful 19th-century German colonial architecture. The city was founded in 1892 as a main harbour for German South West Africa. Some of the municipal roads were of the wide, cobbled variety, with a lot of German styled hotels and traditional beer bars operating around the centre. After enjoying a quick lunch we set off in the car towards the famous ‘moonscape’, where the welwitschia plants are found. These interesting plants have just two leaves that grow and grow into huge, tattered fronds. We had acquired a permit earlier with a plan of the route. The landscape, as the name suggests, was a flat, brown, lunar-like area. There were red and yellow lichen bushes dotted along the side of the road as well as ink and dollar bushes and a few acacia thickets. Tim found an abandoned tank half-submerged in the sand with old, rusted billy cans—all remnants of the First World War. A few wagon wheel markings were etched into the reddish sand and remained virtually untouched, as there was no wind or human intervention to remove them (aside from tourists who, thankfully, appeared to leave things undisturbed). The amazing Welwitschia plant is a living fossil, unique to Namibia and Angola; it is found nowhere else in the world and has been recorded as reaching up to 2,000 years of age. A couple of juvenile red beetles and an adult red-and-black beetle—species that only exist on the Welwitschia— were clinging to the leaves. Col decided to photograph the beetles with his macro lens while I sat in the car. It was an interesting sight watching him move around on his knees, chasing a minute red beetle around on the hot sand while simultaneously trying to keep his huge lens trained on the scurrying insect. Col found, not surprisingly, that he couldn't keep up with it. I vaguely registered the sudden cessation of activity but thought nothing of it until Col rejoined me in the car where he sat staring mournfully at the vast landscape for a few minutes. I asked what was wrong and whether he had managed to photograph the beetle. ‘Yes,’ Col replied sadly. He had, ‘but the silly insect didn’t keep still long enough for a good shot.’ I sensed a ‘but’ coming and he confessed he’d been forced to take matters into his own hands. The beetle had come to a sudden, tragic demise at the receiving end of his tripod, rendering it still enough to allow a close-up photograph—but Col was saddened that he’ been ‘forced’ to kill it. He had sand-burned kneecaps for his pains. The oldest welwitschia is found in this area, cordoned off, but a wooden lookout allows a good view, and it's possible to photograph the plant over the ropes. I made tuna surprise for supper; the surprise was that it was edible. The next day, Col left for Walvis Bay and returned after a very pleasant boat trip, where he had seen bottlenose dolphins, Heaviside’s and dusky dolphins, as well as Wilson’s storm petrels. Later, we drove to Walvis Bay and beyond to the big dunes. We parked and went for a long walk through the valley and up to the highest dune we could find—all for Tim’s benefit of course. It was quite a climb but we traversed its edge to make it a bit less arduous, keeping a collective eye out for the notorious sidewinder as we climbed. There were many large brown beetles scurrying around and prickly bushes with small, yellow Nama melons dotted around the base of the dunes. Col was poking at various holes in search of geckos and lizards, while Tim and I collected an armful of yellow stones, Nama melons, pieces of rock, a petrified steenbok turd and a shell which must have been hundreds of years old, given that we were quite far from the sea. We had supper at Kucki’s, a popular pub serving really good grub, especially seafood. I let the side down a bit by ordering a steak and then sending it back to the kitchen to be cooked a bit more (I generally don’t like my meat to crawl around the plate). The meal was tasty and well presented. The only other occupants were a very noisy German couple who shouted in guttural tones throughout the entire meal, much to our irritation. As they bellowed on, Col leaned over to me and whispered conspiratorially, ‘Never mind the noisy buggers, they didn’t win the war,’ which tickled me no end—especially as the next couple to sit at their table were quintessentially British. ‘We must have the prawns, Richard, the pound’s doing extraordinarily well’. Afterwards Col drove back to the Walvis dunes armed with a torch to search for the ‘lady of the night’ crab, or ghost crab—huge nocturnal crabs that can be seen scuttling around the dunes as soon as it gets dark. 
I got into bed with a book and the noise started right on cue; loud, thumping sounds of distorted bass emanating from the nearby café. I knew we were in for one of those long, sleepless nights that did not bode well for the journey ahead. I turned to Tim and joked, ‘Oh well, as Palm said to Olive, “that’s Lifebuoy!”’ He looked puzzled but I was far too tired to explain the old pun which rather ruined the delivery. The gate at the side of the house sounded like the wail of a Madagascan indri, squeaking continuously as we went in and out to pack the car before setting off for the Namib-Naukluft Park. We drove past Walvis Bay to Paaltjies, where hundreds of greater and lesser flamingoes waded the salt pans. Col suddenly took off, camera at the ready and sprinted towards the startled birds in order to get a good photograph of them in flight. The scenery changed drastically from bare, arid desert to scrub into a more mountainous area dotted with low acacias. Although it felt as though we had been driving all day, we only got as far as Solitaire by four in the afternoon. It was an unexpectedly interesting title spot. As the name implies, there were a only a few buildings and very little else—just a one-stop store where most provisions could be bought with an old petrol pump standing outside the dusty front building. A row of outside loos lay to one side and a little orange orchard where ducks and geese were pottering around alongside a group of albino guinea fowl. Col spotted some rosy-faced lovebirds and called me over to look; they were beautiful, with a striking lime-green and salmon-pink colouring. The car was filled at the pump using an antiquated crank handle before setting off towards the Naukluft Mountains, whilst keeping our collective eye out for the Hartmann’s mountain zebra. Col had promised Tim a Godzilla model if he spotted one, which naturally prompted a great many phantom sightings from the back seat. He had to keep his promise, though, for as soon as we turned the corner, there were two of them standing right by the roadside. They must have been semi-tame, as one of the zebras came right up to the fence and stood so close he could be tickled on the cheek. They were very frisky; full of energy, jumping up and down and biting each other playfully on the ears and legs. It was a wonderful ending to the Namib side of the holiday and since Tim was getting his Godzilla, everyone was happy. We reached Maltahöhe about an hour and a half later, a village in the southern-central area of Namibia close to the Swartrand escarpment. It had been established in 1899 by Henning von Burgsdorff, who named the place after his wife, Malta. It was quite dark by now, so we drove around slowly looking for petrol and a place to eat and lay our weary heads. The whole village was fast asleep however. As we drove through the semi-deserted town we witnessed an amazing sight; a huge meteorite burning a bright blue and yellow streak across the sky to our right. The sunset had been spectacular that evening as well. We pushed on to Mariental, which had been founded in 1912 as a railway stop between Windhoek and Keetmanshoop and named after Maria, the wife of the first colonial settler of the area, Hermann Brandt. I loved the idea of all these old German officers naming outposts after their wives and asked Col if he’d ever name a place after me. He looked at me for a minute and said he needed a wee and could I order him a beer. I guess that was a no then. We stopped at a Wimpy, thankfully open. If there was one thing I regretted not bringing on this holiday, it was a proper alarm clock (this being well before smartphones). Although we managed to sleep until about 3 a.m., we kept checking the time on our watches every few minutes for fear of oversleeping. The result was a fitful doze, before finally giving up around four to continue on towards Keetmanshoop. We chose the windiest spot in Namibia for our repast. It was a situation that demanded a great deal of humour and as both Col and I kept bashing our heads on the metal poles of the rest stop roof, our patience was sorely tested. I was desperate for some tea so quickly assembled the makings on the cement table. I lit the gas stove and poured our precious water into the little tin container I’d used for cooking. It hissed encouragingly but ran out of gas long before Col and Tim had finished guzzling their breakfast. The result was a barely tepid brew that lacked anything resembling a proper tea flavour, so I threw the lot away in disgust and remained tea-less and grumpy, as the dark stain of my failed beverage seeped slowly into the sand at my feet. We drove on to Keetmanshoop in a stony, irritable silence, whilst I berated Col for not allowing me the time to brew a good cup of tea, while he in turn tried to fathom the urgent necessity for it. We stopped at a petrol station, and I spotted a takeaway attached to the shop, and nipped in for the tannin beverage I so desperately needed. Col entered a few minutes later and found me slurping happily at my insulated mug. The sheer humour of our situation suddenly hit me. The three of us looked like a family of dropouts after a rough night on the streets. Tim presented with a filthy, torn T-shirt, shorts, dusty boots and a spiky crest for a hairdo. Col walked in wearing a dirty, dusty jacket with a broken zip, stiff hair, a two-inch stubble on his chin and brandishing a bright blue toothbrush proudly from his top pocket. The overall impression did not improve when he emptied a massive, chaotic pile of coins onto the counter to pay for the juice and tea. The shop attendant carefully counted out the pile of small change, pausing every now and then to look up suspiciously at Col before resuming the count. I just looked like a bag lady who had recently discovered three inches of whisky left at the bottom of a discarded bottle. I took over the driving from Grünau to the border where we cruised through customs in only twenty minutes. We were only three hours from Cape Town but we decided to stretch the holiday and stay the night in the picturesque town of Lamberts Bay on the West Coast. Lamberts Bay is rich in history and famous for its moderate climate, miles of pristine beaches and prolific birdlife. It started out as a humble fishing village, but it has become one of the major tourist attractions on the coast due to its year-round weather and Bird Island. The island itself, which is almost three hectares in size, is connected to the mainland via a concrete breakwater. It’s an important breeding and roosting site for Cape gannets and cormorants and Cape fur seals can often be seen basking across the island’s rocky shores. The sea was a clear aquamarine. We decided to splash out and eat dinner at Muisboskerm, one of the original open-air seafood restaurants on the West Coast—‘the best original open-air seafood restaurant in South Africa’, according to their own advertising, though I’d certainly agree with the sentiment. I phoned ahead to reserve a ‘pew’. It was a rather quaint setup right at the sea’s edge, comprising a central wooden laager of rough-hewn wooden benches, bleached and salt-streaked by the ocean spray, set alongside heavy marble tables with a large braai area to one side. A long windbreak made of melkbos bushes surrounded the seating area and afforded privacy from the coastal road. The food was prepared on-site in three large ovens that overlooked the surf. To one side were three separate toilets constructed from bamboo and covered grass. Our hosts were Edward and Elmarien and they explained in heavy Afrikaans accents that they prepared the multi-course meal in stages and that we were to help ourselves to ‘snacks’ of raw and smoked fish in the meantime. As the evening wore on, the long trestle table near the cooking area slowly filled up with various traditional fish dishes, including yellowtail and snoek, as well as sweet and boiled potatoes, tomato and onion salad, cucumber, pot bread and a rich, farm-style butter. Eating utensils were not provided, as guests used empty mussel shells for scooping up the food which I thought was rather quaint until I managed to pour a large dollop of apricot jam straight down my T-shirt. There was a surprising number of international tourists present, considering the out-of-the-way location and the fact that the atmosphere was very Afrikaans orientated. Or perhaps that was exactly why they came. The lamb was cooked perfectly on the spit and brought out as the pièce de résistance towards the end of the first course. Sometime during the course of the evening, I had cause to visit one of the bamboo-walled toilets. A quick glance into the toilet bowl before I sat down revealed a classic ‘phantom floater’—you know the kind, the stubborn specimen that simply refuses to go away no matter how many times you flush. It is deeply embarrassing when it happens to be your own, but somehow infinitely more horrifying when it belongs to a total stranger. I tried everything to make this one disappear. I flushed and desperately poked and prodded using a long piece of reed extracted carefully from the grass thatch of the roof— to no avail. It remained firmly in place. The worst thing was I was certain the longer I remained barricaded in the loo, the more people would be gathering in a queue outside. They would eventually enter, discover the phantom floater and instantly assume it was mine, staring accusingly at me for the rest of the night while I tried to enjoy my raw herring and apricot jam. With this dreadful social nightmare spurring me afresh, I poked with renewed, frantic vigour until the offending object finally disappeared down the pipe. Reputation intact, I thankfully washed and dried my hands, giving one last cursory glance at the toilet bowl as I prepared to leave— only to nearly fall over in fright. It was back again, bobbing malevolently in the recently flushed water. I gave an involuntary whimper, carefully unrolled several metres of toilet paper and feverishly shoved the entire wad over the offending excrement and bolted for the door. I carefully opened the bamboo door and peered myopically out into the darkness. Seeing no one, I took my chance and stepped out. Just as I clicked the door shut behind me, a female voice from the shadows enquired, ‘Is this the ladies?’ causing me to jump guiltily. I put my head down, mumbled in a disguised, deep voice that the toilet next door was vacant and escaped quickly back to our table. Col looked up from his animated chat with a Dutch couple and asked what on earth had taken me so long. He looked confused when I sat down and whispered with great, shuddering emphasis: ‘Floaters!’ 
It became cold quite suddenly and none of us were dressed appropriately for the coastal night wind, so we bid our newly made friends farewell and headed back to our accommodation and thankfully into a warm bed.

The following morning, I took a slow stroll down to Bird Island. There were hundreds of Cape gannets nesting as well as African oystercatchers. Gannets are highly social birds, breeding in incredibly dense colonies and exhibiting elaborate courtship rituals. These territorial displays include head-bobbing, mutual bill-fencing and sky-pointing to strengthen the pair bonds. When returning to the nest site, gannets perform elaborate greeting rituals, with the males shaking their heads rapidly from side to side and the females offering their nape for gentle nibbling. It was fascinating to watch, so I spent some time at the viewing hide before heading back. We checked out at mid-morning for the final drive back to Cape Town. Col, true to form, insisted on stopping at the local municipal dumpsite before we left town just to take a few close-up photographs of a shiny green beetle sitting on a flower—only Col. We jerked and juddered our way into Table View, stuck in top gear - at least it got us home.
