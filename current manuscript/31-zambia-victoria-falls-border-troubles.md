# Chapter 28: Zambia: Victoria Falls Border Troubles

We were to travel again, a mere handful of days later, due to a last-minute decision to have a Mostert family meet-up. John’s carer drove us to the airport where we sat having coffee at the Mugg and Bean (renamed ‘The Mug n Bug’ by Col because he once had a fly in his drink). I got the wrong milk in my coffee, Tim got the wrong drink and Col’s coffee was actually a hot chocolate.

A small BA plane would fly us to Vic Falls. Tim earlier downloaded a Google map to see how to get to Hwange from the falls. He’s now of an age where his knowledge of all matters relating to technology far surpasses ours. Instead of being the ‘supplement’ in our vacations, he has moved up to ‘addition’ status.

At Victoria Falls Airport I was given a few pages to fill in by the official when I finally made it to the front of the queue. I left the queue to collect and complete the information. This time she said the expiry date was wrong, and the passport number wasn’t clear. She said I couldn’t write the information in at the desk, I had to go away and stand in line again. I refused and continued to write it all there on her small desk (I can be stubborn when the occasion requires it). She said I had to rewrite the entire document as it was now too untidy. I noticed Tim and Col waiting impatiently for me so I lost my cool and told her I was going through. She had to chase after me to return my passport. I was red-faced with heat and irritation by now. In sharp contrast, we found the car hire people to be very helpful and welcoming. They even gave me some water after seeing my sweaty red face.

Col drove for an hour and a half until we came to the turn-off for The Ivory Lodge. There was a bit of a dirt track to traverse before we arrived. Inside the huge open-space lounge area we found Col’s mum, and the entire Mostert family. I only had time to change my T-shirt and we were off on a game drive. Cheetahs are the world’s fastest land animals, reaching over 70 mph in short bursts, thanks to their flexible spines, long legs, and semi-retractable claws for grip, but they overheat quickly. William had spotted a male.

Our guide told us that their unique ‘tear mark’ features are to reduce sun glare, that the spots are like fingerprints (no two are alike), and that they communicate with purrs and chirps instead of roars.

Just as the light was fading to a soft golden hue, Annie found a lion and there was much excitement. It was a white-maned male and he was lying in the dirt road. The ranger brought up the spotlight and pointed it directly onto the large maned lion so we could see him and take photos. Apparently he was one of two brothers; the other was recently found caught in a snare and later died from his wounds. I hate hearing about our wildlife dying needlessly like this and in pain — it’s an unfortunate fact that some humans are cruel.

The ranger tried to start the engine but it had died and after a few coughs and splutters, he radioed for help. Another lodge Range Rover appeared and they push-started our vehicle. I was shivering from cold by now and reluctantly parted with the blanket as we went directly in for supper.

A soft knock at the door announced a waiter and tea on a tray. It was very welcome and I slurped it gratefully before joining the rest of the family for an early morning drive. It was pouring with rain and we returned wet and cold, chatting in small groups while the children ran around letting off steam (literally and figuratively).

Tim discovered that he had dropped his iPhone somewhere between last night’s lion viewing and camp. He was so disappointed. It was soon time for afternoon tea followed by another game drive. Tim stayed behind with his cousins. As we drove over the corrugated sandy road, the driver spotted something. He stopped the truck and retrieved it from the dirt road. I couldn’t believe it when he brought Tim’s phone to us grinning widely. It had been rained on, driven over and probably a few hefty wild animals had walked over it as well but it appeared to be intact. As we headed back, we came across a couple of dancing crested cranes and a few ground hornbills with their distinctive red wattles. We had to stop a while at the Hwange gate as there were queues of tourists (I never count ourselves as tourists, as though by living on the continent that absolves me of the title). Tim couldn’t believe it when I handed him his phone, especially when I said we’d driven over it and it had rained all night. He went quickly to plug it in and power it up.

Tim charged his phone overnight and he switched it on that morning and it worked. I figured this had got to be the best advertisement for the iPhone.

Tim played mbau or ntsoro as it’s known here (also known as mancala). Two players take turns to play this strategy-based game. It is played using small stones, beans, or seeds and rows of holes or pits in the earth, on a board with holes or using other playing surfaces. Tim first learned this game in Malawi and has his own wooden bao board at home. He had taught William and James how to play over the last few days which occupied them for a while.

Tim went white-water rafting with his cousin Mark that day. He was very excited — me, not so much. I worried most of the day till he returned. He’s very capable but my ‘worry’ switch is permanently set to the ‘on’ position when it comes to my offspring.

The rest of us visited the nearby crocodile farm and I learned that the difference between male and female crocs lies in the size of their snout and the females have bigger bellies. Everyone decided to move from the camp to the hotel for the remaining two nights.

After a quick shower and change for dinner, we met the rest of the family at the bar before driving in three cars to The Boma for supper. This is an African-themed restaurant; an enormous thatch-roofed open-sided room with rows of long wooden tables and benches.

At the door, we had a different symbol painted onto our cheeks (mine was of a giraffe) and were given a length of colourfully designed chitenge cloth to wear over our shoulders for the duration of our meal. We had wine to start with then a delicious butternut soup that was served in small iron ‘potjie’ pots (round-bellied iron pots). Crocodile steak and guinea fowl or buffalo meat with salad and bread followed the soup course. I was the first to try eating a mopani worm — a thick white slug that is cooked to a crispy fat blob. It’s considered a delicacy by the locals. I ate it quickly and it wasn’t too bad if you don’t think too hard about what you are eating and immediately chase it down with a strong drink. I was given a certificate afterwards as proof of my bravery. This was followed by a drum session so I selected a tembe drum and we all tried to follow some kind of rhythm. Local dancers moved rhythmically to the drumming before we joined them for an impromptu dance and were given a tin mug of the local drink called ‘Chibuku’ afterwards to cool down.

Last night I dreamt I found a disembodied head in the pantry at work which left me wondering what was in that Chibuku.

After breakfast we got ready for a day in Zambia and took our passports with us before driving to the Zimbabwe side of the falls to walk across the border bridge into Zambia. The long bridge has two customs posts in the middle. The Victoria Falls looked different seen from this vantage point, but no less spectacular. There were long winding paths down to the falls and a fine spray kept us cool as we ventured down all the paths. Zimbabwe was clearly visible from the other side and a huge rainbow spanned the divide in glorious colours.

I waded in to just shy of a metre from where the river plunged into the gorge; a popular activity with the locals is to see how close they can get to the edge of the waterfall without being swept away. I can think of more fun things to do; removing my toenails with a pair of pliers maybe.

Touts were selling all manner of curios. I bought a wooden ‘nyaminyami’ key ring just to get them off my back. They are known as a river god or Zambezi snake spirit, to the Tonga living along the Zambezi River. The Nyami Nyami has the body of a snake and a fish head; it’s believed to protect the Tonga people and give them sustenance in difficult times. I also purchased a necklace from a man selling various pieces of stone, apparently made from the stone from the riverbed (probably came from the rocks in his garden but I believe in supporting the locals). Suddenly there was a clap of thunder followed by a downpour. A man on an old bicycle with a seat made of straw told us he’d ride us to the border for a few kwacha. We politely declined. As we reached the bridge, the rain slowed to a few drops. Forty-five minutes later we were over the border and the sun had come out again and was burning a hole on my head. Our hired car was sitting alone in the Falls car park as everyone else had left.

I had a crocodile tail for supper that night — tastes like chicken.

We exchanged Christmas presents that day at breakfast then packed the cars and travelled to the Gorges Hotel after saying goodbye to the family as they were returning to Zimbabwe and Sweden that day.

The Gorges Hotel is set above the steep gorge of the Zambezi River. It has a magnificent view. Tim showed us where they rafted yesterday and we all peered down at the raging river below and I shuddered at how turbulent the water was — though that’s the point apparently. I ordered a Malawi shandy (Mazoe orange juice, ginger beer and bitters and lemon) before leaving for the airport. Our plane was half an hour late then we had another delay because the toilets had been overfilled. When we eventually boarded, the aisle was damp with toilet water and some people had wet luggage. The pilot joked about needing life jackets if we needed the toilet. Just as we were taking off, my iPad and laptop went sailing down the aisle as I’d forgotten to close my bag properly. I had to undo my seatbelt and tear down the aisle to fetch them before they became contaminated with toilet water (I think it was too late though). I placed them under my seat and held onto them with my toes as we took off.

As we approached South Africa, the pilot, who clearly felt he should have been a comedian, said,

‘Welcome to…’ (theatrical whisper) ‘Where are we again? Oh yes, Joburg!’

Col decided to buy socks and underpants at the last minute and we nearly missed our connecting flight; he’s a hoot sometimes. A few days after we got home I discovered I had bilharzia so I phoned in sick and spent a few days in bed. Nothing like bringing home a little memento from your travels I always say. Actually I’ve never said that.
