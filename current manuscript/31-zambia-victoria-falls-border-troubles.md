# Chapter 28: Zambia: Victoria Falls Border Troubles

We were scheduled to travel again a mere handful of days later, due to a last-minute family decision to have a proper Mostert family reunion up north. John’s carer kindly drove us to the Cape Town airport, where the three of us sat having coffee at the Mugg & Bean restaurant—which had been permanently renamed ‘The Mug & Bug’ by Col because he had once discovered a stray fly floating in his drink there years ago. Naturally, the order went awry: I received entirely the wrong type of milk in my coffee, Tim received the wrong drink order altogether, and Col’s coffee turned out to be a hot chocolate. A small British Airways commercial plane flew our family straight to Victoria Falls. Earlier in the day, Tim had successfully downloaded a digital Google Map to see exactly how to navigate our way from the falls to Hwange National Park. He is now at an age where his practical knowledge of all matters relating to modern technology far surpasses our own. Instead of merely being the quiet ‘supplement’ on our family vacations, he has officially moved up to full ‘addition’ status. At the Victoria Falls Airport immigration hall, I was handed a few pages of official forms to fill in by the desk official when I finally made it to the absolute front of the snaking queue. I was forced to leave my spot in the line just to collect and complete the requested information on a side counter. When I returned to her desk, she looked at the paperwork and barked that the passport expiry date was wrong and claims the handwritten passport number wasn’t entirely clear. She informed me flatly that I could not write the corrections in at the counter desk, but had to go away and stand at the very back of the long line again! I flatly refused to lose my place, and continued to stubbornly write the information right there on her small desk counter (I can be incredibly stubborn when the occasion requires it). She then announced that I had to rewrite the entire document from scratch as it was now far too untidy for her liking. I noticed Tim and Col waiting impatiently for me past the barriers, so I entirely lost my cool and loudly informed her that I was going through regardless. She actually had to chase after my boots to return my passport. I was absolutely red-faced with heat and irritation by this stage. In sharp contrast, we found the car hire agents to be incredibly helpful and welcoming; they even handed me a bottle of cold water after seeing my sweaty, red face. Col drove our rental vehicle for an hour and a half until we finally came to the turn-off for the Ivory Lodge. There was a bit of a rough dirt track to traverse before we finally arrived at the main camp. Inside the massive, open-plan lounge area, we found Col’s mother waiting for us, alongside the entire extended Mostert family. I only had just enough time to change my T-shirt in our cabin before we were off on our very first afternoon game drive. 

Cheetahs and the Snared Lion 

During the drive, our guide, William, suddenly spotted a magnificent male cheetah (Acinonyx jubatus) resting in the shade. Cheetahs are famously the world's fastest land mammals, capable of reaching speeds over 70 mph (112 km/h) in short, explosive bursts. This incredible velocity is anatomically supported by a highly flexible spine, elongated leg bones, and semi-retractable claws that function like running cleats for ground grip. However, due to the immense metabolic energy expended, they overheat exceptionally quickly and can only sustain high-speed chases for short distances. Our guide informed our vehicle that the cheetah's unique facial ‘tear marks’ function primarily to reduce the blinding glare of the African sun, that their coat spots are entirely unique like human fingerprints (no two individuals possess the exact same pattern), and that they communicate socially using vocal purrs and high-pitched chirps instead of roaring. Just as the afternoon light was fading to a soft, beautiful golden hue across the savannah, Annie suddenly spotted a wild lion, creating much excitement across the seats. It was a magnificent, white-maned adult male, and he was lying flat in the middle of the dirt road. The ranger brought up the vehicle's high-powered spotlight and pointed the beam directly onto the large maned lion so we could all see him clearly and take photographs. The tracker whispered sadly that this male was actually one of two local brothers; his brother had recently been found caught in an illegal wire poacher's snare in the park, and had tragically died from his infected wounds days later. I absolutely hate hearing about our African wildlife dying needlessly and in agonizing pain like that—it is an unfortunate, tragic fact of life that some humans are incredibly cruel. The ranger tried to start the engine, but it had died; after a few coughs and splutters, he radioed for help. Another lodge Range Rover appeared and they push-started our vehicle. I was shivering from the cold by then and reluctantly parted with the blanket as we went directly in for supper. A soft knock at the door announced a waiter with tea on a tray. It was very welcome and I slurped it gratefully before joining the rest of the family for an early morning drive. It was pouring with rain, and we returned wet and cold, chatting in small groups while the children ran around letting off steam—literally and figuratively. Tim discovered that he had dropped his iPhone somewhere between the previous night's lion viewing and camp. He was so disappointed. It was soon time for afternoon tea followed by another game drive; Tim stayed behind with his cousins. As we drove over the corrugated sandy road, the driver spotted something. He stopped the truck and retrieved it from the dirt track. I couldn't believe it when he brought Tim’s phone to us, grinning widely. It had been rained on, driven over, and probably walked over by a few hefty wild animals, but it appeared to be intact. As we headed back, we came across a couple of dancing crested cranes and a few ground hornbills with their distinctive red wattles. We had to stop for a while at the Hwange gate as there were queues of tourists (I never count ourselves as tourists, as though living on the continent absolves me of the title). Tim couldn't believe it when I handed him his phone, especially when I told him we had driven over it and it had rained all night. He went quickly to plug it in and power it up. Tim charged his phone overnight, and when he switched it on the following morning, it worked. I figured this had to be the best advertisement for the iPhone. Tim played Mbau, or Ntsoro as it is known here (also known as Mancala). Two players take turns to play this strategy-based game using small stones, beans, or seeds, and rows of holes or pits in the earth, on a board, or using other surfaces. Tim first learned this game in Malawi and has his own wooden Bau board at home. He taught William and James how to play over the last few days, which occupied them for a while. Tim went white-water rafting with his cousin Mark today. He was very excited—me, not so much. I worried most of the day until he returned. He is very capable, but my ‘worry’ switch is permanently set to the ‘on’ position when it comes to my offspring. The rest of us visited the nearby crocodile farm, where I learned that the difference between male and female crocodiles lies in the size of their snout, and that the females have bigger bellies. Everyone decided to move from the camp to the hotel for the remaining two nights. After a quick shower and change for dinner, we met the rest of the family at the bar before driving in three cars to ‘The Boma’ for supper. This was an African-themed restaurant: an enormous, thatch-roofed, open-sided room with rows of long wooden tables and benches. At the door, we each had a different symbol painted onto our cheeks (mine was a giraffe) and were given a length of colourfully designed Chitenge cloth to wear over our shoulders for the duration of the meal. We had wine to start, then a delicious butternut soup served in small iron potjie pots. Crocodile steak and guinea fowl or buffalo meat with salad and bread followed the soup course. I was the first to try eating a mopani worm—a thick, white slug cooked into a crispy, fat blob. It is considered a delicacy by the locals. I ate it quickly, and it wasn't too bad if you didn't think too hard about what you were eating and immediately chased it down with a strong drink. I was given a certificate afterwards as proof of my bravery. This was followed by a drum session; I selected a djembe drum, and we all tried to follow a rhythm. Local dancers moved rhythmically to the drumming before we joined them for an impromptu dance and were given a tin mug of the local drink called ‘Chibuku’ afterwards to cool down. Last night, I dreamt I found a disembodied head in the pantry at work, which left me wondering what was in that Chibuku. 

Across the Border 

After breakfast, we prepared for a day in Zambia, taking our passports with us before driving to the Zimbabwe side of the falls to walk across the border bridge. The long bridge has two customs posts in the middle. The Victoria Falls looked different seen from this vantage point, but were no less spectacular. There were long, winding paths down to the falls, and a fine spray kept us cool as we ventured down. Zimbabwe was clearly visible from the other side, and a huge rainbow spanned the divide in glorious colours. I waded in to just shy of a metre from where the river plunged into the gorge; a popular activity with the locals is to see how close they can get to the edge of the waterfall without being swept away. I can think of more fun things to do—removing my toenails with a pair of pliers, perhaps. Touts were selling all manner of curios. I bought a wooden nyaminyami key ring just to get them off my back. These are known as a River God or Zambezi snake spirit to the Tonga living along the Zambezi River. The Nyami Nyami has the body of a snake and a fish head; it is believed to protect the Tonga people and give them sustenance in difficult times. I also purchased a necklace from a man selling various pieces of stone, apparently made from riverbed rock (it probably came from the rocks in his garden, but I believe in supporting the locals). Suddenly, there was a clap of thunder followed by a downpour. A man on an old bicycle with a seat made of straw told us he would ride us to the border for a few Kwacha. We politely declined. As we reached the bridge, the rain slowed to a few drops. Forty-five minutes later we were over the border, and the sun had come out again, burning a hole in my head. Our hired car was sitting alone in the Falls car park as everyone else had left. I had a crocodile tail for supper that night—it tasted like chicken. 

The Gorges and the Humid Flight 

We exchanged Christmas presents at breakfast then packed the cars and travelled to the Gorges Hotel after saying goodbye to the family, as they were returning to Zimbabwe and Sweden. The Gorges Hotel is set above the steep gorge of the Zambezi River and has a magnificent view. Tim showed us where they had rafted yesterday; we all peered down at the raging river below, and I shuddered at how turbulent the water was—though I suppose that was the point. I ordered a Malawi shandy (Mazoe orange juice, ginger beer, bitters, and lemon) before leaving for the airport. Our plane was half an hour late, and then we had another delay because the toilets had been overfilled. When we eventually boarded, the aisle was damp with toilet water and some passengers had wet luggage. The pilot joked about needing life jackets if we needed to use the facilities. Just as we were taking off, my iPad and laptop went sailing down the aisle, as I had forgotten to close my bag properly. I had to undo my seatbelt and tear down the aisle to fetch them before they became contaminated with toilet water (I think it was too late, though). I placed them under my seat and held onto them with my toes as we climbed. As we approached South Africa, the pilot, who clearly felt he should have been a comedian, said, ‘Welcome to...’ (he added a theatrical whisper), ‘Where are we again? Oh yes, Joburg!’ Col decided to buy socks and underpants at the last minute and we nearly missed our connecting flight; he’s a hoot sometimes. A few days after we got home, I discovered I had bilharzia, so I phoned in sick and spent a few days in bed. Nothing like bringing home a little memento from your travels, I always say. Actually, I have never said that.
