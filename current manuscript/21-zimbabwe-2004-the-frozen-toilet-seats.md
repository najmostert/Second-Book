# Chapter 18: Zimbabwe 2004: The Frozen Toilet Seats

En route to Zimbabwe by car, I booked us into a small apartment for an overnight stop. Early this morning, I stepped into five inches of water on the bathroom floor, which simultaneously froze my ankles and removed any lingering traces of sleep. Col grumbled that he'd suffered the same fate last night but added that as well as freezing his feet, the loose toilet seat had attached itself to his nether regions when he stood up! I laughed at the visual. The border was the usual experience in slow torture and tested my already flimsy patience to the max. We were descended upon by the sick, the wounded and the downright desperate after opening the door. Batting away these pests, we made our way to the queue and prepared to wait. A few touts approached us offering their services, which included the 'mentally challenging' process of filling in forms in English and answering questions such as, ‘Name, occupation and address’ etc.
We felt qualified to do this bit ourselves without the assistance of their ‘expert’ help, and denied assistance until one of them  convinced us that his help would speed up the process. So it was we found ourselves paying for the privilege of them standing in our personal space for two hours while Col laboriously entered the details of his many cameras and equipment. The South African side had the same people informing us of the procedure for crossing the border. I received several kicks to the shin from Col when I heard we had to add carbon tax to the already hefty payment. I’d already endeared myself to him from the start by hooting at the Police Stop. My excuse was that my finger slipped.

Later we stopped for breakfast at the Elephant and Tusker Hotel, an aging beauty of erstwhile years. The boma was empty save for one rather dour family who barely spoke to each other but barked orders at the hapless waiter, who wore a bright green jacket three sizes too small for him. The Bumi River was a distant memory, now buried under pale orange sand. I had sent three texts at the border; two found their target, but one was still hovering somewhere in cyberspace. We left Bumi and hared off towards Harare — a six-hour drive away. The roads were in a terrible state of disrepair compared to 1997, with signs as empty of information as the lanes were empty of cars. Petrol was hard to come by, and it was not uncommon to find lines of cars a kilometre long outside the few working stations. Luckily we had filled up in Beitbridge across the border, where petrol was more easily available. Dry deciduous trees lined the dusty roads; the msasa trees were devoid of their usual pretty orange until September, and the grass was a dry, pale auburn. Few people walked the highways, but those who did looked dejected — quiet despair written on their faces. These sorry-looking people were a product of Mugabe’s tyrannical rule. After nearly four decades of (mis)rule he had brought a once-rich nation to economic ruin by forcing the takeover of white-owned farms at the end of the 1990s, which devastated agriculture and led to hyperinflation. The occasional lonely figure waited at derelict bus stops — not so much for transport, which was unlikely to arrive, as in hope of something better.

Our first forced stop went without incident, despite Col’s misgivings and audible groan when we spotted the police presence. This was probably more because he wasn’t sure of how I’d respond, than the police themselves. I’m not good with abusive authority as you’ll know by now. It’s generally accepted that you suck it in and do what they ask but I find my brain has not received that memo. As we arrived in Harare, having come through a really grotty part of town, we were astounded at the state of general degradation since we were here last. There were people everywhere, walking the streets, selling, talking, standing in groups, vendors selling oranges from makeshift boxes. In other words a typical African scene.

We were staying with Cecilia and Andy. Col visited the bank to get some Zim dollars. The notes all had a minimum of five zeros after whatever denomination it was and we had a few ‘trillion dollar’ notes that were practically worthless. Col and I went off to Mukuvisi, a small game park nearby. Our guide was Ben and he took us on an hour and a half bush walk where we came across giraffe, sable antelope and some zebra. Ben chatted non-stop as we trampled through the golden grass; the town of Harare a purple backdrop in the distance. Ben told us a little about Bob Mugabe’s wife Grace. She was his secretary first and used to be married until her husband disappeared under mysterious circumstances. She was known as ‘Gucci Grace’ due to her propensity for jetting off to Europe on shopping sprees, funded by the government. We murmured our surprise at this audacity then returned to join the others outside for some wine and a chat. Col, Tim and I headed off to Ewanrigg gardens for the morning, which is approximately 38 kilometres from Harare. It is renowned for its extensive collection of aloes and cycads. En route we were stopped at a police block and asked if we had a car radio. Luckily we didn’t. That would have elicited a fine of $10 without the accompanying licence which is also difficult to obtain. I later joined Cecilia for a trip to a supermarket. The shelves were not at all well stocked; most shelves were completely empty. It was a sad situation. Cecilia told us that their friends took turns to enter South Africa to do a load of shopping and take orders for each other. She told me she kept small denomination notes under the sun visor for police stop bribes.

Journey to Mana Pools

We were up early to prepare the cars for our trip to Mana Pools and got there at 5 p.m. and paid a hefty fee as we were from South Africa. Mana Pools means ‘four pools’ in the local Shona language. It’s a massive wildlife sanctuary that straddles the Zambezi river. It has abundant game and camp sites. Mana is also a proclaimed World Heritage Site.
We had a campsite and immediately spotted some female elephants on the way in. The campsite itself was also well laid out and pleasant with the tall mahogany, fig and acacia trees providing adequate shade. It was full of vervet monkeys scampering all over the place, so we would have to be extra vigilant with our provisions. Just as I was thinking this, a monkey jumped through the open window of the Toyota and stole a toilet roll.
It was getting dark, so we quickly set up our two tents. We made 'spam bol' (our take on spaghetti bolognese) for supper and ate overlooking the Zambezi River from the campsite. Cecilia regaled us with tales of hyena antics from the previous trip and Tim said he could see hundreds of eyes peering out of the dark at us. Col turned on the Toyota headlights which revealed a herd of impala staring back at us and went some way to appeasing him. We crawled into our tents early, but sleep was difficult for me with all the sounds of wildlife; hyenas howling, hippo grunts, monkeys chirruping and, in the wee hours, the guttural growl of a lion. I loved it though and it’s these very sounds that I miss most when I’m not in the bush.

First Full Day in Mana

I emerged from our tent with aching bones and wild hair to start a fire for tea. The rest of the family emerged a bit later, grumbling about a lack of sleep and a fear of some of the more alarming sounds in the night. A lone bull hippo was strolling on the periphery of the camp towards the river. Hippos are known for their aggressive and dangerous behaviour and have killed any unfortunate person found to be in their way.
After tea in tin mugs, we battened down the hatches as best we could (these being tents, were canvas flaps and not very secure) and set off for an early game drive.
There was a variety of animals: monkeys, impala, silver-backed jackals, and waterbuck with their distinctive ‘wet paint toilet seat’ bottoms. We also saw elephant—one huge bull with a tusk missing—many hornbills, open-billed storks, saddle-billed storks, more hippos, painted storks, eland, kudu, zebra, crocs, and even a python curled up near the edge of the river. It was a great morning's viewing and we headed back to camp for pan-toasted bread and tea, in good spirits. I noticed a buffalo lurking not far from our tents and eyed him as warily as he eyed us. Buffalo are equally dangerous when confronted. Col gave Tim his old camera, and he stood next to Col, zooming in and out and photographing whatever his dad was photographing, like two peas in a pod. I had a lovely hot bath in the ablutions block before setting off for an afternoon drive around 3 p.m. where we saw a lot of what we saw this morning, then headed back to camp for sundowners overlooking the Zambezi River with hippos snorting their amusement.
Steak cooked over the fire with toasted bread for supper, and a glass of still-chilled wine. This time, I decided to join Cecilia in her tent and leave Tim and Col in the other one. The night sounds of hyenas, hippos, nightjars, and rutting impala were soothing, and I drifted off early.
Two buffalo had wandered into camp this morning—one stood just outside the ablutions block, which made washing a little difficult. I took a really circuitous route and then ran in quickly.

Hyenas, Buffalo, and Tsetse Flies

Last night, I heard noises close by and got up to peer out of the tent to find a huge hyena at the camp table. The smell of barbecued meat was the attraction. There were many other nocturnal noises: hyenas giggling, lions roaring, buffalo bellowing, and grunting hippos to name a few. I was woken again a few hours later by some loud rustling sounds and peered from the tent flap at a beautiful male eland wading through the silver, moonlit water. He silently left the river and stood close to the boys' tent, munching on leaves. It was a wonderful sight and I went back to my sleeping bag feeling happy.
I woke up early and tried to head for the ablution block, but my favourite buffalo was there guarding the entrance. I had to wait till he moved a little way from the door to dart in and wash.
This morning’s drive was not pleasant, as I was bitten excessively by tsetse flies, and my left hand and arm were swollen and painfully itchy. The tsetse fly is a dangerous insect that can spread trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) if severely bitten.
Breakfast was taken at a pan overlooking the Zambezi, where hippos frolicked noisily for our entertainment. Back at camp we found the campsite had been raided by monkeys. They had torn the big tent by dragging the tent peg backwards, leaving it with two huge holes. The smaller tent had a tear along the front flap as well. Cecilia and I repaired the damage with black duct tape. Cecilia told one of the camp cleaners about the monkeys tearing our tents. Unfortunately, he had the IQ of an amoeba and just stared at her open-mouthed, so not a lot would be done about it. We had seen several leguaans along the pan today, looking like prehistoric creatures.
My period had started so, feeling bloated and irritable, I got up around 5 a.m. feeling like a piece of chum bait walking across to the ablution block by the light of a pink Barbie torch, whose beam gave the name ‘penlight’ new meaning. My buffalo wasn’t here but I kept glancing around at dark shapes and twinkling eyes with some trepidation, waiting for some hungry beast to pounce. We removed anything of value from the tents this morning in case the monkeys or baboons wanted another go at ferreting for food. After a fairly uneventful drive, we returned to camp to find that the disgruntled baboons had crapped their annoyance at finding no food. Faecal matter perched down the top and sides of our tents. A family of ‘pumbas’ were scattered in mounds all over camp, as well as the ubiquitous, ablution-guarding buffalo.
Col went off to try his luck with the bee-eaters again while Cecilia and I cleaned off the tents and tidied up before preparing supper. I had trouble with my fire-lighting skills, so Col finished off. We had bacon and scrambled egg for lunch with a three-bean salad and crackers—good camp cuisine. During the bacon-frying process, I had a feeling I was being watched and turned around to find a huge hyena under a fig tree; he was hunched over, salivating at the possibility of some rashers. I shouted loudly in fright and he actually took flight, skulking off to await his chances from the nearby bushes. We had another sundowner over the river before retiring to our tents for the cacophony of evening sounds. Luckily there was no sign of the hyena.

The Journey Back

One of the park officials asked if he could get a lift with us to Karoi. We had a bit of game spotting en route to the main gate but didn’t see much besides wild piggies, a few elephant, and some zebra. We left Mana Pools park and travelled along the red, dusty murram roads in search of some baobab fruit.
The ‘soda bicarb’ fruit of the baobab tree, also known as ‘monkey fruit’, has a tough outer shell. Inside, kidney-shaped seeds are set in a dry pulp. Baobab powder has a sharp citrus flavour similar to sherbet. We found several pods on the ground and had a taste of the sweet-sour delicacy.
We set off again, swerving over dry rivulets of the dirt road until Col passed us in the other vehicle. This was untimely, as shortly afterwards, we had a blowout and the rear left wheel was in ribbons. Both Cecilia and I had to get out in ‘lion’ country to change the huge tyre. It was not an easy process due to the size and the weight of the Toyota. Her 4x4 was relatively new, so she had no idea how to change the tyre. We consulted the guidebook, but it seemed to be written in ancient Greek. Luckily, I had some knowledge based on our tyre changes in Saudi so we found the winch to bring down the new tyre from underneath the Land Cruiser's belly. However, it took us a lot longer to work out how to release the jack from its vice-held grip in the back. I crawled underneath the car, spitting dust, until I saw the winch chain, and I was in the process of guiding Cecilia through the procedure when a succession of 4x4s slowed down and asked if we needed help. I asked one of the drivers if he would try to catch up with Col and ask him to come back to us. They agreed and roared off. Cecilia and I managed to get the tyre off, then I had a nervous wee in front of the back door of the Toyota while Cecilia kept an eye out for predators. We were just trying to work out where to place the jack when Col arrived in a skitter of stones and dust. He and Amos, the guide, completed the tyre-changing process. I was leaning over in a crouched position, showing Col where the jack should go and placed my hand on the ground for balance and encountered a very damp spot and realised too late that it was exactly where I wee’d a few minutes before. After finding some wet wipes for my hands, we continued towards Karoi and stopped at a motel for tea and sarmies. And in my case, to wash my hands properly. As we were munching on sandwiches, Col told us that Amos, after learning Col was a doctor, began telling him stories of woe regarding his prowess, or rather lack of it, in the bedroom. He declared that he needed some pills for his ‘backbone’ (a euphemism for his replicator applicator, we assumed). Col said he gave him some vitamins and told him that would do the trick. We chuckled at this for a while and Tim asked what he meant by backbone. ‘His willy,’ I stated simply. ‘Oh,’ said Tim and took another bite of his sandwich.

Towards the Bvumba Mountains

Col packed the car, which was looking distinctly heavier than when we arrived. We bid a fond farewell to all the kids before they left for school, and then to Andy and Cecilia. It was only 7:30 a.m. when we set off, but we had a long way to go. My tsetse fly bites were looking really horrible. They had left black and blue bruises all over my body—I jokingly said to Col that I looked like a battered wife. As long as I didn’t get trypanosomiasis, it would all fade soon enough.
The Leopard Rock hotel was a beautiful spot, so far up the mountain and overlooking the valley below. The hotel had manicured lawns, lots of bright red aloes, huge fig trees, flowering gardens, and vervet monkeys. One of the guards pointed out a monkey and told me it was a Samango monkey, so I photographed it on the roof (the monkey, that is; I was still on terra firma).

Great Zimbabwe and the Journey South

Later we set off towards the Great Zimbabwe Ruins, stopping at Chimanimani en route. The Chimanimani mountains stood majestically overlooking the small town.
The silver glimmer of Lake Kyle was visible in the distance but we took the wrong turning, as the Zimbabwe authorities in their misguided wisdom had removed most of the signposts with the intention of replacing them with the new name changes. Unfortunately, they hadn’t got to that part yet.
Earlier I asked Cecilia where the Zimbabwe Ruins were, and she replied,
‘Everywhere!’
The ruins were built between the 11th and 15th centuries, and are the largest stone ruins in sub-Saharan Africa, spanning approximately 80 hectares. The ruins had been inhabited in the Iron Age by a Bantu population called the Shona.
We climbed to the top of the fort via the ‘authentic’ route. The view was great from here. We took some photos then walked to the other part of the ruins. The ruins also feature Cecil John Rhodes' grave; he was the Founder of the southern African territory of Rhodesia, which the company named after him in 1895. In his last will and testament he wrote;
“I admire the grandeur and loneliness of the Matopos in Rhodesia and therefore I desire to be buried in the Matopos on the hill which I used to visit and which I called a 'View of the World.”

We had several police stops en route to the border. At the last stop, they said we had been speeding.

‘Why are you speeding in a built-up area?' asked the rotund policeman, fingers twitching for a bribe. Col said he couldn’t see a sign saying it was a 60km zone, which he knew for a fact as there’s an absence of road signs so we were let go.
Tim helped Col fill up our tank with petrol from our spare containers, as we were not likely to find any until we crossed the border and for a while after that.
Col muttered that was going to be ‘fun’ at the border, (with an accusatory glance at me). Actually, apart from the general attitude, which was ‘kraal manure’ at best, we got through customs with minimal fuss. The South African side held the usual officious, unhelpful, and insolent collection of officialdom, and I longed to punch their collective faces or at least poke them in the eyes. We were asked to climb out of the car and stand in a shoebox-sized container of disinfectant, and this apparently was the border ‘Foot and Mouth’ control done with.

An absolutely magnificent sunset spread its vibrant colours over the darkening sky, which sweetened the mood somewhat as we slowly returned home.
