# Chapter 18: Zimbabwe 2004: The Frozen Toilet Seats

En route to Zimbabwe by motor car, I booked our family into a small apartment for an overnight stop. Early this morning, I stepped flat into five inches of standing water on the bathroom floor, which simultaneously froze my ankles and completely removed any lingering traces of sleep from my brain. Col grumbled that he had suffered the exact same fate last night, but added that as well as freezing his feet, the loose toilet seat hadn't remained in place on the porcelain, but had instead attached itself physically to his nether regions when he stood up! I laughed out loud at the visual image this description brought to mind. The international border post was the usual historic experience in slow bureaucratic torture, testing my already flimsy patience to the absolute maximum. The moment we opened the car doors, we were aggressively descended upon by the sick, the wounded, and the downright desperate of the border town. Batting away these persistent pests, we made our way into the long queue and prepared to wait for hours. A few local touts approached our family offering their professional services, which included conducting such mentally challenging processes as filling out standard customs forms in English and answering complex questions such as ‘Name, occupation, and address’. As we felt justifiably qualified to complete these tasks ourselves without the assistance of their expert help, we denied their services, until one persistent tout managed to convince Col that his personal intervention would significantly speed up our vehicle's clearance through the gates. Thus it was that we found ourselves paying cash for the privilege of having them stand directly inside our personal space for two grueling hours while Col laboriously entered the factory serial details of his many cameras and lenses onto the cargo manifests. The South African side of the border featured the identical style of people unhelpfully informing us of the procedures for crossing the line. I received several sharp kicks to the shin from Col under the counter when I openly showed my irritation upon hearing that we were required to pay an extra carbon tax on top of the already hefty vehicle transit fees. I had already successfully endeared myself to Col from the very start of the crossing by loudly hooting our car horn at a mandatory police stop post; my only excuse was that my finger had accidentally slipped on the steering wheel.

The Decaying Beauty of Harare 

Later on, we pulled over at the Elephant and Tusker Hotel—an aging beauty of erstwhile colonial years—to stop for breakfast. The open-air boma dining area was completely empty of customers, save for one rather dour, silent family who barely spoke a single word to each other but barked sharp orders at the hapless waiter, who was wearing a bright green uniform jacket that was quite clearly three sizes too small for his frame. The grand Bumi River was a distant geographic memory, as the channel was now entirely hidden beneath deep drifts of pale orange sand. I managed to successfully send three text messages from my mobile at the border gates; two found their target, but one remained suspended somewhere in cyber-space. We left the Bumi area behind and hared off towards the capital of Harare—a six-hour drive away down the highway. The roads were in a terrible, shocking state of disrepair and structural decay compared to the last time we had driven here back in 1997, with the roadside signs as empty of informational text as the lanes were empty of cars. Fuel was incredibly hard to come by in the country, and it was not uncommon to find lines of cars stretching a full kilometre long outside of the few functional petrol stations. Luckily, we had filled our tanks to the brim back in Beitbridge across the border, where petrol was more easily available. Dry deciduous trees lined the dusty roads, and the famous msasa trees (Brachystegia spiciformis) were completely devoid of their usual pretty spring orange colours until September, leaving the winter bush grass a dry, pale, auburn shade. There were very few people walking along the highways, but those who were trudging the dusty roadsides looked utterly dejected; quiet, raw despair perfectly described their facial features. These sorry-looking people were the direct economic product of the tyrannical rule of Robert Mugabe, the president of Zimbabwe. After nearly four decades of misrule, he had managed to bring this once-wealthy, vibrant nation to absolute economic ruin, primarily by presiding over the violent, forced takeover of white-owned commercial farms at the end of the 1990s, which in turn completely devastated the nation's agriculture and led to rampant hyperinflation. There was the occasional lonely figure waiting at the derelict bus stops along the asphalt—not so much waiting for the transport itself, which was highly unlikely to ever arrive, but simply standing in hope of something better. Our first forced police stop along the route went off without any major incident, despite Col’s intense misgivings and an audible groan from his seat the moment we spotted the police presence ahead. This anxiety was probably more because he wasn't sure of how I would verbally respond to the officers, rather than a fear of the police themselves. I am notoriously not good with abusive authority, as you will well know by now. It is generally accepted by travelers that you simply suck it in and do exactly what the corrupt officials ask of you, but I find my brain has fundamentally not received that behavioral memo. When our car finally arrived in the suburbs of Harare, having driven through a really grotty, broken part of town, we were utterly astounded at the severe state of general urban degradation since we were last here. There were people everywhere, walking the streets, selling trinkets, talking, standing in tight groups, and street vendors selling fresh oranges from makeshift cardboard boxes. In other words, a typical, chaotic African scene. 

Gucci Grace and the Trillion-Dollar Notes 

We were staying in the city with our friends Cecilia and Andy. Col immediately visited a local bank branch to secure some Zimbabwean dollars for our expenses. The banknotes all featured a minimum of five or six zeros printed after whatever the base denomination was, and we were handed a few ‘trillion-dollar’ notes that were practically worthless in real value. Col and I went off to visit Mukuvisi Woodlands—a small game park situated close to the city center. Our guide was a man named Ben, and he escorted us on an hour-and-a-half bush walk through the property, where we successfully came right across giraffe, sable antelope, and a small zebra herd. Ben chatted non-stop as our boots trampled through the golden grass, with the high-rise skyline of downtown Harare forming a purple backdrop in the far distance. Ben gossiped to us about Bob Mugabe’s wife, Grace. She had originally served as his personal secretary and used to be married to an air force officer, until her first husband mysteriously disappeared under highly suspicious circumstances. She was globally known to the locals as ‘Gucci Grace’ due to her infamous propensity for jetting off to Europe on massive luxury shopping sprees, funded entirely by the bankrupt government treasury. We murmured our surprise at this political audacity, then returned to the main gate to join the others outside for some wine and a chat. The next morning, Col, Tim, and I headed off to visit Ewanrigg Botanical Gardens, located approximately 38 kilometres outside of Harare. The site is globally renowned for its extensive, curated collection of aloes and prehistoric cycads. En route down the highway, we were stopped at a mandatory police roadblock and abruptly asked by the officer if our vehicle possessed a car radio. Luckily, we didn't. That infraction would have instantly elicited a cash fine of ten dollars without the accompanying municipal radio licence, which is also incredibly difficult to obtain from the bureaucrats. I later joined Cecilia for a supply trip to a local supermarket. The commercial shelves were not at all well stocked; in fact, most of the grocery aisles were completely empty of basic goods. It was a deeply sad situation to witness. Cecilia told me that their circle of friends take regular turns to drive across the border into South Africa just to do a load of basic grocery shopping, taking down large text lists of orders for each other. She showed me that she keeps small-denomination banknotes tucked safely under her car's sun visor to use for quick police roadblock bribes. 

Journey to Mana Pools: The Toilet Roll Raider 

We were up early the next morning to prepare the vehicles for our expedition down to Mana Pools National Park, finally arriving at the reserve gates at 5 p.m. We were forced to pay a hefty international entrance fee simply because our passports were from South Africa. Mana means ‘four’ in the local Shona language, referencing the four large permanent pools formed by the shifting courses of the Zambezi River. It is a massive, pristine wildlife sanctuary that straddles the river boundary, globally renowned for its abundant big game, unfenced campsites, and its status as a proclaimed UNESCO World Heritage Site. We secured our designated campsite, and immediately spotted a small herd of female elephants on the way into the clearing. The campsite itself was beautifully laid out and pleasant, with tall mahogany, wild fig, and apple-ring acacia trees (Faidherbia albida) providing our tents with adequate shade from the hot sun. The trees were absolutely full of vervet monkeys scampering all over the branches, and I warned the family that we would have to be extra vigilant with our food provisions. Just as I was finishing this sentence, a bold monkey jumped straight through the open passenger window of our Toyota and successfully stole a fresh roll of toilet paper! As darkness fell rapidly over the river, we quickly set up our two canvas tents. We cooked a hot meal of ‘Spam-bol’—our camp take on spaghetti bolognese—for our supper, and ate our plates overlooking the wide Zambezi River from the camp chairs. Cecilia regaled our family with wild tales of hyena antics from her previous safari trip, and Tim suddenly piped up that he could see hundreds of glowing eyes peering out of the dark woods at our table. Col turned on the Toyota’s high-beam headlights into the brush, which beautifully revealed a peaceful herd of impala staring right back at our chairs, going some way toward appeasing Tim's fears. We crawled into our sleeping bags early, but sleep was incredibly difficult for my ears with the sheer volume of wild nocturnal noise echoing through the canvas: spotted hyenas howling, heavy hippo grunts from the water, monkeys chirruping in the branches, and in the wee hours of the morning, the deep, guttural roaring of a wild lion. I absolutely loved the experience, however; it is these very wilderness sounds that I miss most when I am back home in the suburbs. 

The Wet Paint Toilets 

I emerged from our tent the next morning with aching bones and wild hair to start the campfire for our morning tea. The rest of the family emerged a bit later, grumbling loudly about a lack of sleep and a fear of some of the more alarming predatory sounds in the night. A lone bull hippo was casually strolling along the periphery of the camp clearing, heading back toward the safety of the river. Hippopotamuses are globally renowned for their highly aggressive and territorial behaviour, and are statistically responsible for killing any unfortunate person found to be blocking their direct path to water. After enjoying our hot tea out of tin mugs, we batten down our camp hatches as best we could—these being basic tents, the doors were merely canvas flaps and not very secure against baboons— and set off for an early morning game drive. We encountered a fantastic variety of animals along the loops: vervet monkeys, breeding herds of impala, black-backed jackals, and waterbuck sporting their distinctive white ‘wet paint toilet seat’ rings on their bottoms. We also saw elephants—including one huge bull with a tusk completely missing—many hornbills, open-billed storks, saddle-billed storks, more hippos basking in the shallows, painted storks, eland, kudu, zebra, massive Nile crocodiles, and even a large python curled up near the edge of the riverbank. It was a truly magnificent morning's viewing, and we headed back to camp in high spirits for some pan-toasted bread and tea. Upon entering the clearing, I noticed a large Cape buffalo lurking not far from our tents, and I eyed him every bit as warily as he eyed our boots. Cape buffalo (Syncerus caffer) are considered exceptionally dangerous and unpredictable when confronted, earning them a spot in the infamous African 'Big Five'. Col gave Tim his old camera for the morning loop, and our son stood proud next to his father, zooming in and out and photographing whatever his dad was focused on—the two of them looked exactly like two peas in a pod. I enjoyed a lovely hot bath in the park ablutions block before setting off for our afternoon drive around 3 p.m., where we saw a repeat of much of our morning game. We headed back to camp for sundowner drinks overlooking the Zambezi River, with the hippos snorting their loud amusement from the water. Supper was prime beef steak cooked over the open fire served with toasted bread, accompanied by a glass of still-chilled wine from the cooler box. This time, I decided to join Cecilia in her tent for the night, leaving Tim and Col to manage the secondary tent. The night sounds of hyenas, hippos, fiery-necked nightjars, and rutting impala were deeply soothing, and I drifted off to sleep early. The following morning, two large buffalo had wandered directly into our camp area; one was standing right outside the entrance to the ablutions block, which made washing my face a little difficult. I was forced to take a really circuitous, looping route through the bush and then run inside quickly when his head was turned. 

Raided Tents and the Barbie Torch 

Last night, I heard heavy rustling noises close to our table and got up to peer out of the tent flap, discovering a huge spotted hyena investigating our camp table. The rich smell of our barbecued steak meat was clearly the attraction. There were many other nocturnal sounds echoing around our clearing: hyenas giggling, lions roaring in the distance, buffalo bellowing, and grunting hippos from the shallows. I was awakened again a few hours later by some loud rustling sounds in the leaves and peered out to see a beautiful male eland wading through the silver, moonlit river water. He silently exited the river channel and stood standing close to the boys' tent, contentedly munching on tree leaves. It was a wonderful, serene sight, and I went back to my sleeping bag feeling entirely happy. I woke up early the next morning and attempted to head for the ablution block, but my favorite resident buffalo was there explicitly guarding the entrance. I had to wait patiently until he shifted a little way from the timber door before I could dart inside to wash. This morning’s safari drive was not at all pleasant, as I was bitten excessively by local tsetse flies, and my left hand and arm became severely swollen and painfully itchy. The tsetse fly (Glossina) is a dangerous insect vector in sub Saharan Africa, capable of transmitting trypanosomiasis— commonly known as sleeping sickness—to humans through its painful bite. We returned to camp to find that our site had been thoroughly raided by the vervet monkeys during our absence. They had completely torn our big tent by dragging a heavy metal tent peg backwards through the canvas fabric, leaving the structure with two huge holes. The smaller tent featured a long tear along the front entrance flap as well. Cecilia and I set to work repairing the structural damage utilizing strips of black duct tape. Cecilia complained to one of the local camp cleaners about the monkeys tearing our gear, but unfortunately, the man possessed the apparent IQ of an amoeba and just stared at her open-mouthed, so not a lot was ever going to be done about the wildlife. We also saw several large water monitor lizards along the pan today, looking exactly like prehistoric dinosaurs. My period started the next morning, so feeling bloated and thoroughly irritable, I got up around 5 a.m. feeling exactly like a piece of raw shark chum bait walking across the dark camp toward the ablution block. I navigated the path by the dim light of a pink childhood Barbie torch, whose pathetic beam gave the name ‘penlight’ an entirely new meaning. My resident buffalo wasn't at the door this morning, but I kept glancing around anxiously at the dark shapes and twinkling predatory eyes in the bush with some trepidation, waiting for some hungry beast to pounce on me. We removed every single item of value from inside our tents this morning in case the disgruntled baboons decided to have another go at ferreting for our food. After a fairly uneventful drive, we returned to our clearing to find that the baboons had crapped their absolute territorial annoyance at finding no food inside the canvas. Piles of baboon faecal matter perched down the canvas tops and sides of our tents. A family of ‘Pumbas’—wild warthogs—were scattered in mounds all over our camp lawn, alongside the ubiquitous, ablution-guarding buffalo. Col went off to try his luck photographing the bee-eaters again, while Cecilia and I cleaned the dung off the tents and tidied up our gear before preparing lunch. I had some severe trouble with my fire-lighting skills in the wind, so Col had to finish off the logs for me. We enjoyed a lunch of fried bacon and scrambled eggs served with a three-bean salad and crackers—excellent camp cuisine. During the bacon-frying process, I had a distinct feeling I was being watched from behind, and turned around to discover a huge hyena sitting directly under a nearby wild fig tree; he was hunched over, literally salivating at the possibility of scoring some bacon rashers. I shouted loudly in pure fright, and the predator actually took flight, skulking off to await his chances from the safety of the nearby bushes. We enjoyed another sundowner over the riverbank before retiring to our tents for the nightly cacophony of wilderness sounds. Luckily, there was no sign of the bacon-loving hyena. 

Changing Tyres in Lion Country 

Upon our departure, one of the park officials asked if he could secure a lift with our vehicles down to the town of Karoi. We enjoyed a bit of game spotting en route to the main national park gates, but didn’t see much wildlife besides a few wild piggies, a few elephants, and some zebra. We finally left the Mana Pools boundaries and travelled along the red, dusty murram roads in search of some wild baobab fruit. The 'soda-bicarb' fruit of the prehistoric baobab tree (Adansonia)— locally known as 'monkey bread'—possesses a tough, woody outer shell. Inside the pod, kidney-shaped seeds are embedded in a chalky, dry pulp. The baobab powder has a sharp, tart citrus flavour remarkably similar to sherbet. We located several fallen pods on the ground and enjoyed a taste of the sweet-sour desert delicacy. We set off again down the highway, swerving over the dry rivulets of the dirt track, until Col passed our vehicle in the other car. This maneuver proved highly untimely, as shortly afterwards, our vehicle suffered a massive blowout, and our rear left wheel was instantly reduced to ribbons. Both Cecilia and I were forced to climb out of the cabin in the middle of absolute ‘lion country’ just to change the huge tyre. It was not an easy process at all due to the sheer size and physical weight of the Toyota's wheel. Cecilia's 4x4 was relatively new, so she had absolutely no idea how to change a tyre. We consulted the vehicle manufacturer's instruction manual, but the text seemed to be written in ancient Greek. Luckily, I possessed some mechanical knowledge based on our numerous tyre changes back in Saudi Arabia, so we successfully located the winch mechanism to lower the spare tyre down from underneath the Land Cruiser's belly. However, it took us a lot longer to work out how to physically release the jack from its vice-held storage grip in the rear panels. I crawled straight underneath the chassis, spitting out mouthfuls of highway dust, until I located the winch chain. I was in the middle of guiding Cecilia through the physical procedure, when a succession of tourist 4x4s slowed down on the dirt road to ask if we required help. I asked one of the drivers if he would try to catch up with Col's car ahead and ask him to return to our position. The men agreed and roared off in a cloud of dust. Cecilia and I managed to manually heave the shredded tyre off the wheel hub, and then I enjoyed a nervous wee right in front of the back door of the Toyota, while Cecilia kept a sharp eye on the bush looking out for predators. We were just trying to work out exactly where to place the jack head beneath the chassis, when Col finally arrived back in a skitter of flying stones and dust. He and our guide, Amos, quickly completed the tyre-changing process for us. I was leaning over in a crouched position showing Col exactly where the jack arm should go under the steel, and placed my bare hand on the ground for balance, only to encounter a very damp, wet spot on the earth. I realized a second too late that my hand had landed exactly where I had wee'd a few minutes previously! After finding some wet wipes inside my handbag to clean my fingers, we continued our journey towards Karoi, pulling over at a motel for a cup of tea and some sandwiches—and in my specific case, to wash my hands properly at a sink. As we were munching on our sandwiches at the table, Col told us that Amos, after learning Col was a medical doctor, had begun telling him a long string of stories of woe regarding his prowess— or rather, his distinct lack of it—in the bedroom. He declared to Col that he desperately required some medical pills to strengthen his ‘backbone’ (a local euphemism for his replicator applicator, we assumed). Col chuckled and said he had handed him some basic multivitamins, telling the man that they would do the trick perfectly. We chuckled at the story for a while, and Tim asked innocently from his seat what Amos had meant by his backbone. ‘His willy,’ I stated simply to the table. ‘Oh,’ said Tim, entirely unphased, and took another large bite of his sandwich. 

The Samango Monkey of Leopard Rock 

Col packed the car the next morning, which was looking distinctly heavier than when we had first arrived in the country. We bid a fond farewell to all the local kids before they left the house for school, and then said our goodbyes to Andy and Cecilia. It was only 7:30 a.m. when we set off down the highway, but we had a long way to drive. My tsetse fly bites were looking really horrible by this stage; the reactions had left dark black and blue bruises all over my skin. I jokingly said to Col that I looked exactly like a battered wife; as long as I don’t contract sleeping sickness, the bruising will fade soon enough. The Leopard Rock Hotel was an absolutely beautiful spot, situated far up the mountain pass and overlooking the vast valley below. The luxury estate featured perfectly manicured green lawns, lots of bright red aloes blooming, huge fig trees, flowering gardens, and troops of monkeys. One of the hotel guards pointed out a primate to me and mentioned it was a rare Samango monkey (Cercopithecus mitis), so I photographed it on the roof of the building (the monkey, that is; I remained firmly on terra firma). Great Zimbabwe and Foot-and-Mouth Control Later on, we set off towards the historic Great Zimbabwe Ruins, stopping briefly at the town of Chimanimani en route. The Chimanimani Mountains stood majestically overlooking the small settlement. The silver glimmer of Lake Kyle was visible in the distance, but we accidentally took the wrong turning on the highway, as the Zimbabwean authorities—in their misguided political wisdom—had recently removed most of the geographic road signposts with the intention of replacing them with new local name changes. Unfortunately, they hadn't actually got around to that part of the project yet. Earlier in the holiday, I had asked Cecilia where the famous Zimbabwe Ruins were located, and she had dryly replied, ‘Everywhere!’ The Great Zimbabwe Ruins were constructed by a Bantu population called the Shona between the 11th and 15th centuries, serving as the capital of a great kingdom during the local Iron Age. Spanning approximately 80 hectares, they represent the largest stone ruins in sub-Saharan Africa. We climbed to the top of the ancient hill complex via the steep, ‘authentic’ stone route. The panoramic view was spectacular from the summit. We took some photos, then walked over to explore the other sectors of the ruins. The neighboring Matobo region also features Cecil John Rhodes' final grave site. Rhodes was the historic founder of the southern African territory of Rhodesia, which the British South Africa Company had named after him back in 1895. In his last will and testament, he had explicitly written: “I admire the grandeur and loneliness of the Matopos in Rhodesia and therefore I desire to be buried in the Matopos on the hill which I used to visit and which I called a 'View of the World'.” We encountered several police checkpoints en route to the South African border post. At the very last stop, the officer claimed we had been speeding. ‘Why are you speeding in a built-up area?’ asked the rotund policeman, his fingers visibly twitching for a cash bribe. Col argued that he couldn't see a single sign indicating it was a 60km zone, which he knew for a fact because there was a total absence of road signs along the tarmac, so they eventually let us go. Tim helped Col fill up our fuel tank with petrol from our spare plastic containers, as we were highly unlikely to find any open stations until we crossed the border and for a while after that. Col muttered that the crossing was going to be ‘fun’ at the customs gates, delivering an accusatory glance straight at my seat. Actually, apart from the general administrative attitude of the staff—which was ‘kraal manure’ at best—we got through customs with minimal fuss. The South African side held the usual officious, unhelpful, and insolent collection of officialdom, and I longed to punch their collective faces or at least poke them in the eyes. We were instructed to climb out of our vehicle and stand inside a shoebox-sized container of liquid disinfectant, and this apparently was the border ‘Foot and Mouth’ biosecurity control completely done with. An absolutely magnificent African sunset spread its vibrant, glowing colours over the darkening sky, which sweetened our family's mood somewhat as we slowly returned home to Cape Town.
