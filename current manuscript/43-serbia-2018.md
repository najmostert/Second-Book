# Chapter 44: Serbia 2018

On my way to Serbia, I found myself sitting next to a man who had clearly had several whiskeys. He knocked my red wine all over my top, and I had to slap his hand away when he tried to ‘mop’ it up too close to my chest. After an uncomfortable flight, I boarded a FlyDubai plane to Belgrade. A young woman in front of me spent the entire flight obsessively reviewing photos of herself, while her long-suffering boyfriend looked on in boredom. She spends the entire journey looking through hundreds of the same photos of herself, lovingly taken by her long suffering husband who looks on in boredom and only a modicum of interest when a long painted claw rests on yet another pose that differs from the previous one by a centimetre, ‘Look honey, this one you took of me is fab, my tan looks great against that new dress I bought in Paris.’ She continues zooming in and out, presumably looking for flaws. I landed in Belgrade to a grey, muggy heat. Tim and his university friend, Dan, were waiting for me at a coffee shop. We headed to their apartment—a one-bedroom place where they rotated weeks on the couch—and took a creaky lift to the 7th floor. The lounge was a disaster zone of clothes and open suitcases. We walked through Park Tašmajdan to see St. Mark’s Church, then continued to the Belgrade Fortress Kalemegdan, an ancient citadel surrounded by a beautiful park dating back to Roman times. It was a lovely evening, and we were just in time to watch the sunset over the Danube. The local girls were striking, with slim figures and tanned faces, while the men—well, let's just say they had a different look entirely! It was a fascinating start to my Serbian adventure. The statue of a naked man atop a tall column is Pobednik (The Victor), erected to honour the Serbian army’s victory during World War II. He holds a sword in one hand and a falcon in the other. It is said his bare buttocks, which face the city, scoff at the enemy— though I’ll admit I’ve never seen ‘scoffing buttocks’ before! After the last golden rays set over the Danube, we headed to the Rakia Bar to sip the local plum-based spirit, Slivovitz. I had cinnamon-flavoured, Tim had mint, and Dan had almond. 

Sofia, Bulgaria 

At 3:45 a.m., we stumbled into a taxi for the city centre. The bus ride took over five hours; a young woman in front of us spent the entire journey alternating between sobbing loudly and shouting in Serbian during a phone crisis. When we finally reached the border, she jumped off and vanished into the bushes! After passport checks in both Serbia and Bulgaria, the scenery changed to mountainous terrain surrounded by rolling sunflower fields. In Sofia, we met Tim’s university friend, Victor. He took us to a restaurant in Oborishte called Mr. Pizza—though don’t let the name fool you, they have many delicious meals. I enjoyed a great cappuccino and a beetroot-and-cabbage salad. Victor was incredibly helpful, carrying our bags up four flights of stairs to our Airbnb. I claimed the kitchen couch as my bedroom, padding it out with extra pillows and blankets. Later, we visited the Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, one of the largest Eastern Orthodox cathedrals in the world. Its gold-painted central dome is 150 feet high, and the structure is a testament to the trade boom of its time: the marble and lighting came from Munich, metalwork from Berlin, and mosaics from Venice. The cathedral grounds were vibrant with yellow marigolds and pink roses, and several weddings were taking place, with brides posing against the flowers. We joined a two-hour walking tour led by an attractive local guide who took us past the Sofia Balkan Palace, the Ivan Vazov National Theatre, the Museum of Illusions, the Public Mineral Baths, and the Cathedral of St. Nedelya. We ended the day at a restaurant called A La Minute, where we shared a plummy local red wine and a delicious coffee-and-cream cake. 

Museums and Departure 

The following day, we explored the National Gallery, which houses gold, silver, and pottery dating back to the 3rd century. One amusing clay face bore an uncanny, unfortunate resemblance to our former president, Zuma! After watching the changing of the guard outside Parliament, we headed to the Raffey Bar. On our way, we were accosted by a funny Nigerian man who jokingly warned us, "Don't trust Nigerians!"—we left him with a laugh. Victor arranged a taxi for us, and we headed to the bus station. Our driver was an obnoxious man with no sense of humour, though his young co-driver nervously served us chocolate croissants and water. At one point, the driver slammed the bus to a halt, turned around, and began shouting at a random passenger sitting next to Dan. The poor man looked as perplexed as we were! After a bathroom break in Kresna, where I bought roasted sunflower seeds, the scenery became increasingly mountainous as we approached the Greek border. The driver eventually dropped us off at the train station, leaving us to navigate the next leg of our journey. Dan, Tim, and I scraped together our remaining euros for coffee at a small outdoor café before a taxi dropped us off at our apartment. The driver’s daughter was in the passenger seat, and he warned us that the President was in town, promising a demonstration. We were too early to check into our Airbnb, so we left our luggage outside and explored. Thessaloniki is quite warm, filled with graffiti, and like much of Europe, overrun with feral cats. When the demonstration began, it was a loud affair of singing and flag-waving. Later, we found a shop selling prayer beads; the owner—a kind Greek/Turkish man—explained that the warmth of our hands would release different scents from the beads. Mine, which I later hung in my car, smells of cinnamon. 

Into the Clouds: Meteora 

Tim hired a car, and with Dan navigating—which proved not to be his forte—we took a few wrong turns before finally escaping the city. We stopped in Orevena for lunch; it’s a town big on mushrooms, with signs and images of fungi everywhere. As we approached the high mountains of Kastraki, we encountered the surreal, towering sandstone pillars of Meteora, some reaching 400 metres high. Medieval monasteries cling precariously to the tops of these peaks. We stayed in the quaint village of Klabaka at an apartment called ‘Be My Guest’. We hiked up to the Varlaam Monastery, established around 1350 by a hermit who scaled the rock. In the 16th century, the Apsaras brothers revived it using ropes and baskets. Today, gold-leaf paintings and intricate ceilings depict scenes from the life of Christ. We peered down at the village, which looked like a toy town, and spotted a rusty old hoist—the ‘net’—once used to haul monks and supplies up the cliffs. 

Delphi and Galaxidi 

We left Kastraki, passing through beautiful forests and the old industrial village of Elainonas. In Amfissa, we stopped for a chat with a local who pointed us to a fountain in the town centre. The owner of a nearby restaurant welcomed us, though the place was crawling with stray cats. He insisted I drink from the ancient water spring behind his restaurant; it was sparkling fresh! He also took photos of me smiling awkwardly while the lads laughed at my ‘abduction.’ We drove on to Delphi to see the ruins of the Sanctuary of Apollo, dating back to the 4th century BCE. The site is filled with bronze votive offerings, though the temple itself is now just imposing ruins. We stayed in a town called Galaxidi, a pretty place filled with cacti. I learned a painful lesson: never touch a cucumber-shaped cactus. It looked soft, but it was covered in tiny, burning hairs that imbedded into my skin instantly. It reminded me of my boarding school days in Ireland where our headmistress, Miss Christie—a woman with one roaming glass eye—forced us to drink warm, foamy goat’s milk. I’ve hated feta and goat’s milk ever since! We spent the afternoon on a pebbly beach. I had to wear my flip-flops in the water to avoid hundreds of black spiny sea urchins. Later, at the Temple of Poseidon, only Tim went inside due to the high ticket prices. I spent the drive back with my tweezers, painfully picking tiny urchin spines out of my little toe. 

The Ferry to Crete 

We had eight hours to kill before our ferry to Crete. As we walked along, our idle chatter turned to the horrors of sexually transmitted diseases. We heard giggles behind us—two teenage girls were close enough to hear every word. We tried to slow down to let them pass, but they were determined to eavesdrop on our ‘pearls of wisdom,’ stubbornly sticking right behind us until we finally crossed the road! ‘We need to talk more about gluten-free recipes in public,’ I said with a giggle, just to mess with the two teenagers tailing us. The ferry was a huge, ocean liner-sized vessel. After storing our cases, we found our ‘Airseats’—styled like plane seats, but a lot more roomy. The lads went in search of supper while I wandered about, eventually returning to settle in. It was a miserable night; the seats were hard and slippery, and we were parked right in front of a blinding television screen that rambled on all night. By 6 a.m., when we docked in Chania, none of us had slept for more than two hours. We grabbed a cheese roll and coffee before heading to our accommodation in New Kydonia. Crete, the largest island in Greece and home to the ancient Minoan civilisation, is a stunning landscape of majestic mountains and unique beaches. We spent our first morning climbing the steep, rocky steps and cobbled streets of Chania’s Old Town, exploring antique shops and the Old Harbour Fort. At sundown, the boys enjoyed a local beer called ‘A’—the rest of the label was Greek to me—while I photographed the needle-like lighthouse and the forty yachts undulating in the harbour. The next day, we walked past ruins toward Glares Beach and then on to Kate Stalos, a coastal village named for the Minoan myth of Talos, the giant bronze guardian of Crete. As we sat at a restaurant, I noticed a distinct ‘ichthyic’ odour. I scrabbled through my handbag to find the culprit: a small, half-rotted sardine I’d stashed away days ago! It smelled ripe enough to clear the room. Later, we hiked up a mountain to Topolia, a village overflowing with pink bougainvillea and yellow lemons, where Tim enjoyed a traditional Cretan lunch of local sausage and lamb steaks. We spent the night in Pelekanos in a large room near the beach. We walked past cacti—some of which had had smiley faces carved into their flesh—to reach the famous pink-sand beach. The colour comes from millions of microscopic foraminifera shells, creating shades from pale pink to deep crimson. It was spectacular. At the Palace of Knossos, I was fascinated by the kouloures— large, mysterious round stone pits built in the 1700s, likely for burning refuse. The palace was remarkably intact, with blue and red pillars and a tall-backed wooden chair that had survived thousands of years. A peacock strutted around the ruins like royalty. I saw road signs warning of brown bears and found myself desperately wishing one would wander by! 

Frankfurt, Germany 2018 

I bid a sad farewell to Tim and Dan and flew to Frankfurt. I took a taxi into town, found a Starbucks, and successfully ordered: ‘Einen großen kaffee mit milch, bitte.’ (Herr Günzel would have been proud of me). I took the train into central Frankfurt to visit the Lumas art gallery on Kaiserstrasse. It was a small space featuring modern art, including two brightly coloured skateboards painted by Andy Warhol with his famous Campbell’s soup tins. In a moment of travel-induced brain fog, I tried to order a bottle of water at a nearby café, but I botched the German so badly that, instead of saying "a bottle, please," I accidentally called the shopkeeper an "incompetent loser"! He wasn’t amused. I walked across the Eiserner Steg, the famous iron footbridge covered in thousands of ‘love padlocks.’ With time running out, I grabbed a taxi back to the airport, passing autumn trees in brilliant hues of orange, yellow, and red along the Niederrad. At the terminal, protestors held a banner reading ‘Health before Profit,’ providing a sobering look at the state of German healthcare. It was a long, full trip. I boarded the plane for home, ready to trade the excitement of the Adriatic and the islands for the comforts of Cape Town. As for my name, Facebook is still refusing to change me back from "Dora D’Ourabora" for another two weeks.
