# Chapter 44: Serbia 2018

I was sitting at the rear of the plane as I usually do, on my way to Serbia, dreading having anyone sit next to me when sure enough, a large man several whiskeys in, indicated with a nod that he was seated next to me. After take-off and the seatbelt sign only just switched off, he was ringing for the steward to bring him a drink. I was pleased when he told him he had to wait for the drinks trolley. When the pretty stewardess leaned over and asked what we’d like, he said he’d have a whiskey and a beer and I groaned inwardly. I asked for a small glass of red wine. It wasn’t long before my companion jerked my table with his portly paw and knocked my glass of wine all over my top. He leaned over and began ineffectually mopping up the spill with a tissue; I had to slap him when he reached the region above my left breast. I jumped up, mumbling something about cleaning up, and rushed to the toilet to rinse the stain as best I could. I was rather hoping he’d settle down to sleep but he was in chatty mode and asked me several times if I was married, to which I replied, yes! And waggled my left ring finger to prove it. I still wear my wedding rings because I like them.
I settled down to nod off for an hour when the smell of breakfast assailed my quivering nostrils; glancing down at my watch I saw it was 1:30 in the morning and definitely not breakfast time according to my stomach, which was feeling decidedly queasy, having only a few hours before digested supper.

Later I boarded the FlyDubai plane that would take me to Belgrade. There was a young couple in front of me. She spent the entire journey looking through hundreds of the same photos of her truly, lovingly taken by her long-suffering husband who looked on in boredom and only a modicum of interest when a long painted claw rested on yet another pose that differed from the previous one by a centimetre.

‘Look honey, this one you took of me is fab, my tan looks great against that new dress I bought in Paris.’ She continued zooming in and out, presumably looking for flaws.

We landed in Belgrade to a grey, muggy heat that brought rivulets of sweat to my back. Tim and Dan were waiting for me at the coffee shop below the apartment. Dan was a university friend. He and Tim had been travelling together for a few months. They told me they were starving so we took a creaky old lift that threatened to collapse with the combined weight of my luggage and the three of us, up to the 7th floor. It was a one-bedroom apartment which they shared between them; one week Tim on the couch in the lounge, one week Dan. It was Tim’s turn to have the bedroom. The lounge was littered with articles of clothing; an open suitcase had disgorged most of its contents onto the floor. We walked through Park Tašmajdan to see St Mark’s Church, then on to the Belgrade Fortress Kalemegdan. The Fortress was an old citadel surrounded by a beautiful park dating back to Roman times. The local girls there were quite pretty with slim figures and healthy tanned faces; the lads agreed. In contrast, most of the men I saw were round-headed, balding and had beer paunches. It was an interesting combination. We were in time to watch the sunset over the Danube.

A statue of a naked man was supported by a tall column. He was Pobednik or Victor. He was erected in honour of their victory (the Serbian army) during World War II. He held a sword in one hand and a falcon in the other. It was said his bare buttocks, which faced the city, scoffed at the enemy. Yeah, I wasn’t convinced — though I’d not seen ‘scoffing buttocks’ before.
After the last of the golden rays set over the Danube, we headed back towards the Rakia Bar and sat outside sipping the local plum-based alcohol called ‘Slivovitz’, meaning unsurprisingly ‘plum’. They came in various flavours. I had cinnamon, Tim had a mint flavour and Dan had almond.

At 3:45 we stumbled out of bed and grabbed a taxi to the city centre for the bus. This trip was just over five hours. I tried to sleep but there was a young woman in front of us who was going through some personal relationship crisis. She was on her cell phone the entire way, alternating between sobbing loudly to whoever was on the other end or shouting loudly in Serbian. When we got to the border, she jumped off and disappeared very quickly into the bushes. We approached the Serbian border where our passports were checked then again at the Bulgarian border.

## Bulgaria — Sofia

This area was more mountainous, with green land surrounded by sunflower fields. In Sofia we waited for Tim’s university friend, Victor, to arrive by taxi. He took us to a nearby restaurant in Oborishte where they caught up. The restaurant was close to the apartment where we’d be staying. The restaurant was called Mr Pizza but don’t let that fool you. There were many more delicious meals to choose from. I had a great cappuccino and a beetroot and cabbage salad. Victor was slim and dark-haired with a short beard and a shy smile. It was a national holiday that day but seemed quiet right then. Victor came with me to fetch the key from the Airbnb owner. He then carried all the suitcases up four flights of stairs and went back for the boys. There was one room with a double bed, a pull-out bed in the lounge and a couch in the kitchen. I decided I’d sleep in the kitchen and made up the bed with extra pillows from the cupboard and a couple of blankets. Later we went to look at the beautiful golden-domed church, called Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, which is one of the largest Eastern Orthodox cathedrals in the world. The main features were the cathedral’s beautiful gold-painted central dome, which was 150 feet high. The design and construction were representative of the trade boom of the time as the pale green marble and lighting fixtures were created in Munich, the metal elements in Berlin, while the gates and mosaics were shipped from Venice. Many weddings were taking place both in and around the cathedral. Brides stood against the colourful flower beds to have their photos taken.
Vibrant beds of yellow marigolds contrasted sharply with beds of pink and red roses.
Victor joined us and we joined a walking tour led by an attractive blonde-haired local woman. It was just over two hours long but she was very interesting. We visited the Sofia Balkan Palace, Ivan Vazov National Theatre, the Museum of Illusions, Sofia Hotel, St Petra of the Saddle Makers Church, the Public Mineral Baths, the National Museum of Archaeology and the Cathedral of St Nedalya. It was an extensive tour and we ended the day at a restaurant nearby called ‘A La Minute’ for a tasty meal and two glasses of the local red wine; plummy and fruity, before sharing a delicious coffee cake and cream.

The park was huge so we only covered a part of it then took a look at the National Gallery. There were items of gold, silver, pottery; some cute and amusing clay faces — one of which bore an uncanny (some would say unfortunate) resemblance to our former president, Zuma. They had artworks from as early as the 3rd century. There was time to watch a quick changing of the guards outside parliament before heading to the Raffey Bar for some drinks. On our way out, we were accosted by a Nigerian man with an English accent. He tried to get us to go into his bar. He was quite funny and told us not to trust Nigerians! We took his advice and left him with a laugh. Victor had arranged a taxi for us and we parted company to wait for our bus. The driver was an obnoxious guy with no sense of humour that I could detect. His co-driver was young and he served us all chocolate croissants and a bottle of water with nervous energy.
Suddenly the driver stopped the bus, turned round in his seat and began shouting at a random passenger sitting next to Dan. It wasn’t clear what the problem was but he started up the bus again and we carried on none the wiser. From the look on the unfortunate passenger’s face, he was equally as perplexed. We stopped again in Kresna for a bathroom stop and a drink. I bought some roasted sunflower seeds from a small dusty kiosk. Gradually the scenery became more mountainous as we approached the Greek border. The bus driver dropped us all off at the train station.

## Greece — Thessaloniki

Dan, Tim and I sat at a small outdoor café for some coffee with euros that we’d scraped together. A taxi took us to our apartment. The driver’s daughter was also a passenger. He told us that the president was in Thessaloniki at the moment and there was going to be a demonstration later. He dropped us off after only 1.5 km and we discovered we were too early to check in to the Airbnb so left the pile of luggage outside the door and went in search of more coffee before exploring the city of Thessaloniki. Some old ruins we passed were overrun with feral cats; a remarkably frequent occurrence in Europe. As it was very warm we decided to head for the sea. The demonstration of which the driver earlier warned us was in full swing when we got there with loud singing and flag-waving. There was a lot of graffiti around the town and it wasn’t very clean there generally. A small café down a side street looked worth trying out so we entered and had a takadum which consisted of bits of chicken and cheese and a green salad with olives. It started to rain lightly which increased the humidity so we walked back to the flat to chill a bit. The sun went down leaving streaks of gold and red which we enjoyed with a glass of wine then the lads went clubbing.

Later we found a shop that sold prayer beads and chat to the owner. He’s a pleasant Greek/Turkish man who explained the different beads to us and said the warmth of our hands would release different scents depending on the beads. All three of us left with a different set of prayer beads. Mine, which I later hung in my car, smells of cinnamon.

We walked into the village for our morning coffee and sat opposite ‘The Arrogant Bar’ which, despite its name, appeared fairly humble then strolled along the edge of the sea looking at the boats and ships in the harbour and at sea. A homeless woman wearing a red bandana and a lime green top sat on a bench with two dogs and three cats feeding them breadcrumbs.

Tim hired a car and negotiated his way down some pretty busy streets with Dan navigating on his phone. This turned out not to be his forte. As a result we took a few wrong turns. Once we got out of the city it was a little easier and we all visibly relaxed.
Tim headed for Athens via Verona. The area became more mountainous as we stopped at Orevena for lunch. It seemed to be a town that was big on mushrooms as signs and images of various types of fungi abounded. After coffee at Ventis we drove on. Now the hills and mountains became green and blue and there were many fields with sunflowers past their sell-by date as they were turning brown around the edges. As we approached the high mountains of Kastraki, we found the most amazing rock formations; straight, flat longitudinal surfaces with jagged tops. These mountains were renowned for the dramatic and unique rock formations of Meteora. The towering sandstone pillars, some reaching heights of 200 to 400 metres, created a surreal landscape punctuated by medieval monasteries perched at the top of the peaks. The village of Klabaka was really lovely, quaint and pretty with clean streets, water features and lots of colourful flowers all against the backdrop of these majestic mountains. Our hostess was Veronica and she took us to the apartment called ‘Be My Guest’. The boys shared one room with two single beds and I took the double bed as I had the privilege of age. We walked down the sidewalk and found a pretty restaurant decorated with flowers that had a heart-shaped view of the mountains. There were six monasteries clinging to the tops of the main mountain so we hiked up to Varlaam Monastery first. Around 1350, a hermit named Varlaam climbed the steep rock and established a chapel dedicated to the ‘Three Hierarchs’.
In the early 16th century, brothers Theophanes and Nektarios Apsaras, from Ioannina, scaled the rock with ropes and baskets to revive the monastery. Now, beautiful stained-glass windows glowed in the early morning sunlight, gold-leaf paintings brightened the stone walls and intricate painted ceilings depicted scenes from the life of Christ and images of saints.

At Varlaam Monastery, the second largest monastery in Meteora, we took in the stunning views of the surrounding landscape.
We gazed down to the village nestled at the bottom like a toy town. We found an old hoist called ‘The Net’ which had been used by the monks over the years to bring themselves up the mountainside to the entrance at the top. Another monastery but it was closed so instead we walked back down to Kastraki in search of coffee and lunch. En route I found a poster stuck to a wall advertising ‘Grease’ the movie, but it was spelled ‘Greece!’
We left Kastraki early that morning and drove down the mountainside towards the sea. Beautiful forests en route. Past Gravia, Amiss and then drove through the old industrial village of Elainonas.
Tim approached a pedestrian and asked if he could point us towards a good restaurant and he replied that there was a good one in Amfissa by the water fountain in the town centre. The area was very quaint and pretty with the ubiquitous stray cats and dogs everywhere. The owner of the restaurant welcomed us and again, the place was absolutely crawling with stray cats; under tables, lying on the low stone wall, lying in the streets and everywhere in between. The owner showed me an old water spring a little behind his restaurant. He said I should drink the water so I dutifully cupped my hands and took a few mouthfuls. It was sparkling fresh. Next he showed me his olive trees and took a few photos of me with his phone while I stood smiling awkwardly before heading back to the restaurant where the lads laughed at my ‘abduction’.
Tim, Dan and I walked down to the village, past old pomegranate trees and grape vines and found another old church to investigate. Tim drove us to Delphi where they made pots, gold masks and statues. Afterwards we went to see the ruins of the Sanctuary of Apollo which dated back to the 4th century BCE; this temple was an imposing Doric-order temple whose existence weaves through the turbulent history of the site, which underwent three changes before settling into the ruins we found it in then. Aside from a female figure sitting on a three-legged chair, the majority of the exhibits were bronze votive offerings, dating to the 8th and 7th centuries BCE.

Our new rooms were off the street and somewhat difficult to negotiate as the narrow lanes were mostly blocked with parked vehicles. It was exhausting climbing up and down these obstacle-course lanes.
After coffee we drove to the nearby town of Galaxidi. It was a pretty town with many-hued flowers and cacti. I stupidly put my hand on one cucumber-shaped cactus to feel it, as it looked soft. It wasn’t soft at all, instead had tiny hairs that instantly embedded into my flesh and burned like crazy. Painful lesson learned.

We came to a lovely seaside harbour with a small semi-circular beach with black pebbles and a collection of small boats. The roadside was full of restaurants and pretty, colourful bougainvillea abounded.
When I was at boarding school in Ireland, we often went home with the headmistress, Miss Christie, for short holidays as we couldn’t fly home. Miss Christie was a shortish woman with steel-grey hair in a severe bun and a square face. She wore stiff jackets over sensible tweed skirts with one glass eye whose ‘focus’ remained straight ahead while the other eye roamed around giving her the appearance of someone not to be trifled with. Despite her outward appearance, she was a kind woman and often took Jax and me to her small goat farm in Northern Ireland. She owned around twenty Boston terriers and of course several goats. Every morning at breakfast she would pour us a glass of ‘fresh’ goat’s milk, rich, foamy and still warm and on occasion a stray goat hair that floated malevolently on top. I hated it and the warm drink made me heave so Jax was given the task of drinking my glass of milk when her head was turned. I’ve never liked goat’s milk or feta since.

The boys and I walked along the pebbly beach looking for a good place to swim. I had to walk in the warm water with my flip-flops on to avoid the hundreds of black spiny urchins.

That morning we took a look at the ruins of the Temple of Poseidon. The sun was beating down on us even at that early stage of the morning. It would have been too expensive for us all to go so only Tim bought a ticket. This Doric-style temple overlooks the Aegean and was dedicated to Poseidon, god of the sea. Its location provided a strategic lookout point and a navigational landmark and the impressive white marble columns were a popular destination for viewing the sunset.

I discovered a lot of tiny black urchin spines embedded in my little toe, despite wearing rubber sandals, which were quite painful so I spent most of the drive back trying to pick them out with my tweezers.
We had eight hours to kill until the ferry arrived that would take us to Crete.
As we were walking along, idle chatter turned to the horrors of sexually transmitted diseases — when we heard a few giggles behind us. Two teenage girls were close enough to have heard our conversation. We slowed down to allow them to pass us, but no ways were they going to miss the opportunity for more pearls of wisdom, so they remained stubbornly right behind until we eventually crossed the road.

‘We need to talk more about gluten-free recipes in public,’ I said with a giggle.

The ferry itself was a huge, ocean liner-sized boat. After storing our big cases in the hold we found our ‘Airseats’. These were styled like plane seats only a lot more roomy. The lads went off in search of supper while I wandered around before eventually returning to my seat to settle down for a good sleep.

Which never happened. Firstly, the seats were not conducive to a good sleep anyway, as they were hard and slippery, so I ended up on the floor and at 3 a.m. I was still awake with my head under the seat trying to cut out the light that remained on all night. Our seats were also right in front of a huge television screen which rambled on all night. By 6 a.m., when the ferry docked in Chania, none of us had slept for more than two hours. After grabbing our luggage, we stumbled off in search of a cheese roll and coffee before heading for our accommodation in New Kydonia. Crete is the largest island in Greece and was once home to the Minoan civilisation. The landscape was dominated by majestic mountains and some fascinating pink sand beaches. We were planning on finding one of them while we were there.

Breakfast was Greek yoghurt and honey with toast before climbing steep, rocky steps and walking cobbled streets for the ruins at Theotokopoulou, which is part of the Old Town of Chania. It was a quaint old town with many interesting antique shops, leatherware, old buildings and little side roads with small shops and the Old Harbour Fort with its round stone-domed roof. Coffee was sipped in a cobbled-stone café down a side street. The boys had a local beer at sundown called ‘A’ (the rest is Greek to me). I photographed the needle-like Chania lighthouse before watching the sunset cast a golden glow over the harbour. There were about forty yachts gently undulating with small harbour waves.

That morning we headed down towards Nea Kydonia, passing the old ruins towards Glares Beach with its dark grainy sand. It was too pebbly to stay there so we continued walking past the red-domed church to Kate Stalos, a coastal village in Chania, known for its sandy beaches and family-friendly atmosphere. The name ‘Stalos’ is also associated with a Minoan myth about the giant automaton called Talos; a bronze guardian of Crete who was sometimes depicted with wings or throwing boulders to protect the island from invaders. At the restaurant I took out my iPad to draw. Tim wrinkled his nose.

‘What’s that awful smell?’ he asked looking meaningfully at me.

‘I beg your pardon,’ I sniffed. ‘I showered this morning.’ But I caught a distinctly ichthyic odour.

I scrabbled in my handbag and found the culprit; a small sardine from the other day halfway out of a plastic bag. My handbag now smelled ripely of rotten fish.

The tiny ornate Church of Mary Magdalene stood watch nearby with a retiled roof and several turrets. The doorway was just big enough to squeeze through and Tim managed to get a lovely photo of my backside doing just that. We came to a nice wooded area next to the sea which was nice and cool and someone had tied a bag of small rocks to one of the trees and we tried to figure out the purpose.

‘It’s for chucking at the tourists,’ suggested Dan.

That day we walked to a car hire and got ourselves a Mercedes. Tim did the driving as usual while Dan misread the maps. He’s the only person I’ve met with a worse sense of direction than me.
Topolia was a pretty mountain village with churches and red-roofed houses and we stopped at a small café at the top of the mountain road. It was very colourful with walls of pink bougainvillea, blue hammocks and baskets of bright yellow lemons. Tim tried a traditional Cretan lunch here which was a local sausage, lamb steaks with chips and flatbreads. Long strings of dried chillies swung gently in the wind from metal hooks on the wall.
Afterwards Tim drove us through the tunnel at Mythimna to the west coast where we arrived at our accommodation for the night in Pelekanos. It consisted of one large room with an en suite bathroom and three beds along the wall. It was quite hot in the room so we took a walk past rows of cacti (some with smiles carved out of their succulent green flesh) to the beach which boasted the pink sand we’d read about. Sure enough as we approached the beach, the sea rolled over sand revealing black, beige and pink sand. The pink colour of the sand comes from microscopic, single-celled organisms called foraminifera. They have shells surrounding them made from calcium carbonate which is what colours the sand due to the huge number of these foraminifera. The colour ranges from a pale pink to a deep crimson and all the shades in between. It was quite spectacular.

At the Palace of Knossos, we came across some of the large round pits known as kouloures built in the 1700s. No one really knows what the function of these pits was with their low stone walls, but it was suspected that it was for burning refuse from the palace.
Knossos is an ancient Minoan city that was administered by the Romans. There were some amazingly intact murals on the crumbling walls of this ancient palace. Some of the pillars still had a blue and red colouring. I was fascinated by a tall-backed wooden chair, still intact after all this time. A beautiful peacock strutted around the ruins like royalty with their feathered crowns, jewels of colour against the stony backdrops. Signposts off the road warned us of brown bears. I wished some bears would wander by — I’d love to see one.
