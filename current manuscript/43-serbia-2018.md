# Chapter 44: Serbia 2018

I was sitting at the rear of the plane as I usually do, on my way to Serbia, dreading having anyone sit next to me when sure enough, a large man several whiskeys in, indicated with a nod that he was seated next to me. After take-off and the seatbelt sign only just switched off, he was ringing for the steward to bring him a drink. I was pleased when he told him he had to wait for the drinks trolley. When the pretty flight attendent leaned over and asked what we’d like, he said he’d have a whiskey and a beer and I groaned inwardly. I asked for a small glass of red wine. It wasn’t long before my companion jerked my table with his portly paw and knocked my glass of wine all over my top. He leaned over and began ineffectually mopping up the spill with a tissue; I had to slap him when his hand hovered at boob level and jumped up, mumbling  about cleaning up, and rushed to the toilet to rinse the stain as best I could. 
I settled down to nod off for an hour when the smell of breakfast assailed my quivering nostrils; glancing down at my watch I saw it was 1:30 in the morning and definitely not breakfast time according to my stomach, which was feeling decidedly queasy, having only a few hours before digested supper.

Later I boarded the FlyDubai plane that would take me to Belgrade. A young couple sat in front of me. She spent the entire journey looking through hundreds of the same photos of herself, lovingly taken by her long-suffering husband who looked on in boredom and only a modicum of interest when a long painted claw rested on yet another pose that differed from the previous one by a centimetre.

‘Look honey, this one you took of me is fab, my tan looks great against that new dress I bought in Paris.’ She continued zooming in and out, presumably looking for flaws.

We landed in Belgrade to a grey, muggy heat that brought rivulets of sweat to my back. Tim and Dan were waiting for me at the coffee shop below the apartment. Dan was a university friend. He and Tim had been travelling together for a few months. A creaky old lift that threatened to collapse with the combined weight of my luggage and the three of us, rose jerkily to the 7th floor.The boys had rented a one-bedroom apartment which they shared between them; one week Tim on the couch in the lounge, one week Dan. It was Tim’s turn to have the bedroom. We walked through Park Tašmajdan to St Mark’s Church and on to the Belgrade Fortress Kalemegdan. The Fortress was an old citadel surrounded by a beautiful park dating back to Roman times. We were in time to watch the sunset over the Danube.

A statue of a naked man was supported by a tall column. He was Pobednik or Victor, erected in honour of their victory (the Serbian army) during World War II. He held a sword in one hand and a falcon in the other. It was said his bare buttocks, which faced the city, scoffed at the enemy. I wasn’t convinced — though I’d not seen ‘scoffing buttocks’ before.
After the last of the golden rays set over the Danube, we headed back towards the Rakia Bar and sat outside sipping the local plum-based alcohol called ‘Slivovitz’, meaning unsurprisingly ‘plum’. They came in various flavours. I had cinnamon, Tim had a mint flavour and Dan had almond.

At 3:45 we stumbled out of bed and grabbed a taxi to the city centre for the bus. The trip was just over five hours. I tried to sleep but there was a young woman in front of us who was going through some personal relationship crisis. She was on her cell phone the entire way, alternating between sobbing loudly to whoever was on the other end or shouting loudly in Serbian. When we got to the border, she jumped off and disappeared very quickly into the bushes. We approached the Serbian border where our passports were checked then again at the Bulgarian border.

## Bulgaria — Sofia

Sofia was more mountainous, with verdant land surrounded by sunflower fields. Tim’s university friend, Victor took us to a nearby restaurant in Oborishte where they caught up. The restaurant was close to the apartment where we’d be staying. The restaurant was called Mr Pizza but don’t let that fool you. There were many more delicious meals to choose from. I had a great cappuccino and a beetroot and cabbage salad. Victor was slim and dark-haired with a short beard and a shy smile. It was a national holiday  but was quiet right. Victor came with me to fetch the key from the Airbnb owner. He then carried all the suitcases up four flights of stairs and went back for the boys. One room with a double bed, a pull-out bed in the lounge and a couch in the kitchen. I decided I’d sleep in the kitchen and made up the bed with extra pillows from the cupboard and a couple of blankets. Later we went to look at the beautiful golden-domed church, called Alexander Nevsky Cathedral, one of the largest Eastern Orthodox cathedrals in the world. The main features were the cathedral’s beautiful gold-painted central dome, which was 150 feet high. The design and construction were representative of the trade boom of the time as the pale green marble and lighting fixtures were created in Munich, the metal elements in Berlin, while the gates and mosaics were shipped from Venice. Many weddings were taking place both in and around the cathedral and beaming brides stood against colourful flowers to have their photos taken.
Vibrant beds of yellow marigolds contrasted sharply with beds of pink and red roses.
Victor joined us for a walking tour led by an attractive blonde-haired Bulgarian woman. We visited the Sofia Balkan Palace, Ivan Vazov National Theatre, the Museum of Illusions, Sofia Hotel, St Petra of the Saddle Makers Church, the Public Mineral Baths, the National Museum of Archaeology and the Cathedral of St Nedalya. It was an extensive tour ending at a restaurant nearby called ‘A La Minute’ for a tasty meal and two glasses of the local red wine.

The park was huge so we only covered a part of it then took a look at the National Gallery which housed items of gold, silver, pottery; some cute and amusing clay faces — one of which bore an uncanny (some would say unfortunate) resemblance to our former president Zuma. They had artworks from as early as the 3rd century. we had time to watch a quick changing of the guards outside parliament before heading to the Raffey Bar for some drinks. On our way out, we were accosted by a large Nigerian man with an English accent. He tried to get us to go into his bar. He was quite funny adding not to trust Nigerians! We took his advice and left him with a laugh. Victor had arranged a taxi for us and we parted company to wait for our bus. The driver was an obnoxious guy with a permamnent scowl. His co-driver was young and he served us chocolate croissants and a bottle of water with nervous energy.
After an hour of driving the driver suddenly stopped the bus, turned round in his seat and began shouting at a random passenger sitting next to Dan. It wasn’t clear what the problem was and he started up the bus again and we carried on none the wiser. From the look on the unfortunate passenger’s face, he was equally as perplexed. After a brief toilet stop in Kresna, I bought some roasted sunflower seeds from a small dusty kiosk. Gradually the scenery became more mountainous as we approached the Greek border. The bus driver abrubtly dropped us off at the train station.

## Greece — Thessaloniki

Dan, Tim and I sat at a small outdoor café for coffee bought with euros that we’d scraped together. A taxi took us to our apartment. The driver told us that the president was in Thessaloniki at the moment and there was going to be a demonstration later. He dropped us off after only 1.5 km; too early to check in to the Airbnb so left the pile of luggage outside the door and went in search of more coffee before exploring the city of Thessaloniki. Some old ruins we passed were overrun with feral cats; a remarkably frequent occurrence in Europe. As it was warm we decided to head for the sea. The demonstration of which the driver earlier warned us was in full swing when we got there with loud singing and flag-waving. Graffiti was prevalent around town which wasn’t very clean. A small side street café looked worth trying out so we ordered a dakos and a green salad with olives. It started to rain lightly which increased the humidity so we walked back to the flat to chill a bit. The sun lowered itself gently over the horizen leaving streaks of gold and red in its wake.

Tim found a shop that sold tesbih - prayer beads and chatted to the owner. He was a pleasant Greek/Turkish man who explained the different beads to us and said the warmth of our hands would release different scents depending on the beads. All three of us left with a different set of beads. Mine, which I later hung in my car, smells of cinnamon.

We walked into the village for our morning coffee and sat opposite ‘The Arrogant Bar’ which despite its name, appeared fairly humble then strolled along the edge of the sea looking at boats and ships in the harbour and at sea. A homeless woman wearing a quiet expression, red bandana and a lime green top sat on a bench with two dogs and three cats feeding them breadcrumbs.

Tim hired a car and negotiated his way down some pretty busy streets with Dan navigating on his phone. This turned out not to be his forte. As a result we took a few wrong turns. Once we got out of the city it was a little easier and we all visibly relaxed.
Tim headed for Athens via Verona. The area became more mountainous as we stopped at Orevena for lunch. It seemed to be a town that was big on mushrooms as signs and images of various types of fungi abounded. After coffee at Ventis we drove on. Now the hills and mountains wore green and blue with fields of sunflowers past their sell-by date, turning brown around the edges. As we approached the high mountains of Kastraki we found the most amazing rock structures; straight, flat longitudinal surfaces with jagged tops. Meteora mountains are renowned for their dramatic and unique rock formations. The towering sandstone pillars, some reaching heights of 200 to 400 metres, created a surreal landscape punctuated by medieval monasteries perched at the top of the peaks. The village of Klabaka was quaint and pretty with clean streets, water features and an abundance of colourful flowers against the backdrop of these majestic mountains. Our hostess, Veronica took us to the apartment called ‘Be My Guest’. The boys shared one room with two single beds and I took the double bed as I had the privilege of age. We walked down the sidewalk and found a pretty restaurant decorated with flowers that showed a heart-shaped view of the mountains. Six monasteries clung to the tops of the main mountain and we hiked up to Varlaam Monastery first. Around 1350, a hermit named Varlaam climbed the steep rock and established a chapel dedicated to the ‘Three Hierarchs’.
Then in the early 16th century, brothers Theophanes and Nektarios Apsaras from Ioannina, scaled the rock with ropes and baskets to revive the monastery. Now, beautiful stained-glass windows glowed in the early morning sunlight, gold-leaf paintings brightened the stone walls and intricate painted ceilings depict scenes from the life of Christ and images of saints.

We gazed down to the village nestled at the bottom like a toy town. I found an old hoist called ‘The Net’ which had been used by the monks over the years to bring themselves up the mountainside to the entrance at the top. Back down to Kastraki in search of coffee and lunch where a poster stuck to a wall advertised ‘Grease’ the movie, but it was spelled ‘Greece!’
We left Kastraki early morning and drove down the mountainside towards the sea with beautiful forests en route. Past Gravia, Amiss and through the old industrial village of Elainonas.
 Amfissa was quaint and pretty with the ubiquitous stray cats and dogs everywhere. The owner of the restaurant welcomed us personally. He showed me an old water spring  behind his restaurant and said I should drink the water so I dutifully cupped my hands and took a few mouthfuls. It was cold, sparkling and refreshing. Next he showed me his olive trees and took a few photos of me with his phone while I stood smiling awkwardly before heading back to the restaurant where the lads were laughing at my ‘abduction’.
Tim, Dan and I walked down to the village, past old pomegranate trees and grape vines and found another old church to investigate then Tim drove us to Delphi where they made pots, gold masks and statues. Afterwards we visited the ruins of the Sanctuary of Apollo dating back to the 4th century BCE; this temple is an imposing Doric-order temple whose existence wove through the turbulent history of the site, and underwent three changes before settling into the ruins we found. Aside from a female figure sitting on a three-legged chair, the majority of the exhibits were bronze votive offerings, dating to the 8th and 7th centuries BCE.

Our new rooms were off the street and somewhat difficult to negotiate as the narrow lanes were mostly blocked with parked vehicles. It was exhausting climbing up and down these obstacle-course lanes.
After coffee we drove to the nearby town of Galaxidi. It was a pretty town with bushes of flowers and cacti. I stupidly put my hand on one cucumber-shaped cactus to feel it, as it looked soft. It wasn’t soft at all, instead had tiny hairs that instantly embedded into my flesh and burned like crazy. Painful lesson learned.

We came to a lovely seaside harbour with a small semi-circular black pebble beach with black pebbles. The roadside was full of restaurants and pretty, colourful bougainvillea abounded. We stopped for a feta salad. I asked for extra olives with no cheese.
When I was at boarding school in Ireland, Jax and I went home with the headmistress, Miss Christie, for short holidays as we couldn’t fly home. Miss Christie was a shortish woman with a square face and steel-grey hair swept into a severe bun. She wore stiff jackets over sensible tweed skirts with one glass eye whose ‘focus’ remained straight ahead while the other eye roamed around giving her the appearance of someone not to be trifled with. Despite her outward appearance, she was a kind woman and often took Jax and me to her small goat farm in Northern Ireland. She owned around twenty Boston terriers and of course many goats. Every morning at breakfast she would pour us a glass of ‘fresh’ goat’s milk; rich, foamy and still warm and on occasion with a stray goat hair that floated malevolently on top. I hated it and the warm drink made me heave so Jax was given the task of drinking my glass of milk when her head was turned. I’ve never liked goat’s milk or feta since. I discovered all feta was made with either goat or sheep in Greece.

The lads and I took a look at the ruins of the Temple of Poseidon. The sun was beating down on us even at that early stage of the morning. This Doric-style temple overlooked the Aegean and was dedicated to Poseidon, god of the sea. Its location provided a strategic lookout point and a navigational landmark and the impressive white marble columns were a popular destination for viewing the sunset.

I discovered that despite wearing flip flops, a lot of tiny black urchin spines had embedded in my little toe which were quite painful so I spent most of the drive back trying to pick them out with my tweezers.
We had eight hours to kill until the ferry arrived that would take us to Crete.

The ferry was a huge, ocean liner-sized boat. After storing our big cases in the hold we found our ‘Airseats’, styled like plane seats only a lot more roomy. The lads went off in search of supper while I wandered around before returning to my seat to settle down for a good sleep.

Which never happened. Firstly, the seats were not conducive to a good sleep as they were hard and slippery, so I ended up on the floor at 3 a.m. still awake with my head under the seat to try cut out the light that remained on all night. Our seats were also right in front of a huge television screen which rambled on all night. By 6 a.m., when the ferry docked in Chania, none of us had slept for more than two hours.  Crete is the largest island in Greece and was once home to the Minoan civilisation. The landscape is dominated by majestic mountains and some fascinating pink sand beaches. We were planning on finding one of them while we were there.

Breakfast was Greek yoghurt and honey with toast before climbing steep, rocky steps and walking cobbled streets for the ruins at Theotokopoulou; part of the Old Town of Chania. It's a quaint old town with interesting antique shops, leatherware, old buildings and little side roads,  small shops and the Old Harbour Fort with its round stone-domed roof. My coffee was sipped from a cobbled-stone café down a side street. The boys had a local beer at sundown called ‘A’ (the rest is Greek to me). 

Next morning we headed down towards Nea Kydonia, passing the old ruins towards Glares Beach with its dark grainy sand. It was too pebbly to be comfortable so we continued walking past the red-domed church to Kate Stalos, a coastal village in Chania known for its sandy beaches and family-friendly atmosphere. The name ‘Stalos’ was also associated with a Minoan myth about the giant automaton called Talos; sometimes depicted with wings or throwing boulders to protect the island from invaders. At the restaurant I took out my iPad to draw. Tim wrinkled his nose.

‘What’s that awful smell?’ he asked looking meaningfully at me.

‘I beg your pardon,’ I sniffed. ‘I showered this morning.’ But then I caught a distinctly ichthyic odour that emanated from my lap.

I scrabbled in my handbag and found the culprit; a small sardine from the other day halfway out of a plastic bag. My handbag now smelled ripely of rotten fish.

The tiny ornate Church of Mary Magdalene stood watch nearby wearing a red roof and several turrets. The doorway was just big enough to squeeze through and Tim managed to take a lovely photo of my backside doing just that. We came to a cool wooded area at the sea where someone had tied a bag of small rocks to one of the trees and we tried to figure out the purpose.

‘It’s for chucking at the tourists,’ suggested Dan.

We hired a Mercedes and Tim drove as usual while Dan misread the maps. He’s the only person I’ve met with a worse sense of direction than me.
Topolia is a pretty mountain village with churches and red-roofed houses and we stopped at a small café at the top of the mountain road. It was very colourful with walls of pink bougainvillea, blue hammocks and baskets of bright yellow lemons. Tim tried a traditional Cretan lunch here which was a local sausage, lamb steaks with chips and flatbreads. Long strings of dried chillies swung gently in the wind from metal hooks on the wall.
Afterwards Tim drove us through the tunnel at Mythimna to the west coast where we arrived at our accommodation for the night in Pelekanos. It consisted of one large room with an en suite bathroom and three beds along the wall. It was quite hot in the room so we took a walk past rows of cacti (some with smiles carved out of their succulent green flesh) to the beach which boasted the pink sand we’d read about. Sure enough as we approached the beach, the sea rolled over sand revealing black, beige and pink sand. The pink colour of the sand came from microscopic, single-celled organisms called foraminifera. They had shells surrounding them made from calcium carbonate which was what coloured the sand due to the huge number of these foraminifera. The colour ranged from a pale pink to a deep crimson and all the shades in between. It was quite spectacular.

At the Palace of Knossos, we came across some of the large round pits known as kouloures built in the 1700s. No one really knew what the function of these pits was with their low stone walls, but it was suspected that it was for burning refuse from the palace.
Knossos was an ancient Minoan city that was administered by the Romans. There were some amazingly intact murals on the crumbling walls of this ancient palace. Some of the pillars still had a blue and red colouring. I was fascinated by a tall-backed wooden chair, still intact after all this time. A beautiful peacock strutted around the ruins like royalty with their feathered crowns, jewels of colour against the stony backdrops. Signposts off the road warned us of brown bears. I wished some bears would wander by — I’d love to see one.
