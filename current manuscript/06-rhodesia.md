# Chapter 5: Rhodesia

I was on my way to Rhodesia—this was pre-independence— with my on-again, off-again boyfriend, Gerry. He was currently using one of three aliases, which should have given me pause, but no one listens to that quiet voice in their head when they are young. It might have seemed like a terrible time to travel to a country that was actively at war. In the 1880’s, the region had become a British colony called Southern Rhodesia, which lasted until 1965 when the white minority unilaterally declared independence from Britain to avoid majority rule. After that, the country was known simply as Rhodesia and it was currently in the middle of a brutal civil war that would ultimately last fifteen years. I had taken a short leave of absence from my work in the peritoneal dialysis unit. I’d been to Rhodesia once before but that was as a child with the family many years prior. The main reason for my journey this time was a deeply personal one: my dearest brother, John, had recently been involved in a catastrophic diving accident. He had been diving off rocks and swimming in a river all day with friends and his girlfriend. She had asked him for one last photo of him diving off a rock and he had obliged but didn't surface. One of the party jumped in and discovered him floating unconscious in the water. They pulled him out while another ran to the nearest hotel to summon an ambulance—there were no mobile phones in those days. Long story short, he was paralysed from the neck down and transferred to a specialised spinal unit in Pretoria. He had been there a month before his girlfriend finally alerted me to the fact. It was a terrible shock and I was anxious to see him while we passed through Pretoria. To help cover costs, Gerry had placed an advertisement asking for people to join us and share the petrol. From this we recruited three companions: Jim, Liz, and Ron. We met at Gerry’s place, where he immediately threw a spoke in the wheel by announcing that he would only drive as far as Pretoria and stay there a day or two. He said Jim could take his car—a massive Chrysler V8—and drive the rest of us across the border to Rhodesia where he would meet us later. I wondered how he planned to catch up with us without his car, but felt too annoyed to ask. He added that he had made arrangements for me to stay with Ron at his parents' place in Salisbury, as he didn't want me ‘on his back all holiday’—charming as always. This was obviously one of our ‘off-again’ periods. Gerry drove us all to Pretoria while I sat in the back with Ron and Liz. It was a fairly uneventful drive and we reached the city by midday. After dropping Gerry off, clutching a pile of vinyl records under his arm, Jim took over the steering wheel. We all got on well and the cabin was filled with chatter and laughter. There was a slight snag however, when we discovered we couldn't obtain a re-entry visa for the car until two o'clock the following afternoon. In the meantime, I was dropped off at the H.F. Verwoerd Hospital where John was recovering. To my surprise, the nursing staff informed me that he had gone to the cinema with his physiotherapist. I asked which cinema so I could follow him. By the time I arrived, the film had already started. I explained to the woman at the ticket office that I was a nurse and desperately needed to see my brother for just five minutes. She let me through. John was at the very back, sitting in the aisle in a wheelchair with his physiotherapist beside him. He was shocked but delighted when I knelt down next to him and whispered, ‘Hi, Noj.’ Our pet names for each other were Naj and Noj—Jan and John spelled backwards. He turned his head and I tried my best to prevent my shock from showing on my face. He looked different. His hair was curlier and his arms were folded tightly in front of him as though to keep balanced. I whispered to him that I had come hoping to chat but realised it was terrible timing since he was in the middle of a film and we were leaving the city shortly. I introduced myself to the physiotherapist, chatted briefly to John, gave him a massive hug and promised I would see him again on our way back from Rhodesia. I left the cinema feeling very unsettled and upset. The others kindly gave me space to be quiet in the back of the Chrysler while I tried to come to terms with the devastating reality that my dear brother would never be able to walk again. We set off for the border post at Beitbridge. It was pitch dark when we arrived, so we decided to sleep in the car to ensure we were first in line for customs in the morning. It was bitterly cold and we spent a miserable night trying to keep warm and slept sitting upright. I woke up stiff and tired, stepped out to stretch and use the border post toilets before joining the queue. People had been gathering since 4 a.m. before the tedious bureaucracy of customs commenced. The car had to be insured for the territory, so we pooled our money for the fee. ‘Do not travel alone under any circumstances,’ the border official warned us sternly, instructing us to join a military convoy about a kilometre ahead. We missed the main convoy departure by ten minutes, but Jim drove quickly and we caught up with the tail end of it in Enkeldoorn. An armoured vehicle rumbled ahead of us and five civilian cars moved slowly in front of our Chrysler. We travelled in convoy as far as Gwelo, where we had a quick stretch before heading on to Fort Victoria. There, we spent an hour relaxing, having tea and chatting with some of the other drivers. The town was full of anti-mine vehicles and soldiers clad in camouflage with rifles strapped to their sides. I exchanged some South African Rands for Rhodesian Dollars and bought a sandwich before we continued towards Salisbury, the capital. One of the soldiers looked closely at the Chrysler before we left and remarked that he didn't think we would make it to the capital because the retread rubber on the tyres was unravelling. Although we made it there, the rear tyre was hazardously bare by the time we pulled into Avondale, the pretty, tree-lined suburb of Salisbury where Ron’s parents lived. We met his family and I was shown to my room. Ron’s mother was a physiotherapist so I told her about John and the immense shock of seeing him in the wheelchair. She listened intently, murmuring gentle words of sympathy before suggesting that I later visit a young quadriplegic man she knew named Trevor, so I could ask him questions about managing the condition. The next day, I went into the Avondale shopping centre with Jim and Ron where I bought a pair of leather sandals. Suddenly a commotion erupted and the shop doors were hastily slammed shut. ‘What’s happening?’ I asked a shopkeeper who was frantically packing away some bags. He told me the security forces were searching the premises for insurgent activity. The police performed a thorough body search on several individuals before handcuffing them before systematically searching various vehicles parked outside. Twenty minutes later, the doors were opened and we were told we could leave, which we did with alacrity. The constant presence of armed soldiers and police was deeply disconcerting—a stark, reminder that a war was raging all around us. After breakfast the next morning, we drove into the city centre because Bishop Abel Muzorewa—the Zimbabwean politician who was making history as the Prime Minister of the short-lived Zimbabwe-Rhodesia—was giving a public address. He was arriving at his new place of governance on a traditional wooden cart drawn by four oxen. The cart held all his earthly possessions, including, quite visibly, his wife. We arrived just as a massive crowd had gathered and listened to him speak from his oxen cart. He was wearing a magnificent leopard skin across his back and was waving a short ceremonial spear. His wife sat beside him with a large woven basket balanced on her head, though what was inside it was anyone’s guess. He gave a long, impassioned speech, pausing when he noticed our group in the crowd and remarked that he was very pleased to see some ‘white faces’. Later, we headed over to the Monomotapa Hotel, a magnificent, tall, semi-circular building that offered panoramic views of Salisbury and the many blooming purple jacaranda and copper Msasa trees. I was fascinated to note that we were the only white people utilising the facilities. A striking contrast to South Africa at the time, where strict apartheid laws dictated entirely separate spaces for white and black people. We randomly decided to hitchhike to Bulawayo, so Ron's mother dropped us off on the main highway. Bulawayo was 450 kilometres away from Salisbury and looking back, why we chose to hitchhike in a war zone is completely beyond me, but it was simply how we had always got around. Remarkably, the three of us secured a lift almost immediately from a man in a sedan who took us as far as Gatooma. It took another hour of waiting by the roadside before we caught our next lift with a travelling medical salesman, who talked non-stop all the way to Bulawayo where he was dropping off pharmaceutical supplies. Jim borrowed his sister Wendy’s car the following morning and we took a drive out to the Matopos. Before we left, a friend of Wendy’s handed me a semi-automatic 7.65mm Spanish Star pistol to wear for protection. She provided a leather holster, which I strapped somewhat self-consciously around my waist, praying to God I wouldn't have to use it as I was far more likely to do myself damage. We arrived at the Matobo National Park in south-west Rhodesia. Famous for the Matopo Hills—a breathtaking range of balancing granite rock formations sculpted by centuries of erosion. The ancient walls of Nswatugi Cave featured magnificent Stone Age rock art dating back some 1,300 years. We hiked up the massive boulders to view them. The park boasted a significant population of black eagles and both black and white rhinos. The historic grave of Cecil Rhodes was carved directly into this granite summit of Malindidzimu, a hill known as the ‘View of the World’. We scrambled over the smooth boulders to the gravesite while I kept a nervous hand on my holster, hoping I wouldn't accidentally fire a few shots onto my foot. It was incredibly windy at the summit and my hair whipped around my face like a small tornado. There was a heavy, palpable historical presence carved into those granite rocks and hanging in the crisp air. 
Gerry finally arrived in Rhodesia, accompanied by his friend Connor. The following day we packed up Ron's mother's Citroën as the Chrysler was still out off action and set off for Lake Kariba—a drive of about 490 kilometres. We had borrowed sleeping bags and a canvas tent from Ron’s folks, planning to camp along the way. After stopping at the Sinoia Caves for the night, Ron found a suitable clearing and we set up camp, then walked over to a nearby motel for a much-needed drink. 
After a campfire breakfast of toast and barbecued meat, we set off for the Carabbea Bay Hotel which featured sprawling, magnificent gardens that ran alongside the water's edge. When we arrived, the grounds were teeming with young people—many of them off-duty soldiers in army uniform—and festive celebrations were in full swing with loud music and flowing alcohol. We stood at the bar for a while, drinking cold beers and soaking up the lively atmosphere. A few beers later, a group of Rhodesian army lads loudly suggested a competitive tug-of-war between themselves and the South Africans, pointing directly at Gerry, Connor, and Ron. The stakes were set at a crate of beer. They lined up on the grass and began pulling with everything they had on a thick length of rope they had managed to forage. The Rhodesian soldiers were in peak military condition and effortlessly hauled our boys clean over the line in the sand. They won the match and the crate of beer which Gerry and his companions had to begrudgingly purchase. Gerry was a notoriously bad loser. He made an offensive, insensitive comment to the victors and within seconds, one of the massive Rhodesian soldiers marched over and clobbered Gerry with full force. Gerry was far too intoxicated to defend himself, so I instinctively threw myself between them to intervene. The next thing I knew, another soldier had inadvertently punched me squarely on the jaw, sending me flying backwards onto the grass. It was like being in the middle of a dog fight. I picked myself up, rubbing my bruised chin, just in time to spot my handbag floating away down the fast-flowing Zambezi River. My passport was inside! I scrambled frantically over to the riverbank to retrieve it. Meanwhile, Gerry was being hit repeatedly and was now flailing in the water. I was assisted in pulling my bag from the current while Connor rushed into the shallows to drag Gerry out, only to receive a heavy blow to the face himself. The entire scene had turned terrifyingly violent. These soldiers were fighting a real war; they were full of pent-up energy, trauma, and volatile anger. The hotel manager marched out and ordered our group to leave the premises immediately. We were a sorry-looking bunch back at our campsite when we took stock of the damage. Gerry’s face was a shifting kaleidoscope of bruises, one of his front teeth was broken and his left eye had swollen completely shut. Connor had a heavily bruised jaw and apparently, so did I. Gerry retreated to sleep in the back of the car, looking so thoroughly miserable that I brought him some supper—which he refused—and a pile of blankets. We crawled quietly into our respective tents. The previous night's drama cast a damper on the group the following morning, though Gerry seemed to be in slightly better spirits. I collected some firewood from Epsom, the cook employed at the campsite and built a large fire to boil water for coffee. After washing the dirty utensils, Connor, Jim, Ron, and I drove out to a nearby crocodile farm. The facility was completely deserted, bar a few water tanks and a small detail of soldiers, so we were free to wander around. It looked terribly unkempt, but I suppose the war had ensured fewer visitors and the daily maintenance of the crocodiles had fallen by the wayside. They had massive concrete pools filled with prehistoric-looking crocodiles lying completely motionless in the sun. Wandering through an old display room, I found a glass specimen jar containing a perfectly preserved crocodile foetus and was tempted to take it for my collection of old bones and feathers. After one final breakfast over the coals we joined the military convoy and headed back to Salisbury, arriving shortly after lunch. At this point, I had begun worrying about my job at the hospital. I was supposed to be back on duty in a few days' time but was reliant on returning with Gerry who’d arbitrarily decided he wanted to extend his stay in Rhodesia. Panicking, I walked down to the post office, drafted a formal telegram and sent it to the Chief Matron of the hospital, stating that due to ‘unfortunate circumstances’, my return to Cape Town would be delayed. Exactly what these circumstances were would be decided nearer the time. 
The following morning, I decided visit Trevor Blythe, the young quadriplegic man Ron’s mother had mentioned. I found him lying in a bright room, propped up on a mountain of white pillows. I introduced myself and sat by his bedside, chatting with him for well over an hour. He was a lovely, inspirational person and I gave him a warm hug before I left. I spoke briefly with his mother on the way out, then walked back to the house, thoughts fluttering around my brain like trapped butterflies. I decided the first thing I had to do for John was to arrange his medical transfer to a spinal unit in Cape Town so I could visit regularly. 
Our group reached the Beitbridge border post by 6 p.m. We cleared customs successfully but desperately needed a place to sleep for the night. A roadside motel was nearby, but since funds were tight, Gerry and Connor shared a room while Jim and I volunteered to sleep in the Chrysler. It was bitterly cold and incredibly uncomfortable and neither of us slept much. I woke early and Gerry dropped me off near the Pretoria hospital, telling me bluntly that he would return to collect me exactly one hour later. I walked around the massive corridors for ages before finally locating John's ward. Gerry had given me a ridiculously short window of time, but I eventually found John sitting in a sunny solarium. His face lit up the moment he saw me. Conscious of the ticking clock, I bypassed small talk and got right to the point. I told him firmly that I was going to pull every string available to arrange his transfer to Cape Town. He seemed relieved and visibly relaxed. We chatted about his rigorous physiotherapy sessions and I brought him up to date with the family news. Mum was still in the UK and had only just found out about the accident after receiving my letter; she was naturally devastated and was trying to secure a flight out to South Africa as a matter of urgency. I shared the helpful tips Trevor’s mother had given me, but the hour flew by far too quickly. Soon, I was hugging my brother goodbye, promising to see him again very soon. I left him sitting alone in the sunroom, his arms folded in front of him and a wave of intense guilt washed over me. I hated Gerry for giving me so little time with him. When I walked back down to the designated drop-off point, Gerry wasn't there. I waited, and waited, and waited. A full hour later, his car finally rumbled up. I was furious; I could have spent that time with my brother. When I finally arrived back at the small apartment I shared with Jacqui in Cape Town, I brought her up to date on the journey. I was emotionally and physically spent, so I said a quiet goodnight and went straight to my room, dreading the morning. I hoped I still had a job. 
The next morning, Jacqui and I walked to the railway station to take the suburban train to work; she worked at the same hospital as an ECG technologist. I headed straight to the Chief Matron’s office and sat down, facing a panel of two. They naturally wanted an explanation for my week-long absence. I started to explain, but frayed nerves and raw emotions took over and I sobbed into my hankie, explaining that I had been trapped in war-torn Rhodesia while visiting my brother, who had just been tragically paralysed. I became incredibly economical with the truth; I implied, without explicitly stating it, that he had been wounded in action during the civil war. There was a magnificent amount of creative inference going on between my heavy sobs. The matrons showed sympathy but remained firm on hospital policy: I would have to work back my missed time, a total of eight days. This meant that for the next two and a half months, I would only receive one day off a week. Relieved that was over, I walked down to my unit, unlocked the doors and began preparing the complex lines of the peritoneal dialysis machines before my four patients arrived. When they were hooked up, they told me how much they had missed me, which brought a much-needed smile to my face. Later, I met Jacqui in the staff tearoom and filled her in on the disciplinary meeting. She remarked that I was lucky not to have been sacked, but she burst out laughing when I told her the matrons genuinely believed I had been dodging bullets and landmines on the front lines in Rhodesia. Looking back at the Carabbea Bay Hotel fiasco, some of it wasn't entirely far from the truth. 

Colin

This memoir would not be complete without recounting exactly how I met my husband. I was in the car with Gerry, who had drifted back into my life after a six-month separation. We had been back together for almost a year, but recently arguing incessantly and the relationship was distinctly rocky. We’d spent the afternoon in Hout Bay Hotel where a man at the bar claimed he could read palms. He’d stared intently at my right palm for a minute before pronouncing I would marry a dark-haired man and have one male child. Gerry, being distinctly blonde, was not amused and loudly denounced the man as a complete fake. We were returning home along Camps Bay on the winding, tree-lined coastal road when suddenly a black BMW came zooming up aggressively behind us. The driver attempted to overtake us on a blind bend, pulling back into the lane just in the nick of time as an oncoming vehicle rounded the corner from the opposite direction. He repeated this hazardous manoeuvre several times before finally pulling out again, almost forcing our van into a ditch as he cut us off and sped away. Gerry managed to steer the car safely back onto the tarmac, but he was now as mad as a box of frogs. He gave chase, tailing the BMW as it flew around the twisting corners in the distance. The road eventually intersected and we followed the vehicle down into the lower main road. The road expanded into two lanes and up ahead the traffic light turned red. Gerry surged past the BMW, slammed on his brakes and shoved open the driver's door, saying he was going to ask that maniac why he was driving like a lunatic. Gerry had a volatile temper and at six foot tall with the rugged face of a boxer, he could look incredibly intimidating. I sat in the passenger seat, keeping a steady, anxious eye on the red traffic light, thinking, Come on, hurry up and get back in the car. I turned around to look out the back window to find Gerry bent over the driver's side of the BMW, gesticulating wildly and shouting. I sighed and turned back around in my seat. The traffic lights shifted to amber, when suddenly, the unmistakable, deafening crack of a gunshot echoed throughout the car. I swung around, my heart hammering wildly against my ribs. I knew for a fact that Gerry didn't possess a weapon of any sort. At first, I couldn't see him through the window. Then, to my horror, I saw him collapsed on the tarmac near the rear of the BMW. As I watched in shock, the car suddenly revved its engine and zoomed off into the traffic. I threw open my door, scrambled out of the van and ran to where Gerry was writhing on the tarmac. ‘I’ve been shot!’ he gasped, disbelief and shock written all over his face. ‘He could have killed me!’ He groaned heavily, clutching at his leg. The muscle at the back of his thigh was drawn up tight and a small pool of blood was spreading onto the tarmac. As I pressed a thick wad of tissues onto the wound to stem the bleeding, a small crowd of onlookers materialised. A few minutes later, the wail of a siren announced the arrival of an ambulance. I was still in a state of shock when a kind man stepped out of the crowd and told me he would move Gerry’s van out of the traffic or drive me to the hospital in it, noticing I was far too shaken to drive. I nodded numbly. Fortunately, the hospital was only a few streets away. Gerry had left the keys dangling in the ignition, so my rescuer simply climbed into the driver's seat and we shot off. He parked at the emergency unit and asked if I was going to be alright. I nodded and thanked him profusely. He told me he would walk back to collect his own car and I thanked him again as he disappeared. Rushing inside the casualty entrance, I frantically asked the nurse at reception where they had taken Gerry. She gestured towards a treatment door to the left, trying to tell me that the doctor was busy with him, but I ignored her and burst through the doors. Gerry was lying on a narrow metal trolley, groaning in agony. The doctor was bending over him, trying to administer an intravenous injection to ease the pain so he could properly assess the trajectory of the bullet. He looked up sharply as I made my dramatic entrance. ‘Who are you?’ he demanded. I replied that Gerry was my boyfriend, that I was a trained nurse and could assist. He nodded, 
‘Okay, fetch that intravenous set from over there on the shelf and bring it to me,’ he instructed, pointing towards an open cupboard. I brought it over and he added, ‘You’d better wash your hands if you’re going to help.’ I spent the next twenty minutes assisting him as he expertly inserted a cannula into Gerry's vein, while Gerry looked green. I cut several strips of micropore tape and handed them to the doctor, peering closely at his plastic name badge as I did so. Dr C. Mostert, it read. He opened the fluid line, straightened his back and gave me a grim, tired smile. ‘What happened? He says a man just shot him from a car?’ I quickly recounted the road-rage events that had led to the shooting while Gerry lay quietly groaning on the trolley. Dr Mostert told me I had better head to the waiting room as Gerry would likely need to go to the operating theatre later to have the bullet extracted. He thanked me for my assistance and I said goodbye to Gerry, who had finally fallen asleep. I don't remember how I managed to get home that night, but the following weeks were a chaotic blur of hospital visits, police statements and long shifts at work. Gerry was ultimately discharged with the bullet left intact; the surgical team decided that attempting to dig it out would cause far more nerve damage than leaving it alone. The morning after the shooting, I was walking to work and passed a newspaper vendor on the pavement. I did a double-take. There, in bold print on the front-page placard, was the headline: Motorists in Sea Point Shooting. I bought a copy and read the dramatic report of how a ‘confrontation between two motorists in Sea Point ended in an assault and a shooting.’ The article went on to quote me directly, detailing how I had explained the incident to the authorities. I had absolutely no memory of speaking to a reporter but assumed a policeman or a journalist must have been there. None of us could understand why the driver had shot Gerry. The state subsequently lodged a formal charge of attempted murder against the driver and I was subpoenaed to appear at the Supreme Court as a key witness. On the day of the trial, the court was already in progress and I was instructed to sit on a wooden bench outside the courtroom doors until called to give testimony. I sat down next to someone, only realising it was Dr Mostert when he turned, smiled and asked how I was holding up. We sat together on the bench, discussing the medical details of the case to pass the time until the heavy doors finally opened and the bailiff called my name as the first witness. The shooter; a wealthy businessman, had given himself up at the police station. His counsel was a terrifying man to be on the receiving end of. His questions to me were blunt to the point of rudeness; I felt as though I were the one on trial. I remember using the word ‘gesticulated’ to describe the scene and he repeated the word back to me with a sneer. I detested his manner but answered the questions as best I could and left the witness stand trembling with anger. Outside, it was Colin’s turn to give evidence as the doctor who had treated the bullet wound. He testified that the trajectory of the bullet into the back of Gerry’s leg clearly indicated that he had already turned away from H.L. and therefore could not have posed a threat to him. Knowing how terrifying the defence counsel's cross-examination was, I turned to Colin as he rose to enter the courtroom and said, ‘Good luck in there. You’re going to need it!’ The next day, the case was front-page news, complete with a photograph of Gerry and myself leaving the court. Gerry looked like a thoroughly upstanding member of society in his black suit and yellow tie, captured mid-stride and leaning on a wooden walking stick. I was looking down, my face vividly betraying the strain of the court session; I was wearing my nurse's uniform, as I had to return to work afterwards. As a result of the press coverage, I found myself a bit of a local celebrity with total strangers calling out to say how sorry they were about what had happened and hoping my boyfriend would get justice. Everyone at work stopped me to ask about the case. This scenario continued for a month, with frequent trips to court. Each time, I found Colin sitting on the wooden bench outside and we chatted some more. We eventually moved away from discussing the case and began asking each other more personal questions. I learned that he was originally from Zimbabwe and in a few months' time, would be leaving South Africa to join the well-known, ‘Operation Raleigh’, travelling to South America as the expedition doctor—which sounded incredibly exciting. His father was a doctor, as was mine. We both shared remarkably similar backgrounds growing up: he in Rhodesia, as it was then and me in Uganda. 
The results of the court case arrived a month later, on the same day that my good friend Viv had asked me to join her to celebrate her birthday. To our outrage, the wealthy businessman won the case, successfully citing that he had been in fear for his life when he shot Gerry. Strange then that he’d found it necessary to lean all the way out of his car window to shoot a man who was running away from him. It was a complete travesty of justice. His lawyer was right at the top of his profession, cost a absolute fortune and obviously knew exactly how to manipulate the system. I felt incredibly deflated and irritated by the outcome. Although Gerry and I were taking another of our standard ‘breaks’ from the relationship, I still felt bad that justice had not been served. Gerry still had the bullet lodged firmly in his leg, an injury that would affect his mobility for years to come. I told Viv that as it was a Sunday night, I really wasn't feeling up to celebrating, using the excuse of having to work the next morning, but she managed to convince me to go to a venue called Seagulls in Sea Point for ‘just one drink’. It was her birthday, after all and I reasoned that perhaps I would feel a little better if I got out of the flat for a bit. A few hours later, I arrived at the venue clutching a present, fully planning on taking a taxi home a short while later. I followed the sounds of early revelry upstairs and located Viv alongside a large table of friends. I greeted her with a hug, handed over her present and whispered that I might not stay for very long. I took a vacant seat and chatted to the people around me for a minute or two, when I had the distinct feeling that someone was staring at me. I glanced around the crowded table and my eyes fell on a man who seemed familiar, though in this setting I couldn’t quite place him. He smiled and we simultaneously said, ‘Hi! How do you know Viv?’ It was Colin, Gerry’s casualty doctor. He immediately moved over to join me on the empty seat to my right. I was rather pleased, because the man on my left had been trying to chat me up, but I found him boring. Colin told me he knew Viv from ballroom dancing classes; it turned out he was the dance partner she had been telling me about who worked at Somerset Hospital. Four hours of effortless chat and shared laughter later, he offered to drive me home. I thanked Viv for the invitation and she smiled, nodding knowingly towards Colin. ‘See you at dance class next week!’ she called out with a laugh, adding that perhaps I would be joining them too. I laughed at the thought, picturing myself attempting formal ballroom dancing; I was more of the ‘fling yourself wildly around the dance floor’ type of girl. I climbed into Colin’s blue Mazda 323 and felt a few loose springs press uncomfortably into the back of my thighs. We chatted all the way back to my place in Mowbray. I was completely fascinated by his gear changes; the gearbox had apparently come entirely loose from its structural moorings, and every time Colin changed gear, the entire mechanism shifted around as one floating unit. He dropped me off at home, we exchanged telephone numbers—strictly landlines in those days—and I figured that was more or less that. He was flying out to the South American jungle for six months in a mere seven weeks' time, so I doubted he would have time for anything more than work. In fact, he did phone me, inviting me to a braai at a friend's house. For the next seven weeks we met up regularly. By the time the day of his departure arrived, he turned up at my apartment with his car, a few boxes of books and his prized collection of butterflies. He told me he was leaving his car for me to use while he was away and asked if I would look after his belongings. The next day, he was off on his grand adventure as an expedition doctor. He gave me his poste restante address and over the coming months wrote me long, descriptive letters. After his return to Cape Town, we picked up where we had left off, bought a little house together in Table View right next to the vlei, and four years later we were married. Nine months after that, our son, Timothy, was born. The Hout Bay palm reader had been right after all: Colin had dark hair and I only ever had one son.
