# Chapter 42: Singapore 2024

Today would have been Jonny’s birthday, and I suddenly realise that I am due to return on the anniversary of his death. I shake my head, trying to clear my sad thoughts. Henry is my Uber driver today and we chat all the way to the airport. 
Once on board, I’m feeling hungry, having last eaten yesterday at 2 p.m., so there’s not much left of my five inch square of veg curry. I diligently work my way through the entire tray, even grabbing my companion's cheese when he hands back his tray.
I ask the air hostess what local time will be on arrival and note that it’s cutting my connection time very fine. As soon as we land, I’m one of those obnoxious people who stand up immediately and hover in the aisle, ready to leave. I rush past everyone, saying, ‘Excuse me, excuse me!’ and arrive breathless at passport control. Luckily, it doesn’t take long and I manage to get to the gate just as they call passengers to board. This time I am seated next to a Thai woman and her small, as in stature, daughter and I’m crammed next to the window seat. They do not speak English so I just smile and mime when I need to ask them something. This plane is full and noisy with several crying babies and screaming toddlers. Changi Airport is hot and damp and I feel like yesterday’s breakfast.
The entire process for entering the country is digital. I need to complete a long questionnaire using a self-service passport control (I need assistance, though, as I have tried every combination for placing my passport on the pad except tossing it into the air and seeing where it lands). I find my suitcase without a problem and go in search of coffee. They only have rice or soya milk. I choose the soya, which doesn't taste great but I need the caffeine then head towards the famous Rain Vortex, the world's tallest indoor waterfall, which is encased in a glass and steel dome that lets in natural light. It is surrounded by thousands of trees and plants, plus the Skytrain track that connects Terminals 1, 2, and 3. There are already hundreds of visitors gazing into their phones for a selfie. I take several photos of the water feature and the gorgeous, steaming tropical plants. I read that a yoga class is available at Canopy Park and that there’s an indoor pool and a movie theatre. Changi has won a multitude of awards and I can see why. I’m feeling tired, so I take my cases outside and wait for a Grab.

The driver is a pleasant man who speaks good English, so we chat all the way to my hotel. He gives me some tips for local eating and points out some buildings of historical interest. One such building is the long-named, ‘Nan Hwa Chong Fish Head Steamboat Market’. The story goes; someone with a name that sounds like a sneeze, ‘Ah Chew’ to be exact, arrived in Singapore from China and started selling traditional fish soup using a wei lu (charcoal hotpot) and fish heads. The soup was boiled over a charcoal fire. The restaurant is called a ‘steamboat’ after the immigrants who arrived that way ninety years before. I make a mental note to tell Tim about it.
Once we reach our destination, I discover that I have not changed the payment on my Grab app from cash to card, and I don’t have any Singapore dollars. The driver suggests that I ask the receptionist. I trail after him up the small, wide steps into the Rest Bugis Hotel. The receptionist smiles a welcome and I immediately ask her if I can borrow S$20.50! She looks nonplussed for a minute until I explain why. She tells us that the hotel doesn’t have cash, but she reaches into her own purse and pays the driver. I thank him and turn to the receptionist, who says her name is Maria.
‘Thank you so much,' I burble gratefully. ‘ I’ll pay you back as soon as I find an ATM’.
She smiles and I’m shown my room by the bellhop. I follow him idly wondering why they’re called bellhops as he’s neither hopping nor bell-shaped. He comes to the door and opens it carefully as though to avoid startling a naked guest who’s forgotten to check out. The room is really small. It’s so small I’d have to step outside to change my mind. There’s only room for a bed and a small sideboard. It is also very hot, so I put my key card in the slot and wait for it to cool down. In the meantime, I change into something cooler and go down to ask Maria for directions to the ATM.
The damp heat slaps me in the face as I leave the air-conditioned hotel to find a 7-Eleven. I am directed across the road and find a few ATMs in a closed room. I choose one and I’m pleased when it spits out a few hundred Singapore dollars. I return to the 7-Eleven and buy some nori and a small container of fresh milk then wander around, looking at colourful buildings and shop windows. The many hued houses remind me of District Six back home. There are quite a few mosques as well and I later hear that this is a predominantly Muslim area. I repay Maria and we chat during the course of which, she asks me if I have ever tasted durian. I tell her I haven’t but have heard it has a rather unpleasant smell. She says it’s delicious and she will show me a place where I can try some after she knocks off at 7 p.m. I go to my cupboard sized room and jump onto the bed to lie down for a short rest. The air conditioner doesn’t appear to be working so it’s only a little cooler. I fiddle with the remote and go back down to join Maria. She changes into a dress, brushes her lovely dark hair and we descend the steps back into the humidity. The light is dwindling as dusk falls.
Maria stops outside a small, open-fronted shop. The smell of durian permeates the air long before we got here. They only sell durian and there are shelves of the fruit as well as boxes for gifts. It is a polarising delicacy; people typically either adore it or find it completely overwhelming.The fruit is encased in a thick, beige husk covered in sharp, woody thorns.Maria tells me not all durian is equal and the best is of a certain ripeness. She speaks in rapid Mandarin to the shopkeeper, who brings out half a durian from a small fridge and selects a morsel with a knife for me to try. I hold my breath and slip a large piece down my gullet. It is hard to describe the flavour; thick and creamy, like a sweet custard and not nearly as bad as the smell. But I decide immediately that there’s no way I am going to eat a whole one. I smile politely and say how tasty it is. I can see from Maria’s wistful expression that it’s a rare treat for her, as it’s also quite expensive. I tell her I’ll buy a gift box and give it to her for her family. The shop keeper gives me the rest of the piece that I tasted and I decide I will keep it in the fridge for Tim. Maria is delighted with her smelly fruit but warns me against eating it in the hotel room, as durian is not allowed. I tell her I will keep it wrapped in the small bar fridge and she nods her agreement. I walk back to the room for a cold shower in the cramped cubicle then take one step from the shower onto the bed. I’m snoring away almost immediately but wake up an hour later, sweating like crazy and red-faced. It is so hot in the room I can hardly breathe. I’m suddenly feeling really awful, so I get dressed and walk down to the night receptionist to ask her to either change my room or see to the air conditioner. The woman is dressed in a niqab and abaya and only her eyes are showing. These eyes swivel towards something on the wall and she says that my air-con is not even on. She fiddles with the remote switch and says it should be working now. I stagger back to the room. It does cool down, but only to 25°C, so my sleep is fitful.
I wake up feeling quite groggy after a long but disturbed sleep, as the temperature in the room is still uncomfortable. Tim messages to say he is on his way, so I get dressed and wait in the lobby for him. I ask Maria as soon as I see her if she can turn the air-con right down and she says she will put an extra card in the room so it stays cool all the time, not just when the room card is in. I receive two more messages on my phone; one from my tenant at home saying there’s mould in the bedroom and an instant booking from Airbnb ( the latter because I’d forgotten to block off the dates). Both messages send me into a tizzy. I hate trying to sort things out from the other side of the planet. I look up and see Tim approaching the glass door. We hug and I smile and introduce him to Maria. She gives him his room card and welcomes him. Tim tells me that he got up at 5 a.m. this morning and took his motorbike to the airport in Bali, where he left it in a car park. He goes to look at his room and says it’s so small he can barely fit in the toilet without being simultaneously in the shower. It’s not ideal, but it is only for a few more days.
My right eye is giving me trouble. It is swollen, painful, and tearing up and feels worse as the day progresses.  My eye has now swollen up drastically and I can’t see anything out of it. We find a chicken restaurant and try a local-style roast sesame chicken with lemon rice. Tim says I should try some chicken feet but I’m definitely not going there. We walk on to a large mall and find a Starbucks, where we sit ( me with one eye closed and oozing) with our iced ‘gochicha’ green tea. 
I want to visit the famous Raffles, named after Sir Stamford Raffles, the British diplomat and founder of modern Singapore. The hotel quickly became the place for glamorous, globetrotting socialites from all over the world and staying here became a rite of passage among adventurers. The hotel opened on 1st December 1887. The famous drink called the ‘Singapore Sling’ was created by the Raffles bartender, Ngiam Tong, in 1915. It consists of mostly gin with lime and pineapple juice, curaçao, grenadine, and cherry liqueur. We come across a few staff members catching bats in a mist net that is spread over a few trees. They ask me not to photograph them so we just stand watching for a minute or two. One of the men says, when I enquire what type of bats, that they are the common Lesser Dog-faced Fruit Bat which makes me want to see them even more.
As tables are limited, a waiting time is inevitable. A waiter leads us to an interior with a long wooden bar, a black-and-white circular-patterned floor, faux round marble-top tables, wingback and cane chairs, and wooden finishes. Rows of actual raffia ceiling fans move in opposing directions from a thick wooden beam.
We perch on stools with a drinks menu. A huge hessian bag of red-skinned peanuts in their shells sits in the centre of the table. A waitress approaches and we order one Singapore Sling for myself and a Sakura Sling for Tim. His drink is made from sake instead of gin and contains German elderflower liqueur, Calvados, hibiscus and cayenne pepper cordial, and green Chartreuse. 
I gaze around the busy room with piles of peanut shells all over the floor. From this, I gather it’s okay to grab handfuls of peanuts, remove the shells, and toss them on the floor. This must be the only place in Singapore where littering is allowed.
Our drinks arrive and we sip cautiously. They are the most expensive drinks I have ever bought at S$70 each. Although delicious, they’re definitely not worth paying for more than one. The place is full of tourists and we surmise there will be a new line of them waiting to come in, so we slurp down the expensive nectar, pay, and leave for our tiny room.
I sleep badly. The bed is too small, my nose too blocked, my eye too painful, and my head too jet-lagged. I am still staring, open-mouthed with a stuffy noseso I get up, feeling very delicate with a puffy, weeping, red eye. I dress in the shower/toilet. Tim suggests we stop at a small GP's surgery down a side street. Inside, the only furniture is a small desk with a receptionist on one side and four chairs on the other. The doctor is a small, brown-haired woman who listens to my tale of woe intently. She takes my blood pressure, pulse, temperature and weight before gazing into my eye with her penlight torch. She is very thorough but says she is unsure if it is conjunctivitis or glaucoma - the former easy to treat, the latter requiring surgery. I meet Tim and tell him I need to go to the hospital. He looks alarmed, so I reassure him, saying I think it is just conjunctivitis.
It is quiet inside Raffles Hospital so I don’t have to wait long before seeing another doctor. He also seems unsure or unwilling to give a straight diagnosis and says he will refer me to an eye specialist and warns me it will be expensive. I tell him I would rather just  monitor the eye myself. I’m convinced it’s just infected and ask for antibiotic drops. I return to the receptionist and pay S$160 for fifteen minutes of mostly self-diagnosis. My sore eye, blistered feet, and lack of sleep are catching up with me, so we find another mall for a drink. Next, we walk through the Gardens by the Bay where there are many interesting water features, ponds, statues, birds and small forested areas. It is very pleasant walking around despite the heat. Lily-trotters pick their way daintily over large lily pads. They have long toes and claws that enable them to walk on floating vegetation such as lily pads.
A restaurant we pass sells ginger claypot and onion frogs and if that isn’t an awful enough vision, they can also serve to the discerning diner, ’Frog Porridge’ I gulp and hurry on.
We order a Tiger beer at a small brewery. Dragonfly Lake with the Singapore version of the London Eye lies in the distance with Marina Bay Sands Mall, where we have made a reservation for dinner. The mall is built to resemble a ship atop three buildings and hangs high above the city. We find the Chinese restaurant we booked, called simply, ’Blossom’ and led to a round, linen-covered table by a waitress wearing a black dress and a bright smile. We order duck, chicken, a mushroom and rice dish, dumplings, and some tasty sauces. The meal is tasty and very filling but the cheque makes my right eye even more watery. 
Tim wakes me up at 8:30 a.m. with the great news that it is 8:30 a.m. I’m still groggy with sleep but get dressed so we can take a bus into town. Singapore is very tech-savvy, something you notice on arrival at the airport. It is very hot despite the early hour as we head towards the iconic, lotus-shaped ArtScience Museum. It is refreshingly cool inside. This exhibit is huge and features interactive digital installations and there are twenty-one galleries, so we start on the first floor, which is all about digital art; in one room, for example, you are surrounded by ‘flowers’ which move as you move and form clusters all over the walls and floor. Another exhibit allows you to draw or colour in various sea creatures which are later animated.  Another room is entirely surrounded by floating sunflowers and daisies. We move on to a narrow passage filled with beautiful curtains of light which flash and glow as you pass by and are super ethereal. Some idiotic tourists are trying to walk between them despite notices asking to stay on the path.
Tim says he wants a fruit bowl having spied a vendor at the side of the harbour so we order an açaí berry, coconut and chia seed bowl and sit on a nearby bench eating it with plastic spoons. I notice some activity in the sea and wander over to look. 
I can’t believe my eyes when I spot two smooth-coated otters. They have swum right up to the edge of the marina; each clutching a huge red emperor snapper in their paws. I am delighted at this unexpected sighting and discover later that these otters are part of the ‘Bishan 10’ and are quite famous.  They were first spotted in 2014 in Bishan-Ang Mo Kio Park, a rare sighting in an urban area. They moved to Marina Bay after clashing with another group of otters. Sir David Attenborough described them in a documentary in 2016.
A group of young locals ask me to take photos of them against the iconic Singaporean backdrop and they perform the usual contortions as I snap away. A plump man with several cameras slung around his neck sits next to me on the bench and introduces himself as a photographer from Malaysia. We chat about our travels until Tim returns. Our next stop is the marketplace at Lau Pa Sat, a huge place dotted with stalls selling all kinds of Asian-inspired fare. There are numerous wooden tables and chairs on which to eat. We both order a traditional hawker-style fish soup which consists of a tasty broth with wide cabbage leaves and vegetables. It is topped with some unidentifiable crispy stuff—probably fried onion. I’m amused to see that ‘Buddha Jumps Over the Wall’ is a dish on offer. On investigation, I find out it’s an aromatic Chinese soup, a luxurious dish featuring a variety of ingredients like abalone, sea cucumber, and ginseng. It’s known for its rich, complex, and savoury flavour.
After lunch, we walk into Chinatown and find a small brewery there called ‘Lion Brewery’. We pop in for a beer and   I order a raspberry Lion beer, which, unsurprisingly, tastes of raspberries then we walk to Clarke Quay to watch the sunset. In the distance an enormous swing chair gently rises into the air with people strapped in. There’s a lot of screaming which I’m hoping is signalling fun and not actual pain. The bridge is soon lit up and glowing in the evening light. Soon boats, buildings and even people are glittering with white, red and multi coloured lights making for an atmospheric scene. We walk along the bridge around 8pm and suddenly there’s an explosion of fireworks lighting up the sky. This is apparently in preparation for celebration of Singapore’s independence from Britain 49 years ago. A fighter jet roars overhead with deafening sound. Mach 4 or 5 I suppose.

After a slow start to the morning, I pack and discover a nasty smell when I open the bar fridge. There’s a piece of rotten durian wrapped in flimsy tissue paper. The smell of fresh durian is not unlike a pair of hikers week old sweat-stained socks; this is a double whammy when I find the small carton I was supposed to have given Tim when it still looked like fruit. I chuck it into the waste bin with several dry heaves. It takes me another ten minutes to clean the fridge of any lingering smells.
After leaving our luggage we set off in search of a Pondok for some Laksa, another local dish consisting of fish or chicken, lemongrass, vegetables and noodles.
The Asian Art museum has eleven rooms of exhibits with works by Dada, Dali, Balinese, Chinese, Japanese and Javanese art. I find it very interesting despite my sore blistered feet and spend an hour walking up and down and through the various exhibits. I order a taxi for the airport where check in is completed online at one of the computers for this purpose. We have a drink at one of the many cafe’s. A white robot with a shiny black tray moves silently around the floor collecting dirty plates and cups from customers. I almost drop my mug while trying to place it on the tray as it passes. We’re the first on board Air Asia, a small plane that will take us to Bali. 
As I sit back sipping my water, I think about my impression of Singapore. It wasn’t what I was expecting. I thought it would be hot and humid and all the buildings would have vertical plants. Certainly it was hot and there were a few buildings with said plants but overall it appeared to be a mix of Bali, Korea and maybe Japan in design. The people are mostly polite and spoke good English. There are a lot of rules which is why I suppose the place works and is very clean, but it also seemed a bit ‘1984’ at times when everyone is encouraged to look out and report people ‘doing the wrong thing’.
