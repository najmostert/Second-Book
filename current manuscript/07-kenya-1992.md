# Chapter 6: Kenya 1992

I was six months pregnant, so we thought it would be a wonderful idea to embark on a late honeymoon—a rugged, backpacking sort of vacation. Kenya in the late afternoon was humid so we stood with our bags and heavy cases, looking around hopefully for a vehicle. We didn't have long to wait before locating a friendly taxi driver to take us into central Nairobi, which provided me with my first opportunity to speak Swahili after many years. ‘Wewe unajua wapi Hotel Ambassadeur?’ I asked him over the back of his seat. ‘Ndio, Memsahib. Sio mbali,’ he replied with a smile. It wasn’t far. Col and I looked out eagerly at the passing countryside and chatting to the driver. The road was deeply pot-holed, the distant mountains dressed in soft pastel colours and people were walking along the roadside carrying immense loads on their heads and backs. We arrived at the Hotel Ambassadeur—though anything less ambassadorial I can’t imagine—within half an hour. Our room was on the top floor. By now, the shoulder strap of my rucksack had snapped completely, forcing me to clutch it awkwardly with one hand to keep the entire bag from spilling open and ejecting its contents onto the pavement. Our tour bus finally arrived with Fred Ngoli in tow. Fred, a friendly man from the Luo tribe, was to be our guide for the remainder of the week. He was a stocky fellow with a round face and a permanent smile and he wore a light brown safari suit. We met the only other member of our tour party; an elderly man from the United States with the delightful name of Wendell Stout. He turned out to be a pleasant, quietly spoken man with a magnificent shock of steel-grey hair. As our bus began to navigate the bumpy road towards the Maasai Mara, we chatted companionably about our respective experiences in Africa; he told us that this was his second trip to East Africa. The landscape didn't just change; it practically erupted. After a steady, long climb up the mountain, the world suddenly dropped away into the Great Rift Valley—a gargantuan scar on the earth’s surface that managed to look simultaneously rugged and ethereal. This colossal geological theatre was a jagged system of deep trenches and towering ridges, carved out over millennia by the slow, relentless pull of tectonic plates. As the earth literally tore itself apart, it left behind a breathtaking tapestry of plunging escarpments and shimmering soda lakes; we were totally captivated. It was also wonderful to experience the red murram roads again for the first time since leaving Uganda, alongside tall, yellow savannah grass so distinctive of East Africa. Fred, noticing that I could speak Swahili, remarked kindly that I spoke ‘like a native!’ and began asking me questions in the language. I practised a few replies, haltingly at first, until the vocabulary came flooding back to me. We had the first of many inevitable stops at a tourist curio stall, bringing with it the first experience of the holiday with an East African ‘long drop’ toilet. It consisted of a dilapidated wooden shack situated some feet away from the curio stalls, with a partly rotting swing door hanging precariously from broken beams, around which buzzed a dense multitude of huge, shiny green flies. Because the interior was pitch black, I had to carefully shuffle inside until I located the pit by means of cautious prodding with the toe of my shoe. The dim light revealed a large brown cockroach scuttling frantically across the dirt floor. I am reasonably certain I was in and out of there in under thirty seconds. Our next stop was at a local ‘hoteli’—which was really just a tiny roadside shack; the word ‘hotel’ was considered an essential status addition to any of these small dukas, even if most didn't possess guest rooms, or even walls for that matter. We had a cup of chai and a half-melted chocolate bar each, then pressed onwards towards Narok, the gateway town through which almost all transport enters the Maasai Mara. Our third stop was at an authentic Maasai boma. After conversing with the Chief’s son in Swahili, I managed to haggle the price of viewing the boma down a little. The village consisted of a traditional circular kraal with mud-and-wattle walls and packed dung floors where the ngombe (cows) wandered in and out at will. The walls surrounding the settlement were constructed of nothing more than tightly packed, viciously spiked branches of thorn trees, perfectly suiting a nomadic, pastoralist tribe. The Maasai were a magnificent, proud nation, intensely colourful in their distinctive red shúkàs and intricate beaded jewellery. I took a few photographs and haggled over the price of two beaded bracelets, which I eventually secured for 100 shillings, after explaining to them that ‘Bwana’—meaning ‘big boss’ but also doubled as ‘husband’—had purchased his entire ceremonial shield and spears for only 300 shillings. An elder, or mzee, was sitting outside the largest hut as befitted his senior station, posing with quiet dignity for a few extra shillings. 

Just before we crossed the boundary into Keekorok camp, we stumbled across two lions lying casually by the side of the road. One was a beautiful, black-maned male. We sat watching them in silence for a while before pressing on. Keekorok was the oldest lodging in the reserve and a lovely-looking camp, with brightly coloured bougainvillea dotted around the grounds. Our tent was comfortably furnished with two camp beds and a wooden writing table, all sheltered beneath a permanent thatched wooden roof that protected the structure from the extreme equatorial elements. Fred met us at 4 p.m and we set off on an evening game drive. The reserve offered a veritable feast of wildlife: hundreds upon hundreds of wildebeest, Thomson’s and Grant’s gazelles, lappet-faced vultures, tawny eagles, more lions, and then a superb, dramatic sighting of two cheetahs at a fresh kill, having just brought down a wildebeest. It was an extraordinary afternoon of game viewing. Next, we encountered a lone bull elephant with magnificent, sweeping tusks, a silver-backed jackal, a spotted hyena, superb glossy starlings, and vulturine guineafowl— beautiful, striking birds. Topi, Coke’s hartebeest, impala, plains zebras, Maasai giraffes with their distinctive white legs, Maasai red-necked ostriches and wattled starlings completed our extensive checklist of sightings. One of the loveliest aspects of Kenya was that even the common animals and birds possessed variations in colouring or pattern compared to the rest of Southern Africa, making almost everything feel new and interesting. The Maasai staff entertained us after dinner with their traditional, high-jumping warrior dance called the Adumu. The young Maasai warriors, or morans, performed a series of extraordinary vertical leaps, forming a tight circle and taking turns jumping as high into the air as they physically could to demonstrate their competitive strength and endurance. It was an impressive performance; the accompanying rhythmic, guttural chanting sent shivers down my spine; it was quite disconcerting really and gave one the distinct impression that they would make formidable enemies. On the way back to our tent—having left Col and Wendell chatting late into the evening—an old Maasai woman suddenly appeared out of a bush and began aggressively fastening beaded bracelets all the way up my arm until it became difficult to move my arm. She then demanded immediate payment in a piercing, high-pitched voice and was incredibly insistent. I compromised by purchasing two of the bracelets before backing away quickly into the shadows. That night, I had vivid nightmares of Maasai warriors and charging wild animals and witches. All sorts of wild night noises drifting through the canvas added to the atmosphere: low moans, heavy grunts, and high-pitched hyenas cackling. I felt better in the early hours of dawn when we made our way to breakfast around 6 a.m. It was a culinary delight, served out on the open verandah while beautiful yellow weaver birds and purple glossy starlings flew around the tables like living jewels. Later at the Mara River, we watched a massive pod of enormous hippo (kiboko), snorting, grunting and cavorting lazily on the muddy banks. That evening at the lodge we watched a documentary about the great wildebeest migration. These gnus could be remarkably thick-headed. They would attempt to cross the swollen, crocodile-infested Mara River during their migration —even when it would have been significantly easier to simply walk around the bend of the river. The adult wildebeest would swim across frantically, leaving their young behind on the bank. The calves became disoriented when trying to swim after them. The adults would suddenly decide to swim back across the current to fetch the calves, passing them mid-river without even noticing which ultimately led to the calves drowning from pure exhaustion. Meanwhile, resident leopards would take the opportunity to pick off the carcasses, hoisting around seven drowned calves at a time up into a single tree to create a makeshift larder. 

After our arrival at the Outspan Hotel, we climbed stiffly off the tour bus and checked in for our excursion to Treetops—the famous lodge where Princess Elizabeth famously ascended into the structure as a princess in 1952, only to descend the following morning as Queen on hearing of the sudden death of her father, King George VI. Located in the Aberdares National Park, Treetops had originally opened as an overnight treehouse stay for wealthy and intrepid visitors in 1932. It overlooked a large natural watering hole and salt lick, situated about a three-hour drive from the capital, Nairobi. The dark, wooden lodge was constructed directly around several living trees, some of which poked right through the floorboards and ceilings, adding to its rustic charm. It was getting dark by the time we settled in and we didn't want to miss a thing; our treehouse experience was to be a brief, one-night affair due to the expense. After a quick shower we joined Wendell in the downstairs observation lounge overlooking the waterhole below as the evening mist began to blanket the forest. A resonant brass gong summoned us to dinner, which was held in a low, wooden-beamed dining room reminiscent of the galley of an old ship. The dining table was a single, long wooden trestle that seated about twelve guests. It featured a strange but ingenious contraption—much like a long wooden skate used to slide heavy plates, condiments and platters up and down to the guests. Our names were printed on little seating cards and we were amused to notice that we had been listed as the ‘Mosteots’. Fred arrived to collect us the following morning and we set off northwards towards Samburu. The landscape changed remarkably as we drove; first, the cold, misty montane forest gave way to greener, flatter agricultural terrain, which then transitioned into a flat, yellow-grassed valley as we moved onwards towards the northern frontier district. Suddenly, the environment shifted dramatically again becoming intensely hot, dry, and dust-covered, dominated by low thorn scrub. The tall Somali men we passed were dressed in pristine Islamic white robes, (thobes) paired with small, embroidered white caps. I engaged in another round of bartering with Somali hawkers by the roadside, successfully exchanging my cheap pair of plastic yellow sunglasses for two beautiful copper and brass wire bangles. After we passed several members of the nomadic Rendille tribe herding their livestock, Fred cautioned us against taking any photographs of them, remarking that they were to use his exact words, ‘very savage’ if provoked. Fred dropped Wendell off at his hotel in Nairobi, before delivering us back to the Ambassador. What an absolute racket! I had forgotten how noisy central Nairobi could be; there was an endless, swirling mass of people crammed onto the pavements, getting on and off buses, taxis, and matatus. I was convinced half of them were going absolutely nowhere because the sheer density of the crowd didn’t appear to dwindle. It was a physical challenge just trying to cross the street. We were both hot, exhausted, hungry and thoroughly irritable and our hotel room resembled an Indian railway station with all our bags, heavy suitcases and unravelling rucksacks strewn chaotically across the floor. Col spent an hour trying to get hold of a Mrs Roche on the hotel landline, with no luck. Suddenly in the middle of dialling, he sat bolt upright on the bed, started frantically scratching and announced in horror that he had lice. I was mildly hysterical with laughter. To escape the room we decided to try out the nearby 680 Hotel for a cold beer, discovering that it only cost 20 shillings —by far the best price we had found so far. Treating ourselves afterwards to ice cream and a fruit pancake in the lounge. The evening was somewhat spoiled when the barman charged Col triple the standard price for a gin and tonic; it seemed good luck carries a time limit. 

The next morning we flagged down a taxi to take us to Mrs Roche’s, a legendary backpackers’ hostel advertised in the Lonely Planet guide. It was a delightfully ramshackle collection of wood and corrugated iron rooms with travellers camping all over the small grass garden, some sleeping out on the open verandah. Mrs Roche was an elderly German woman who had lived in the house for decades; when her husband died a few years prior, she started hosting overland travellers to supplement her income. The cheap, centrally placed house had quickly become immensely popular with the international backpacking community. She was a lovely old lady with short grey hair cut into a neat, practical bob and was always eager to help. Our small room in the corrugated pre-fabricated section contained two basic iron beds and absolutely nothing else—except perhaps, a hidden multitude of cockroaches and mice. However we were paying next to nothing and were now on the strict budget portion of our holiday. The communal loo was a bit of a nightmare, featuring a half-rotting wooden door that didn't quite close and a plastic bucket filled with murky water sitting on the floor for manual flushing. A fascinating mix of global travellers were camping alongside us—Americans, Germans, Israelis, Dutch and French overlanders. 
I seemed to be getting bigger in the tummy; excellent holiday fare contributing to a rapidly growing baby. Most of the clothes I’d brought with me from Cape Town no longer fitted. After a simple breakfast of cheese spread and fresh tomatoes on bread with sweet melon and mango, Col and I went in search of Mrs Roche to settle up. She kindly agreed that we could leave Col’s heavy camera gear and some of our larger suitcases locked in her main house for safekeeping until we returned from the north. We took a matatu into the city centre which was an experience in itself. The rules of the road appeared to be minimal, operating on the strict adage of ‘every man for himself.’ The town centre was crazy, with hundreds of people milling around, jostling and bustling about on the pavements. We purchased a few basic necessities, including soap and gas canisters for our camping stove before boarding the overnight train to Kisumu. Colin had secured a private cabin equipped with four narrow bunk beds and a wooden table that cleverly converted into a washbasin—though the plumbing was non-functional. 
We were travelling down the floor of the Great Rift Valley and it was beautiful to watch the dramatic scenery rolling past the open window while still light enough to see. The driver was travelling at a tremendous rate of knots and we hoped he hadn't consumed too much pombe, - the local brew. The train guard eventually stuck his head into our compartment to inform us that the train was running late, which meant we’d miss our connecting bus to Kakamega.  Col and I unrolled our heavy bedding and crawled into our respective bunks which were really comfortable while sleeping to the rhythmic, hypnotic rumbling of the train. The town of Kisumu arrived suddenly early morning and a taxi dropped us off in the middle of a bustling African marketplace which erupted in a deafening cacophony of sights and sounds. Tiny wooden stalls selling individual bananas, ripe tomatoes, groundnuts, tin oil lamps, sweets and live chickens were doing a brisk trade. People crowded up to our taxi windows, aggressively hawking biscuits, cheap watches and sticky boiled sweets. We managed to squeeze ourselves into another matatu that was heading in the general direction. When I say squeeze I mean it in the most literal sense—I was so tightly compressed against the passenger door, It left a handle-shaped indentation across my pregnant stomach. The matatu dropped us outside a large wooden gate and we trudged up a long, winding, unpaved path. It was hot, heavy, exhausting work in my pregnant condition, but proved worth it when we finally caught sight of the Rest House—a beautiful stretch of wooden-roofed rooms set high on a ridge overlooking the dense canopy of the Kakamega Forest. Our room was spotlessly clean, if basic, with two iron beds, a couple of wooden stools and an en-suite bathroom; it was perfect for us in its rustic simplicity. The dining canteen across the dirt road was no more than a tiny timber shack selling ugali, beans and fresh chapatis. I was a bit wary at first, knowing that venomous snakes and other mildly poisonous creatures lurked in the dense undergrowth, but the environment was so spectacular and interesting that I quickly relaxed, taking in the symphony of sounds and sights the rainforest had to offer. We located three distinct species of monkey during our walk: the beautiful black-and-white colobus, the energetic red-tailed monkey and the elusive blue monkey. At around 3:30 p.m. the sky darkened and it suddenly began to pour with rain. We were soon to discover that in typical tropical rainforest fashion, this downpour would form the same pattern for the remainder of the week. A small group of us sat huddled on the verandah on rickety wooden chairs, quietly watching sheets of rain bounce off the foliage. As I sat writing up my travel diary, Col was holding our little aluminium camping pan out over the edge of the roof to collect fresh rainwater—which was easier than having to boil all our daily drinking water. Just before it got completely dark Col and I took a brief walk along the perimeter track. The mud was so thick, clay-like, and viscous that it caked the soles of our boots, making us both about four inches taller by the time we returned to the verandah. That morning, I set off on a forest walk with Leonard—our self-appointed guide and the part-time manager of the rest house—alongside two Swedish lads who had arrived late last night. The first thing we encountered was a highly venomous Jameson’s mamba coiled tightly around a low-hanging branch right over the path—it gave me quite a start and I walked past with one eye fixed firmly on it. The forest was alive with vibrant colour and sound; bee-eaters, weavers, hornbills, and brilliant sunbirds darted through the canopy. Leonard stopped to show us a strange, pod-like bush that literally exploded in your hand the moment it was touched. It was called the spotted jewelweed, or the ‘touch-me-not’ plant. When the seed pods reach full maturity, they rely on explosive dehiscence, violently scattering their seeds the moment contact is made by a passing bird or animal brushing against the bush. 
It was becoming increasingly difficult for me to keep up with the pace of the group, attempt to stay upright on the slick, muddy paths and keep a sharp eye out for snakes at the same time. Fortunately, the colobus monkeys came quite close to the clearing of the camp so we could comfortably watch their acrobatic displays while sitting down and sipping our hot chai. Col took some beautiful photographs while setting off towards the remote pump house armed with his tripod, a butterfly net, binoculars, cameras and a small wooden box for collecting specimens. He arrived back three hours later, looking thoroughly exhausted but feeling chipper; he had successfully spotted the magnificent great blue turaco and collected several rare butterflies. It was starting to cloud over ominously again but I was determined to buy some onions and tomatoes from the local village shops a few kilometres down the road to supplement our dinner. We hadn’t gone more than a kilometre from the gate when the heavens suddenly opened. We dashed for shelter, taking refuge under the overhanging tin roof of a mud hut, where a local man sitting on a bicycle informed us encouragingly that the shop wasn’t far away. I’d forgotten the African tendency to distort time and distances to suit the sensibilities of the wazungu, so we walked quite far before rushing to a nearby shelter from another downpour shortly afterwards. This hut housed an old man, four young children and several chickens, all of whom gazed silently at us. Eventually we left the shelter and found the ‘shops’ which were really just a few bamboo lean-tos selling a modest array of fruit and vegetables. One stall had three bananas, a pile of green tomatoes and a few red-skinned onions on a makeshift bamboo table. Beyond that, there was only a single sack of maize meal. I bought five green tomatoes, some onions, and two bananas for the princely sum of 10 shillings (the equivalent of 50c). I shoved the vegetables in the pockets of my wet trousers; we must have looked a strange sight back at camp, wet and bedraggled with weirdly bulging pockets. I set about making a concoction to go with the chapatis I had bought earlier. It tasted okay, washed down with a hot cup of tea. I must have been asleep an hour when a great thudding of footsteps and loud voices awakened me. It was one of the locals from the village and a pal who had managed to secure the room next door to us after some suspicious dealings with Leonard. I was so cross that I banged loudly on the wall to shut him up, then the leak in the bathroom started—drip, drip, drip—driving us both mad. Col got up and wrapped my newly washed trousers around the offending plumbing, which seemed to help a bit. 
I returned from buying a Fanta at the duka, to find a complacent-looking Leonard picking his teeth. He casually announced that there was no water. When he saw my dismayed glance down at my muddy clothes, he added reassuringly but without much conviction, ‘Maybe tonight.’ I didn't have anything clean to wear as the daily rain had prevented any clothes from drying and Col had used my only clean trousers to stop the bathroom leak, so I decided to remain in my wet, muddy clothes until the water returned. A traveller from Holland joined us, introducing himself as Bart. We chatted a bit about our respective journeys and he told me he had travelled across Indonesia and India recently. He mentioned with a chuckle that his guidebook to Kenya mentioned a certain ‘Light-fingered Lennie’ under the heading for the Kakamega Forest Rest House. It looked like rain again; I wondered if I should get up and collect some water for washing or just stand out in the downpour until I was clean. Col returned saying he hadn’t seen much so we headed down to the pump house for some water; it was a real pain not having any at the rest house. The mud had dried thickly on my legs so I gave them a good scrub. We had just enough time to rinse our legs and feet and fill up two water bottles when the heavens opened once more. In the meantime, a group of eight Israelis arrived. They trooped onto the verandah to look for a room. I told them that everything was taken but that Rondo down the road was a lovely place. When they heard how expensive Rondo was, they decided to pitch a tent on the ground underneath the rooms instead. One of the Israeli guys came up to chat (or perhaps just to get out of the rain), having heard we were from Southern Africa—for political reasons at that time we didn’t explicitly say South Africa. Col then abandoned me to resume his butterfly observations, leaving me to answer a number of intricate questions about Zimbabwe where we supposedly lived. Luckily, I had been there twice, so I could venture some sort of response. The blue monkeys were busily swinging from the forest branches and I watched the black-and-white colobus monkeys crashing through the canopy. Other sounds were the cheep-cheep of the sunbirds and the raucous cry of the hornbills—a true cacophony of forest noises. 
I thought we might eat at the canteen today (be still, my beating heart) as our food supplies had almost dried up. Bart had eaten only a plate of very hard beans for his supper last night. He was such a guileless chap; his room, for which he had paid full price, had two beds in it and each night the spare bed was occupied by two locals who crept in after dark and slipped out before dawn. This was an arrangement that Leonard had undoubtedly organised for personal pecuniary reward. Bart just accepted it without any fuss. He had also kindly loaned the use of his bathroom to the last two travellers who had arrived. More people were starting to arrive at the camp so I packed in case we had to get out quickly, holding onto our bathroom access for as long as I could—though I suppose we could always use Bart’s like everyone else. The rain pelted down in buckets at 3 p.m while we sat out on the verandah reading, chatting, writing and gazing out at the sodden forest in companionable silence. The air was filled with the distinct smell of grass—and I don’t mean the stuff cows eat. It was quite companionable with everyone doing their own thing. Supper at the canteen was actually quite tasty that night. Most of us sat at the big wooden tables; Ursula, Jo, Bart, the German chap, a new guy from Brighton, Col and myself. We laughed a lot and shared a round of Tusker beers. They were a well-travelled bunch and the guy from Brighton seemed to have been everywhere Col had, so they had plenty to chat about. At 6:30 tin plates of chapatis, spinach and fried eggs arrived and we all tucked in using our fingers due to a lack of utensils. 

Bart’s room had become like Grand Central Station—full of strange people, luggage, and backpacks. We were due to leave pretty early the next morning, so I hoped our tent was going to be okay. With no working torch I resolved to confine my nocturnal wees to the area right outside the tent flap. The next morning, Bart joined us to wait for a matatu and found himself buried at armpit level of local passengers as we all squished into the back of the vehicle. The entire centre aisle was filled with people bent double. It was mildly amusing until the conductor tried to cram even more passengers inside, shouting, ‘Songa! Songa!’ (Move up!). ‘Songa wapi?’ I cried out in exasperation, bellowing that there was ‘nowhere left to bloody songa,’ much to the amusement of the other passengers. Two more lads squeezed in anyway, clutching their squawking hens upside-down under their arms. Bart thoughtfully righted one of the birds, to its owner’s astonishment. After stopping at a petrol station we were asked to move to yet another matatu, which annoyed us considerably. To make matters worse the driver refused to move until the vehicle was absolutely sardine-full. 
An extremely uncomfortable journey ensued, eventually bringing us to Kitale where we were hurriedly pushed into the worst matatu yet in terms of crowding. From there, we set off towards our final destination, contorted at knee level like travelling goats. We had planned to buy something to eat and drink at Kitale, but were shepherded far too quickly onto the next vehicle. ‘Never mind,’ I whispered into Colin’s armpit, ‘we can have a nice meal at Barnley’s house.’ Famous last words. It started to pour with rain as we were dropped off at the bottom of the track to walk a further kilometre to Barnley’s. What a lovely place it was; a beautiful garden alive with trees, birds and flowers in abundance. We immediately spotted a lovely double-toothed barbet and many colourful sunbirds. Our joyful arrival soon turned to despair when we learned that the lady of the house was out and the place appeared to be full. According to the cook, all the guest houses and permanent tents were taken. He told us to wait for ‘Memsahib Julia’, who would assist us further. Bart was worried because he didn’t have a tent of his own if nothing else was available. We stood in the doorway of the house staring out at the sheet of rain anxiously awaiting Julia’s arrival to learn our fate. We were so hungry by this point that we started tucking into a piece of Bart’s five-day-old dry bread. Julia arrived at about 4 p.m.—a pretty young woman, probably in her late twenties with straight brown hair. As her car pulled up we spotted our old friends the Israelis (all eight of them). They grinned broadly on seeing us again and we managed a weak smile in return. Fortunately, Julia assured us that there were in fact, two tents left, but she explained that she was not cooking for guests at the moment because she was alone; her brother had just tragically died of cerebral malaria and her mother had gone to his funeral. I apologised and expressed how sorry we were to hear that. She simply shrugged her delicate shoulders and gave a small, brave smile. She sold us sausages and bread and gave us some loo paper and soap. The tents were fantastic—luxuriously furnished with camp beds, a table, a little cupboard with chairs, a torch, and fresh water provided. The Israelis had taken over all the communal facilities, though, so there was no hot water left, leading to a bit of a scramble to get to the washbasins and loos. The camp was quite full by now. The lads got the campfire going and despite feeling really tired I managed to cook the sausages and rice with toast. Just as I was wondering if we had enough gas left to boil a pot of tea, one of the servants arrived with a tray of tea. I could have hugged him—what service! We had eggs and many cups of chai for breakfast the next morning before setting off with two of the Israelis for the bottom road, hoping to catch a matatu to visit the Saiwa Swamp National Park. These swamps are located north of Kitale and the only way to navigate them is on foot. We wanted to see sitatungas—the rare, swamp-dwelling antelopes that reside in the area. Having no luck with a bus, we walked three kilometres before catching a very full matatu to the swamp turn-off. At the gate, we paid an entry fee and started to walk towards the lookout area. The forest was a very lush riverine canopy, resulting in a cooler temperature. At the wooden lookout, we climbed the rickety steps into a tall tree and from there caught our very first sighting of a sitatunga—two females and a young male. Two grey crowned cranes were dabbing at the grass, a pretty parrot flew overhead and a long-tailed whydah and hadeda ibis rose into the air as we passed. We stopped for some water and a fifteen-minute rest before setting off along another path that was supposed to lead us to a second lookout. ‘I think this path is a circular route,’ one of the Israelis noted thoughtfully so we continued walking. I was feeling pretty grim by now having walked for six solid hours, eventually feeling too exhausted to carry on any further. Col said he would go on ahead with the others and come back for me if they reached the correct path. I lasted all of five minutes on my own; the ground was full of biting ants and other creepy-crawlies and the path was far too damp to sit down on. I decided to carry on and try to catch up with the group. To do so I had to pass a swarm of wild bees, climb over a giant fallen tree and cross a swampy marsh by balancing on two slippery bamboo poles. I climbed up and slipped down, nearly hysterical—too exhausted to continue but too scared to stay put. My abdomen felt heavy as if I had a lead balloon in there instead of a seven-month-old baby. Eventually I caught up with the others only for them to give me the ‘good news’ that the path simply ended in the thick brush. ‘We glimpsed a De Brazza’s monkey back there,’ Col said pointing vaguely behind him. At that stage, it could have been the last remaining eyeball-munching, Bony-eared assfish* and I wouldn't have cared less. I wearily trudged back as though on autopilot—simply placing one foot in front of the other. Even Col admitted to being completely exhausted. Eventually we reached the end of the main path. Col and Bart decided to see what the final three lookouts had to offer. Unless it involved a tall, iced drink with a pink umbrella, I wasn’t interested and carried on to the gate where I waited with the remaining Israelis. Col and Bart returned half an hour later, having seen absolutely nothing and we began the long walk back to the main road in total silence. There was a small duka selling Fantas. I bought some avocados from a local who tried rather single-mindedly to convert me to his faith. I told him somewhat acerbically that I had been putting my trust in the Lord all day. On the way back, swarms of children came out as if from nowhere, chanting, ‘How are you… how are you… howareyou…!’ until it nearly drove us mad. We started off politely saying, ‘Fine, thank you,’ then admitted we were tired, and finally decided that saying nothing at all was the best way to prevent us from saying anything we would later regret. Back at Barnley's, I bought some more eggs from Julia and had a cold shower because, Israelis. 

Bony-eared assfish (Acanthonus armatus - a real deep sea fish!

The next morning we caught the 7:45 a.m bus to Nakuru. The top of the escarpment was beautiful with luscious, verdant forest on one side and a dramatic valley plunging below. En route we were forced to stop due to major roadworks. A bus tried to pass us coming from the opposite direction, but our bus driver stubbornly refused to let it through which led to an intense argument over who had the right of way. Our bus would move forward a foot followed by the opposing bus moving a foot in the opposite direction like some strange, chess game. A crowd gathered very quickly to enjoy this unexpected bit of entertainment. The ultimate impasse arrived when both buses were nose-to-nose. Eventually our driver was forced to back down; he had to drive backwards down the track, turn around and park behind five other waiting vehicles. ‘That’ll learn you’ I thought smugly. The bus dropped us off fairly close to the Mau View Lodge where we said a fond farewell to Bart. Loaded down with our heavy rucksacks we headed into the lodge. Honestly, ‘The Dump’ or ‘The Fleapit’ would probably have been more appropriate names but for 200 shillings, I suppose we couldn't complain. The rooms featured two beds with yellow nylon sheets, a rock-hard mattress with matching, equally hard pillows. The bath had no plug, the sink had no running water and the loo seat was cracked in half. To top it off the front door wouldn't lock because it was missing most of its handle. We carefully hid most of our valuables in the room and headed into the nearby town centre. It was every bit as horrible as wandering around Nairobi, or perhaps a little worse. Col hugged his camera bag tightly to his chest, his eyes darting everywhere. He tried to organise a trip into the national park with a rather shady character wanting 3,000 shillings for the privilege, so we declined. Instead, we booked our train tickets from Nairobi to Mombasa and found a legitimate car-hire place that agreed to rent us a vehicle to take us on a five-hour trip through the park. 
Col took a tepid bath as quickly as he physically could before the sock that was temporarily serving as a plug let all the water escape. I was feeling thoroughly hypoglycaemic, tired and grumpy so we headed to the Waterbuck Hotel for supper. Walking back to the Fleapit in the pitch dark after dinner was scary; the road was full of deep potholes, there were no working streetlights and the local drivers possessed neither headlights nor common sense so we were in fear of being run over. The promised early morning tea didn't materialise either so I finished packing and sat outside on the concrete steps waiting for our driver Simon to take us into Lake Nakuru National Park. The area was quite beautiful if incredibly dusty; white dust clung to every branch and bush and coated our faces. Later, in Naivasha we set off in a northerly direction walking another three kilometres to Elsamere—the conservation foundation set up by Joy Adamson of Born Free fame. In fact, it was the very last place she lived before she was tragically killed in 1980. It was a slog to get there in the boiling sun, most of it uphill, but it was a magnificent place and definitely worth the visit. The house was shaded by fever trees and the resident caretakers and researchers were a very friendly bunch. We drank refreshing glasses of iced water while watching the black-and-white colobus monkeys hanging in the trees. They were the direct descendants of the original group that Joy had started feeding as part of her wildlife projects. Their antics were quite amusing; like boisterous children, they threw small twigs at me as I watched from beneath a tree. Back in camp it was a mad rush to prepare and cook supper, pack our gear and start filling a flask of tea for the next day before it got completely dark. I’d just managed to boil the water when darkness fell. The cheap torch I’d bought suddenly gave out leaving me scrambling for candles to finish the delicate tea-making process. I was just telling Col to stir in the required three teaspoons of tea leaves when suddenly the last candle went out and we had to carry out the final steps by the light of a few matches. Col was moaning that I should just be able to do without tea for one morning. He really should have known better than that. 

The next morning we woke after no more than two hours’ sleep due to a combination of severe sunburn and intense anxiety about missing our connection. I kept hearing Col’s watch alarm going ‘pip’ every hour on the hour. It was freezing walking to the top of the hill to wait for the bus. As I started boarding, the bus began to roll forward so I found myself teetering precariously on the lower steps trying desperately to keep my balance. Thanks to the heavy rucksack on my back, a prominent baby bump in front and a bag and flask of hot tea in each hand it proved almost impossible. As a result of a sudden lurch, my flask smashed violently against a seat with the swinging motion of the bus; scalding hot tea poured all over my hand. I was being pushed by the sheer force of bodies into the already crowded aisle and without a spare hand, the broken flask splattered and sprayed hot tea all over the bus and passengers dressed in their Sunday best. Col had been swept along by the crowd to the very back but instead of offering help, everyone just looked on impassively. I tried mopping up the hot tea with my hat, accidentally cutting my hand on the jagged glass of the flask, which added blood to the general mayhem. Someone jabbed hard into my distended stomach and the remains of the shattered flask went hurtling along the aisle floor. Col and I were forced to stand swaying with all our heavy gear until we reached Naivasha. After leaving the bus we headed towards the railway station. Our compartment was significantly nicer than the last one. Eight o'clock came and went and we were still completely stationary. We heard there was a slight technical problem so took ourselves off to the dining car for dinner sitting with a lovely group of travellers from Australia. Just after 10 p.m. the train suddenly lurched into movement. The ticket conductor thought this was an excellent time to wake us up again and ask to see our tickets. We slept rather well after this to the gentle, rocking rhythm of the carriage, waking at about 6 a.m. as we passed through Tsavo National Park; a vast expanse of red soil and dry scrub. When we finally arrived in Mombasa the coastal humidity hit us immediately as we walked to another bus stop to wait for a matatu. ‘One matatu, two matatus, three matatus, four...’ I sang hoarsely to myself with a dust-clogged throat. When the vehicle finally arrived, it was the usual nightmare. so full inside that I simply climbed wearily up onto the roof rack via a short iron ladder and clutched onto a large burlap sack of rice for support. The other passengers looked on in consternation but there was no way I was going inside that hotbox. Col hung onto the side of the vehicle by his fingertips as usual—I think he was growing his fingernails for just such occasions. To make matters worse it suddenly started to pour with rain—bloody sod! it almost never rains on the coast at this time of year. We arrived wet, bedraggled with sore fingers outside Madame Veronique Mzimale’s villas. A few discarded items in our room led me to believe the accommodation was generally let by the hour. It featured a mosquito net with so many gaping holes it may as well have been advertising the availability of free human flesh to the insects. After breakfast Col leapt off to change his camera lens before leaving for a boat dive. Later, with still no sign of him I started to fret; he’d been gone all day without any fresh water straight after a deep dive. I worried he might be narced or trampled by a rogue buffalo. My imagination was working overtime so by the time he finally pitched up, I was nearly hysterical with worry. Col, unfortunately was in no mood for my histrionics; he said the matatu had broken down in the middle of nowhere and he had been forced to push it for miles in the midday heat. 
We woke early on our final day with Col in two minds whether to go diving, birdwatching, photographing, or jogging in the forest—and in what combination. In the end, he typically did all four. He set off for the village to hire a bicycle and I guessed that was the last I would see of him until the afternoon. I decided to take advantage of the low tide and investigate the coral rock pools where I met an Italian who shared similar thoughts and he insisted on showing me the contents of the pools—albeit from the safety of terra firma. Neon tetras, sergeant majors and vibrant, striped anemone fish, crabs and many others darted around in colourful projections. 

The Gedi Ruins were remarkable—a crumbling complex of lime and coral stone buildings, many of which had ancient trees growing right out of the stonework. It was a good two hours' walk around the site, so Col purchased a guide booklet for the ruins. A troop of monkeys were screeching up in the tall trees—a regional variant of the Sykes' monkey. They featured long black tails, slate-grey coats across their front and back and a distinct, rusty-coloured patch under their bottoms. The site was full of huge, deep historic wells and long drops; it was a bit eerie looking down into their echoing depths. A local man was selling fresh green coconuts so I bought two. The coconut milk wasn't too bad, but the flesh was far too soft—a bit like eating raw calamari. My feet were starting to swell up badly so Col negotiated a lift from a passing banana seller who took us right outside the hotel via the village, riding in state on his wooden cart. Later we drove out to his friend Richard’s place, which was near the local snake park. In fact, we had to walk through the snake park to reach his house which was fascinating. It was a large, open building with a magnificent thatched roof. Inside, it featured no windows, no doors and absolutely no furniture—the rooms were completely empty. Richard lived entirely outside the house on a massive, open-sided verandah. A large, divan-type sofa was surrounded by butterfly nets, tables, reference books and specialised pinning equipment. His bed was suspended directly from the rafters, accessible by a set of wooden steps that spiralled up to meet it. A massive mosquito net covered the bed and loads of potted plants lined the steps leading to the bed. The entire place had an unreal, mystical quality about it and I was fascinated. Richard showed us some of his live larvae and caterpillar. Col purchased a flame-bordered charaxes pupa from him. Afterwards he sprinted off into the forest canopy to check if any specimens had been caught in his traps. Sykes' monkeys crashed around the tall branches above and then I stumbled across a colossal column of driver ants, almost six feet across. Apparently they’re capable of carrying off a small baby. I instinctively clutched my extended stomach. While rushing to Mombasa, couple of oncoming vehicles tried to drive us off the road leaving Col to perform some fancy manoeuvres to prevent our early demise. At a police checkpoint, with the usual officious, uniformed idiot strolling casually up to our window, Col nearly ran over his boot as we pulled up to hurriedly plead that we were in a desperate rush to catch our train. Thankfully he waved us through—though perhaps the fact that we held his foot to ransom helped. The closer we got to the city centre, the worse the driving became. 
Our names were printed on a small card at the entrance to our sleeper cabin; this time we’re listed as the ‘Mostepots’. Apart from a single violent lurch that woke me in the early hours of the morning, I slept surprisingly well. After checking into our hotel back in Nairobi we unexpectedly met up with our old Israeli pals from the Kakamega Forest for a catch-up regarding our respective activities since the Saiwa Swamp. Finally we embarked on the long drive back home to Cape Town. I was now more than ready to return to civilisation and give birth to my very active son.
