# Chapter 47: EUROPE 2019

On board Turkish Airlines I try to go to the loo as soon as I deem the aircraft is sufficiently high in the ether but it remains engaged for another forty minutes. I’m rather hungry so the query for ‘chicken or pasta’ comes as a welcome relief.
The cute young steward asks what I’d like to drink. I ask him what the local ‘Reki’ is like; he recommends it and pours me a glass with water and ice and warns me it could be,’dangerous!’ As he hands me the glass. I assure him I’ll be careful and take a sip. It’s basically alcoholic aniseed at its most potent and I cough several times. He agrees and saunters off leaving me to enjoy my morsel of salmon, chicken pasta and tiny square of lemon cake. 
A ‘dear little boy’ in the front seat whom I’m in danger of smothering quietly with my square of hard airplane pillow given the opportunity, has not once, since take off, been quiet. Not even a millisecond. He talks, squawks, shouts and screams while his, (presumably deaf) parents look on indulgently.

Trays having been removed, my steward engages in more chit chat and asks if I enjoyed the Reki?  I say it was delicious (but truthfully secreted most of it into an empty water bottle). He asks if I’m I going to be staying in Istanbul, if so where etc, all while niftily packing away used trays onto the cart. I say I’ll be staying a week in Istanbul and he smiles approvingly and leaves me with a wink and a weak cup of tea. I’m hoping to catch up on a movie as the on board wifi isn’t working. I try not to be annoyed by considering the amazing feat of science and technology that it’s even possible to do so while sitting in a ‘tin can’, hurtling 35 thousand feet above earth. I mean, how quickly we become blasé about things. So I settle into my allotted six inches of space to watch something frivolous. 
Sleep does not come quietly into this good night, to misquote Dylan Thomas, so I get up and walk around the aisles, visit the loo for another spray of mens cologne and drink water from the kitchen jug. When I’m really feeling the effects of a lack of sleep, some or other meal time commences and a rubbery omelette arrives with an assortment of unidentifiable congealed accoutrements.

COPENHAGEN Denmark

The plane leaves Turkey for Amsterdam at ‘some world time’ on a small plane.
My room in Copenhagen is large, colourful and airy.

It takes quite a while as Tim and I walk along river banks that are veiled in an Autumn mist. We find ourselves in Christiana or Freetown; a place famous for its homeless, hippy characters since 1971. 
As an International community commune it has its own flag - a red banner with three yellow discs. Some common laws were in place within the town. For example, no private cars are allowed so residents with cars used to park on the streets surrounding the town.
I heard that Christiana operates under a super anarchist democracy and meetings are held to discuss and decide on matters such as the community’s overall finances, revenue and expenditure as well as business and construction.

I peer inside but we don’t go in at this time. Brightly painted walls and graffiti give colour to the grey surroundings and a hodge podge of abandoned items lie around as if awaiting collection. As we saunter along the very wide bridge past Nyhaven, brightly coloured houses run along the banks of the river and some old ships have been converted into restaurants.
A Christmas Market beckons from a nearby street and we wander down to buy some mulled wine - and get to keep the cups. We carry on wandering down past all the brightly lit stalls, twinkling lights and Christmas decor till we come across a quaint, very busy local pub, with the interesting name, ‘Hos Gitte Kik’, which you access down one flight of stairs. It’s warm and very lively inside but also extremely cramped. I almost put my hand in someone else’s pocket we’re so close together. Tim finds a small ledge and we squeeze ourselves against the wall.

I peer out of the large window in the kitchen area. It’s cold and dark this morning despite the late hour. After adding hats and jackets to our clothing we make our way into the light drizzle towards Christiana. Tim and I walk through the muddy streets looking at the strange, eclectic collection of rusty exhibits and colourful buildings which are ramshackle and falling apart mostly. In summer there are parties with theatre, drink and dope (despite the ban) but being winter it’s now quiet. We eventually come to a canal in Nyhaven with rows of old, wooden and colourfully painted houses on either bank. In the canal, old wooden boats are trapped within the low bridges of the lochs until the drawbridge is raised and the water spills through. I like the slightly pastel colours of the buildings; an array of ice cream colours against the grey.

Tim and I dress warmly and set off towards the metro station where we buy two tickets for Louisiana, a small village about forty minutes away from the city. The Louisiana Museum of Modern Art is said to be one of the best. Their Collection comprises a good 4,000 works. It covers the period from 1945 to the present and includes almost every genre – with an emphasis on painting and sculpture.

SWEDEN

Karlskrona; Sweden’s most important naval base, was founded in 1680 as a naval city and the city's unique location on islands in the middle of the archipelago and its strategic importance for the defence of Sweden's coastline increased its significance. It’s also Sweden’s only Baroque city; one with an urban plan from the 17th and 18th centuries, characterised by grand, symmetrical layouts, wide avenues, monumental buildings, and public spaces. These cities integrate art, architecture, and sculpture to create dramatic, emotionally powerful, and visually unified streetscapes and public areas. It certainly looks pretty as I gaze around at the colourful flower baskets attached to lamp posts and the smooth wide roads. The ‘Gingerbread’ house is as pretty as ever and they’ve added three bedrooms for the boys upstairs. We chat to James and Ollie and catch up on our travel stories with Mark and Julie.

The beautiful sculptures in Hoglands park are of the Blekinge Girl, also called ‘When Beauty Came to the Village’  and ‘the Frog’ by Per Hasselberg;  a sculpture of a seated female figure. The Frog depicts a girl of 15 years old, sitting like a frog. In front of the female figure is also depicted a small frog. Hasselberg got the idea for The Frog when his model sat down to rest. The figure is considered naturalistic and as a symbol of the budding femininity of early adolescence.Tim thinks up some fun games to play and amuses his cousins with his ‘Buffalo, Buffalo, Buffalo’ story. Everyone listens to his description but remain puzzled. Saying the word, ‘buffalo’ three or eight times as a sentence is actually a case of lexical ambiguity; a way of giving more than one meaning for a single word. 
In this sentence buffalo has three (and up to eight) different meanings and functions as a verb, proper noun and a noun. It’s hilarious listening to him rattle off sentencers with the same word.

Ireland
I feel quite excited as I have not been back to Ireland since boarding school days. It is raining and the snow has turned slushy at Arlanda. A few hour’s later I look out of the porthole as we descend into Shannon airport and find the weather sunny and oh so green. We land smoothly, (always a plus) and walk easily through passport control. What a friendly reception. The passport official actually jokes with us in a thick Irish accent. The 747 bus takes us to Essex Quay, with only a five-minute walk to the Staycity Aparthotel. The Irish name for Dublin is Baile Átha Cliath (Ball-ya-awha-clia) meaning, (take a breath), ‘The town of the ford of the reed hurdles’ and that’s because reed matting was laid across the River Liffey so people could cross it at low tide in the days of yore.
The Brazen Head is located on Bridge Street so we head down there. This is the area from where the original settlement that was to become Dublin got its name.
The Brazen Head is Ireland's oldest pub. According to their website, there has been a hostelry here since 1198 and the present building was built in 1754 as a coaching inn. However, it continues, ‘The Brazen Head appears in documents as far back as 1653’.
Beside the pub, the Father Mathew Bridge crosses the River Liffey. It’s at this very spot that the original crossing of the river was located. Here, the aforementioned reed matting was positioned on the river bed, which enabled travellers to cross safely at low tide. 
An advertisement from the 1750's reads, “Christopher Quinn of The Brazen Head in Bridge Street has fitted said house with neat accommodations and commodious cellars for said business”.
The Inn walls are made of stone and look old and weathered with lichen and moss growing in the cracks.  The bar style is Georgian. A suit of armour with hopefully no one in it, resides at the entrance. I am fascinated with the history of the area and area and read up as much as I can. Inside, there are brass fittings, old wood, and oil lamps giving a very atmospheric vibe. We find a red bench booth and order a typical pub meal of meat and potatoes with a Guinness.

Afterwards we continue looking around Merchants Quay past the beautiful architecture of Christ Church Cathedral and find a small, cosy coffee place for a cappuccino After a short rest, we head out again towards Dublin Castle and St Werburgh's, which is lit up with Christmas lights. Dublin Castle looks amazing at night as strong lights light up the exterior and silver and gold turrets gleam brightly against the velvet night sky.
We come to the famous Temple Bar Pub, built in 1840 and, as with everything at this time, it’s lit up in Christmas reds and golds. The streets are still paved with historic cobblestones. "Bar" historically referred to a sandbank along the River Liffey and was named after Sir William Temple. 
Asking a local for directions in Ireland is less a lesson in geography and more a masterclass in cultural cartography, where navigation relies entirely on the dual pillars of spiritual salvation and social hydration. 
‘Ah, you want the main road?’ An old gentleman murmurs, leaning in as if sharing a deep biological secret. 
‘Right, go past St. Mary’s Church—not the new one, the one where the roof leaked back in '84—and take a sharp left where the old presbytery used to be. Now, don't go into O’Connor’s—though they pour a beautiful pint of the black stuff—just look directly across the lane at the Methodist hall. Turn right there, follow the curve past St. John the Baptist, and keep going straight until you smell the hops from Murphy’s Tavern. If you haven't lost your faith or your sobriety by then, the destination is just a stone's throw past the next chapel on your left.’ 
A hand tips his cap and he shuffles off.

We enter the National Gallery of Ireland housing a great collection of European and Irish art and spend a happy hour or two there before stopping afterwards at a restaurant on St Andrew's Street (Ireland is ironically full of saints and pubs).After tea, I watch a short cooking programme on the telly just to listen to the gorgeous lilting, Irish brogue.Horses and carts are pulling people along adding to the illusion that we’ve moved back in time.

Killarney(Church of the Sloes) dates back to the year 1604 and it’s three lakes glisten in the morning sun and Irish Red Deer roam freely in the remnants of the Irish oak and yew forests. Queen Victoria, who came here in 1861, described Killarney as a ´fairyland´ for,

Trinity College was founded in 1592, and is the oldest university in Ireland.  For the first 201 years, it was exclusively a Protestant institution. Catholic students were first admitted in 1793, Dad was catholic, so that was useful.
