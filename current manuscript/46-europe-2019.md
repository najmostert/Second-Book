# Chapter 47: Europe 2019

On board Turkish Airlines I was rather hungry so the query for ‘chicken or pasta’ came as a welcome relief.
The cute young steward asked what I’d like to drink. I asked him what the local ‘raki’ was like; he recommended it and poured me a glass with water and ice and warned me it could be ‘dangerous!’. I assured him I’d be careful and took a sip. It was basically alcoholic aniseed at its most potent and I coughed several times. He agreed and sauntered off leaving me to enjoy my morsel of salmon, chicken pasta and tiny square of lemon cake.
A ‘dear little boy’ in the front seat had not once, since take-off, been quiet. Not even a millisecond. He talked, squawked, shouted and screamed while his parents looked on indulgently.

Trays were removed and my steward engaged in more chit-chat and asked if I enjoyed the raki. I said it was delicious (but truthfully secreted most of it into an empty water bottle). He asked if I was going to be staying in Istanbul, if so where etc, all while niftily packing away used trays onto the cart. I said I’d be staying a week in Istanbul and he smiled approvingly and left me with a wink and a weak cup of tea. I was hoping to catch up on a movie as the on-board wifi wasn’t working. I tried not to be annoyed by this considering the amazing feat of science and technology that it’s even possible to do so while sitting in a ‘tin can’, hurtling 35 thousand feet above earth. I mean, how quickly we become blasé about things. So I settled into my allotted space to watch something frivolous.
Sleep does not come quietly into this good night, to misquote Dylan Thomas, so I got up and walked around the aisles, visited the loo for another spray of men’s cologne and drank water from the kitchen jug. When I was really feeling the effects of a lack of sleep, some or other meal time commenced and a rubbery omelette arrived with an assortment of unidentifiable congealed accoutrements.

## Copenhagen Denmark

The plane left Turkey for Amsterdam at ‘some world time’ on a small plane.
My room in Copenhagen was large, colourful and airy.

It took quite a while as Tim and I walked along river banks that were veiled in an autumn mist. We found ourselves in Christiana or Freetown; a place famous for its homeless, hippy characters since 1971.
As an international community commune it has its own flag — a red banner with three yellow discs. Some common laws were in place within the town. For example, no private cars are allowed so residents with cars used to park on the streets surrounding the town.
I heard that Christiana operates under a super anarchist democracy and meetings are held to discuss and decide on matters such as the community’s overall finances, revenue and expenditure as well as business and construction.

I peered inside but we didn’t go in at this time. Brightly painted walls and graffiti give colour to the grey surroundings and a hodge-podge of abandoned items lie around as if awaiting collection. As we sauntered along the very wide bridge past Nyhaven, brightly coloured houses run along the banks of the river and some old ships have been converted into restaurants.
A Christmas market beckoned from a nearby street and we wandered down to buy some mulled wine — and got to keep the cups. We carried on wandering down past all the brightly lit stalls, twinkling lights and Christmas décor till we came across a quaint, very busy local pub, with the interesting name, ‘Hos Gitte Kik’, which you access down one flight of stairs. It was warm and very lively inside but also extremely cramped. I almost put my hand in someone else’s pocket we were so close together. Tim found a small ledge and we squeezed ourselves against the wall.

I peered out of the large window in the kitchen area. It was cold and dark that morning despite the late hour. After adding hats and jackets to our clothing we made our way into the light drizzle towards Christiana. Tim and I walked through the muddy streets looking at the strange, eclectic collection of rusty exhibits and colourful buildings which are ramshackle and falling apart mostly. In summer there are parties with theatre, drink and dope (despite the ban) but being winter it was then quiet. We eventually came to a canal in Nyhaven with rows of old, wooden and colourfully painted houses on either bank. In the canal, old wooden boats are trapped within the low bridges of the locks until the drawbridge is raised and the water spills through. I liked the slightly pastel colours of the buildings; an array of ice-cream colours against the grey.

Tim and I dressed warmly and set off towards the metro station where we bought two tickets for Louisiana, a small village about forty minutes away from the city. The Louisiana Museum of Modern Art is said to be one of the best. Their collection comprises a good 4,000 works. It covers the period from 1945 to the present and includes almost every genre — with an emphasis on painting and sculpture.

## Sweden

I tip-toed around, dressed quietly and sat in the kitchen and chatted to a girl from Indonesia. This is the nice thing about hostels; you have great opportunities to meet different people from all over the world and hopefully engage in some interesting chat. Tim and I were ready to check out by ten and bought two tickets to Karlskrona. I asked a few people which platform we needed but they didn’t know either. Eventually a tall bearded man with a dog assisted us (the man mostly). Halfway after passport checks in Malmö, we were asked to exit the train due to technical difficulties and board another train. So grabbing the luggage, we rushed over the tracks to the other train and found some downgraded seats in the bicycle section.

Karlskrona, Sweden’s most important naval base, was founded in 1680 as a naval city and the city’s unique location on islands in the middle of the archipelago and its strategic importance for the defence of Sweden’s coastline increased its significance. It’s also Sweden’s only Baroque city; one with an urban plan from the 17th and 18th centuries, characterised by grand, symmetrical layouts, wide avenues, monumental buildings, and public spaces. These cities integrate art, architecture, and sculpture to create dramatic, emotionally powerful, and visually unified streetscapes and public areas. It certainly looked pretty as I gazed around at the colourful flower baskets attached to lamp posts and the smooth wide roads. The ‘Gingerbread’ house is as pretty as ever and they’ve added three bedrooms for the boys upstairs. We chatted to James and Ollie and caught up on our travel stories with Mark and Julie.
Tim was still sleeping when I received a phone call from the South African police which startled me. It transpired that Nelly’s phone was stolen so they needed a statement from her. She’d given them my number. The beautiful sculptures in Hoglands Park are of the Blekinge Girl, also called ‘When Beauty Came to the Village’, and ‘the Frog’ by Per Hasselberg; a sculpture of a seated female figure. The Frog depicts a girl of 15 years old, sitting like a frog. In front of the female figure is also depicted a small frog. Hasselberg got the idea for The Frog when his model sat down to rest. The figure is considered naturalistic and as a symbol of the budding femininity of early adolescence. Tim thought up some fun games to play and amused his cousins with his ‘Buffalo, Buffalo, Buffalo’ story. Everyone listened to his description but remained puzzled. Saying the word ‘buffalo’ three or eight times as a sentence is actually a case of lexical ambiguity; a way of giving more than one meaning for a single word.
In this sentence buffalo has three (and up to eight) different meanings and functions as a verb, proper noun and a noun. It was hilarious listening to him rattle off sentences with the same word.

It was minus 10°C that morning and although it was warm inside, a step outdoors required several layers.
Tim borrowed Mark’s car and we drove into Trossö. I was amused to see a sign below a fire extinguisher that said ‘Skum’. I guess it’s as good a description as any. Later on we drove further across town and passed a sign for ‘The Kruthus, pub and Kāk’ which amused the child in me as ‘kak’ means excrement in South Africa (and possibly a few other languages as well).

It snowed last night; a thin layer all over the garden and a few slippery, icy bits too which I was trying to avoid. The Swedes are prepared for winter with snow tyres, crampons for boots etc., unlike at home where winter takes us by surprise every year as though we’re the only ones who haven’t got the memo. Jools was baking that morning and making pepparkakor rusks which is a type of flat cinnamon-based biscuit. The warm, spicy smells of baking permeated the kitchen.

Today we took the train to Ronneby Airport after a fond farewell to Mark, Julie and Tim’s cousins before boarding a small plane for Stockholm.
It was a short flight followed by a bus into the centre of Stockholm.
Tim dragged my big red suitcase as well as his to his farmor’s (father’s mother) place.
It was still snowing lightly and sounds of vehicles driving by were muffled with the snow.

It was already dark by 4 p.m. so we walked around town looking into store windows that were resplendent with Christmas décor. Huge reindeer made from wire and lights added to the festive air as did the many lit candles that had been carefully placed outside department stores. Some stores had plates of pepparkakor biscuits left on the doorstep for anyone to take which I thought was a lovely festive idea and immediately thought both the biscuits and plate would disappear in seconds should the idea ever take off at home.

I woke early and looked from the lounge window at the sunrise which glowed in pink and yellow streaks across the rooftops. A man was rafting down the swirling waters of Norrström river in a bright red canoe. We watched him for a while as he expertly tipped his canoe this way and that through the grey water before we carried on to the National Museum to look at the exhibits, marble statues, and oil paintings by esteemed Swedish artists of yore. We visited Gamla Stan by bus that day — the old part of the city where the streets are cobbled and the shop windows have seats, small panes, stone archways, and beautifully decorated window fronts. A julmarknad (Christmas market) was taking place down a side street. It was quite festive there and we spent a while trying a few snacks and looking at all the various things on offer before taking the bus back to Upplandsgatan and watching winter sports on the telly with Anne-Sofie. She explained the rules of curling to me. That morning Annie picked us up and took us to Farstalandet, which means ‘the mainland’. They have an outdoor museum there with some interesting sculptures in a large garden area. It was a cloudy morning but didn’t rain. Afterwards, we stopped and had lunch next to a roaring fire. The balcony had a few inches of thick snow covering it that morning. Tim and I ventured out fully covered and headed for Vasastan for an early lunch and coffee. It is always a treat for us to experience snow, especially around December as it’s so pretty and festive.

## Ireland

I felt quite excited as I had not been back to Ireland since boarding school days. It was raining and the snow had turned slushy at Arlanda. A few hours later I looked out of the porthole as we descended into Shannon airport and found the weather sunny and oh so green. We landed smoothly (always a plus) and walked easily through passport control. What a friendly reception. The passport official actually joked with us in a thick Irish accent. The 747 bus took us to Essex Quay, with only a five-minute walk to the Staycity Aparthotel. The Irish name for Dublin is Baile Átha Cliath (Ball-ya-awha-clia) meaning, (take a breath), ‘The town of the ford of the reed hurdles’ and that’s because reed matting was laid across the River Liffey so people could cross it at low tide in the days of yore.
The Brazen Head is located on Bridge Street so we headed down there. This is the area from where the original settlement that was to become Dublin got its name.
The Brazen Head is Ireland’s oldest pub. According to their website, there has been a hostelry here since 1198 and the present building was built in 1754 as a coaching inn. However, it continues, ‘The Brazen Head appears in documents as far back as 1653’.
Beside the pub, the Father Mathew Bridge crosses the River Liffey. It’s at this very spot that the original crossing of the river was located. Here, the aforementioned reed matting was positioned on the river bed, which enabled travellers to cross safely at low tide.
An advertisement from the 1750s reads, “Christopher Quinn of The Brazen Head in Bridge Street has fitted said house with neat accommodations and commodious cellars for said business”.
The inn walls are made of stone and look old and weathered with lichen and moss growing in the cracks. The bar style is Georgian. A suit of armour with hopefully no one in it resides at the entrance. I was fascinated with the history of the area and read up as much as I could. Inside, there are brass fittings, old wood, and oil lamps giving a very atmospheric vibe. We found a red bench booth and ordered a typical pub meal of meat and potatoes with a Guinness.
‘Sláinte mother,’ said Tim with a clink against my glass,
‘What’s it like being back in Ireland?’
‘It’s a bit weird,’ I replied looking around as though to find the weirdness.
‘When I lived here, there was a lot more English being spoken and on road signs etc., whereas I see a lot more Gaeilge now.’
Afterwards we continued looking around Merchants Quay past the beautiful architecture of Christ Church Cathedral and found a small, cosy coffee place for a cappuccino. After a short rest, we headed out again towards Dublin Castle and St Werburgh’s, which was lit up with Christmas lights. Dublin Castle looks amazing at night as strong lights light up the exterior and silver and gold turrets gleam brightly against the velvet night sky.
We came to the famous Temple Bar Pub, built in 1840 and, as with everything at this time, it was lit up in Christmas reds and golds. The streets are still paved with historic cobblestones. ‘Bar’ historically referred to a sandbank along the River Liffey and was named after Sir William Temple.
Asking a local for directions in Ireland is less a lesson in geography and more a masterclass in cultural cartography, where navigation relies entirely on the dual pillars of spiritual salvation and social hydration.
‘Ah, you want the main road?’ An old gentleman murmured, leaning in as if sharing a deep biological secret.
‘Right, go past St. Mary’s Church — not the new one, the one where the roof leaked back in ’84 — and take a sharp left where the old presbytery used to be. If you hit the Holy Virgin statue, you’ve gone too far, so backtrack past the cemetery until you spot O’Connor’s Pub. Now, don’t go into O’Connor’s — though they pour a beautiful pint of the black stuff — just look directly across the lane at the Methodist hall. Turn right there, follow the curve past St. John the Baptist, and keep going straight until you smell the hops from Murphy’s Tavern. If you haven’t lost your faith or your sobriety by then, the destination is just a stone’s throw past the next chapel on your left.’
A hand tipped his cap and he shuffled off.
‘Did you get that?’ I asked Tim hopefully. He shook his head and laughed.
That afternoon we walked past St Patrick’s Cathedral; a huge brown-stone building that was completely covered in lights, before taking a walk through St Stephen’s Green park (I mean, aren’t all parks in Ireland green). This one has wide pathways, fountains, lush viridian grass, statues, and ponds. We entered the National Gallery of Ireland housing a great collection of European and Irish art and spent a happy hour or two there before stopping afterwards at a restaurant on St Andrew’s Street (Ireland is ironically full of saints and pubs). After tea, I watched a short cooking programme on the telly just to listen to the gorgeous lilting Irish brogue. Horses and carts were pulling people along adding to the illusion that we’d moved back in time.
It was embarrassing when I found in the Avis building that I couldn’t drive the car out of the yard, being used to my automatic for many years now, so Tim had to take over. We headed down to County Limerick and stopped at the Barack Obama Plaza in Moneygall for the loo and some food. I discovered that Barack Obama visited his ancestral home in May 2011. Everywhere you look there’s a reference to his visit. Apparently, his great-great-great-grandfather came from here. Well, there are none so Irish as the Americans I always say (actually I’ve only said it twice). We arrived in Killarney quite late and checked into a lovely old hotel called The Old Weir Lodge. There’s a welcoming reception area with an open log fire, exposed dark wooden beams and elegant country-style furnishing.
Killarney (Church of the Sloes) dates back to the year 1604 and its three lakes glisten in the morning sun and Irish Red Deer roam freely in the remnants of the Irish oak and yew forests. Queen Victoria, who came here in 1861, described Killarney as a ‘fairyland’ for,
‘Where else would you find such magic!’ Where indeed.
The lakes were glowing in the mist, snow-capped mountains and lichen-covered trees stand sentry around its edges. Long-horned goats were drinking from a nearby stream near the River Galway. A quaint yellow road sign states that ‘leprechauns cross’ here. I was captivated; you can feel the spirits of ancient times in the old stone ruins and crumbling gravestones.
At Derrynane beach on the Kenmare River we took a brisk walk across the white sand. The tiny Abbey Island is reached during low tide but it was high tide at the moment unfortunately. The ruins of Ahamore Abbey date from the tenth century. With so much ancient history in Ireland, it’s sometimes hard to take it all in.
Our next stop was Ross Castle with its many rambling stone turrets. Ross Castle perches in an inlet of Lough Leane. I later read that O’Donoghue still slumbers under the waters of the lake. They say that every seven years, on the first morning of May, he rises on his magnificent white horse. If you manage to catch a glimpse of him you will enjoy good fortune for the rest of your life. I desperately looked out for Mór, then remembered it was December and consoled myself with the knowledge that good fortune often shines upon me regardless of having seen a white nag or not.
After a walk around the castle, we returned to the city centre of Killarney where a Christmas parade was taking place. It was dark now as we waited with the rest of the crowd, the sound of excited children and their parents waiting in anticipation for the floats to pass. When they came into view, sleighs were pulled by Father Christmas himself, giant gingerbread men, Batman and Robin, giant Minions, colourful lights, and snowmen. It started to drizzle, but that did little to dampen the atmosphere. We watched for over an hour, light snow decorating our heads, then went in search of supper at ‘James Murphy’s’ bar.
Today we were heading slowly back to Dublin via the Rock of Cashel, also known as St Patrick’s Rock. Supper at the Brazen was quite crowded, but we found a spot sharing a large wooden table with a group of young women. It was great craic!
I have always wanted to return to the iconic Bewley’s — a tea shop I visited with various grandparents while in Ireland during my childhood. It was founded in 1840, so it’s been around a while. We found it easily enough and it is still the lovely old institution I remember — only it seemed bigger. There are a few levels to the place, fire-lit fireplaces warm the rooms and the smell of coffee and freshly baked scones filled my nostrils. We were led to a table on the upper level and ordered tea and pastries which were expensive but delicious.
I couldn’t believe that only a few short weeks ago we were walking in snow. That day Blackrock was warmed by a beaming sun over a lovely park and as we strolled through it, came across an abandoned shopping trolley in the middle of a pond. I was sure there’s an interesting story attached to that. I imagined some bored teens fuelled by an overconsumption of sugary caffeinated beverages, ‘borrowing’ the trolley from a nearby supermarket and launching it complete with empty crisp packets and a soggy sock into the tranquil waters of the pond, where the bizarre sight becomes a local curiosity.

A rainy, cold day greeted me that morning, so we dressed accordingly and set off via a yellow double-decker bus to the other side of Dublin and followed this with a visit to Trinity College, Dublin, where Dad studied medicine. Trinity College was founded in 1592, and is the oldest university in Ireland. For the first 201 years, it was exclusively a Protestant institution. Catholic students were first admitted in 1793; Dad was Catholic, so that was useful.
After tea and toast, we walked to Stillorgan village and had a look around, and later, the Dún Laoghaire shopping village. We popped into a café for coffee and looked around St Joseph’s Church as they celebrated 150 years. It is beautiful inside. There are colourful arches, stained-glass windows, and vases of flowers.
My cousin John took us to Powerscourt Golf Club in Enniskerry. The club has a lake and forest as well as manicured gardens, fountains, statues, paths for hiking, and a Japanese garden. We climbed up the ‘Pepperpot Tower’ and looked around the verdant surrounds. Afterwards, he drove us to Coliemore Harbour and we stopped at Dillon’s Park to look at an ancient and ‘holy’ well. These were sacred pagan sites and once a part of early Celtic Christian rituals. John drove into the village for a stroll around, looking at shops and Dalkey Castle, which is a rare medieval townhouse-castle, given that it is the only remaining one of seven that is open to the public. These fortified castles were built in 1390 in Dalkey by medieval merchants looking to protect their goods before transportation to Dublin city centre. In Viking times, it was named Dalkeyja, meaning ‘Thorn Island’, as its shape resembles a thorn.
Tim and I met up with Matt, an old friend of my brother and we popped into the National Museum of Ireland to have a look around. They have some really interesting exhibits, including some mummified men — Old Croghan Man was found in a peat bog which preserved the body well. He was tall at over 6 feet, 3 inches. I enjoyed the museum while chatting intermittently with Matt.

Cousin John and Eleanor took the bus into town with us. The streets were sparkling with lights and Christmas décor. We entered McDaid’s Pub, established in 1799. This is a Christmas tradition for my cousins, apparently. It was already bustling with patrons.
Later Matt fetched a black and white watercolour portrait that my brother painted for him. It was strange looking at something John did so long ago. Matt said the likeness was good. He told us many stories about my brother and his great ‘forgery’ abilities. He once drew a pound note to buy cigarettes with. I laughed a bit nervously and said John had huge artistic talent and emphasised that he painted scenery, rather than bank notes in his latter years.
I told them of the time John rode a 50cc motorbike all the way from the previously named Transvaal to visit me in the Cape, some 1,450 km. The motorbike wasn’t powerful enough to be allowed on the national roads and highways (minimum was a 100cc). Undaunted, John painted a ‘1’ before the 50 on the side of his bike, thereby increasing the bike’s ‘power’ and ‘legality’ — even if only visually. During this same period, he’d found some money-making scheme by selling life-size ‘pink panthers’ to those who were weird enough to want one. He asked me one day if I would ride with him into town and hold onto one of the huge panthers where a parent had promised to buy one for their six-year-old. We made an interesting sight putt-putting uphill on a six-lane highway, John in front, myself riding pillion with a life-size pink panther in between. Another painting was brought out, this time in colour, of a mountain scene and birds. This one shows a maturity in his work and I liked it. Both are framed.

It was Christmas Day and after tea and soda bread, we left for Eleanor’s sister’s place for Christmas dinner.
It was a relaxed and hearty evening with lots of good Christmas fare, Christmas crackers, and lots of wine. We played games with great hilarity then left around 11 p.m. We said our final goodbyes as we were leaving tomorrow.

## England

After a bit of a lie-in we caught a bus to the airport. I was so grateful for my aunt Doreen to have afforded us the opportunity to stay in her flat thereby saving us a lot in accommodation. I was also sorry we didn’t have time to see her this visit.
The plane to the UK was a small plane. It was so small that Tim had to bend his head when he stood up. After disembarking, we took a train into Westminster and walked to our accommodation; the Sanctuary House Hotel. Our room was a cosy twin-bedded room with very busy wallpaper on the second floor. We chilled for a while then set out in search of supper. I photographed the ubiquitous red phone boxes that are still around although fewer in number and walked past Westminster Abbey, which was lit up with a ghostly white glow. It was strange being back in London again; especially without being able to see my dearest gran. We came across a few landmarks, such as the Abbey, Victoria Tower Gardens, and Buckingham Palace’s Wellington Arch, then found a small restaurant for supper. It was already quite dark by 5 p.m. Afterwards, we took a stroll through Hyde Park. The Winter Wonderland Christmas Market was in full swing.
Tate Modern was built in 1900 and is one of the largest museums of modern and contemporary art in the world. We were pleasantly surprised to see there’s no admission charge and left our rucksacks in a locker before having a look around. In one room, we found a giant heap of life-like oranges, and I wasn’t sure if the intention was to make one thirsty but that’s what I felt after gazing at them.
After collecting our rucksacks we took a stroll down the Thames, stopping in Jubilee Square to watch a man in a straitjacket try to escape it in one minute. I wasn’t sure he was going to make it but after a final shrug, he was out and bowed triumphantly to scattered applause. That morning we passed a man strolling along with a gull on his head. He seemed totally unperturbed. The gull merely sat on his cranium, looking around with interest. A very British moment.
We were staying with my niece Jesse and her husband Stephen. That morning I made us some tea before setting off to explore Reading. Reading Abbey is a ruin from the time of King Henry I in 1121. There’s not much of it left, but enough to appreciate the history. The chapter house is an impressive space and is the most complete room within the Abbey Ruins. It was a multi-purpose room that served as the meeting place for the community of monks at the Abbey.

## Edinburgh

Tim and I had seen as much of what there is to see in Reading so we were heading for Scotland for the last few days of our holiday. It was dark when we arrived in Edinburgh and found the small B&B we had booked, conveniently situated just twenty minutes away from the airport.
Edinburgh Castle stands starkly against a grey sky up on the hill and I was seeing it for the first time since we lived here while I was at school. Dad was completing his fellowship at the Royal College of Surgeons at that time and we rented an apartment where you could see the castle from the kitchen window. We walked now what is known as ‘The Royal Mile’, past interesting-looking shops and festive fare. Hogmanay is celebrated in Scotland on New Year’s Eve and we were both keen to join in their celebrations. We walked through Edinburgh Castle, past turrets and looked over the high walls to the city of Edinburgh below which was bathed in sunlight before walking back along the river to the hotel. Tim was going out to celebrate Hogmanay. My throat felt a bit scratchy so I took a tablet and had an early night.
I woke up in the New Year at 2 a.m. with laryngitis and a fever. I swallowed some antibiotics, which luckily I brought with me for just such a purpose and fell into a fever-induced sleep. Tim was not back when I woke again after a few hours of fitful sleep. For some reason, I decided to record myself on my phone, hoarse and breathing with difficulty, saying I had taken an antibiotic much the same way you’d confess to having swallowed a bottle of sleeping tablets! I blamed the fever for this dramatic response and went back to sleep until after 5 p.m., when Tim eventually returned. He was surprised to see me so ill after he left me in fairly good health the night before. He went out again for supper while I just slept and recovered. I hoped this was not an indication of the New Year to come. (Well we all know how that turned out!)
Whilst visiting the Scottish National Gallery of Modern Art, a neon legend reads, ‘Everything is going to be alright’. Considering what was to come for the entire world in only a few months, this message was hopeful, to say the least.
It was dark outside when we finished and everything was lit up. Colourful lights hung everywhere; around trees, across archways and on buildings. Across the way, we could see Edinburgh Castle glowing with a blue sheen on the mountaintop.
I wanted to visit Eglington Crescent that day, where we lived for a year when I was a child. We found it with the help of Google Maps. I remembered that if you look out of the window, you can see the castle and found the approximate entrance opposite the flat where we would walk into the park. I spotted a restaurant across the road called ‘Shebeen’ that says it is a steakhouse and serves braai meat — it’s South African owned. We took the train back down to London. Passing stations with names like ‘Hett’ and ‘Spittal’. From the London train station, we took another train to Heathrow. I hugged Tim goodbye as he was heading on to Norway to meet his father for a trip to see orcas, while I had to catch Ethiopian Airways back to Cape Town. We stopped briefly in Bole, Ethiopia. I couldn’t believe my fantastic holiday was over. What a blast! Five countries: Denmark, Sweden, Ireland, Scotland, and the UK. A month later, I recalled how sick I was over New Year and figured I probably suffered from Covid while in Edinburgh.
