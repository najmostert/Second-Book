# Chapter 31: Christmas in Kenya

We returned to Kenya after many years to spend Christmas with the family. We made a split-second decision to send our luggage on to Nairobi—a move that turned out to be a mistake, as we only had an hour between flights. The Cape Town-to-Johannesburg leg was uneventful, but as we sat in the terminal, I glanced at the flight information and nearly spilled my coffee: the flight to Kenya had been cancelled. Before I could tell Col, the next flight was cancelled too. The information kiosk was vague and unhelpful, and we were sent from pillar to post. Eventually, Col joined the main airline queue, while I decided to try the First Class line. It was a good move; I managed to catch an official who actually helped, and he sorted out the incompetence of the ticket office. We were given two tickets on the 2:30 p.m. flight—which wasn't even on the board yet, given it was only 9 a.m. We were anxious to get to Nairobi in time to catch our next plane, but it wasn't to be. Shortly after takeoff, an air hostess approached and informed us that one of our suitcases was missing. I was convinced it was mine, but I told Col that if it was his, he would at least have four changes of clothing. He looked at me, puzzled, and asked how I had worked that out. I explained that his trousers— which had removable zipped legs—could be worn four ways: as full trousers, with one leg removed, with the other removed, and finally as shorts. We landed at 4:20 p.m. and rushed to baggage claim. Sure enough, it was my case that was missing. I told Col he could keep his zipped trousers; I was the one who would be wearing my jeans and top four ways, mostly back to front. The damp heat hit us like a wet sock as we wandered around the small Nairobi airport looking for answers. Col insisted that the authorities sign a lost luggage report, as they told us to wait until we reached Mombasa. Having missed our connection, we ended up at a nearby hotel for the night. I couldn’t believe it when the alarm startled me out of my sleep for the second time in two days. We trooped downstairs for a quick breakfast before heading back to the airport. Our flight was delayed, but by 10 a.m. we were on board for the short hop to Mombasa. They had barely finished flinging peanuts at us before we touched down. Col drove us, using Sonja’s directions, towards our accommodation. It took three hours, and by the time we reached ‘Manfridays Midas Cove’, it was already 3 p.m. A smiling Kenyan with lovely white teeth showed us to our room—a beautiful open-plan stone rondavel with a thatched roof, stone seating with cushions, and a large bed draped in mosquito netting. Sonja, one of the owners, greeted us, and we soon left for the house where Col’s family were staying for Christmas Eve. It was another hour away, so we were exhausted when we finally arrived at a big house with a huge verandah. Col’s mum, sister, brother-in-law, and the children were all there. We hugged, had a drink, and caught up. Col and I left early after Christmas. It took an hour to get back to Sonja’s place. Papu, the other owner, met us; he was a short, stocky man with a Seychellois-Indian mix and a welcoming American accent. We had a cold shower and joined Sonja and Papu at the bar for wine. Later, we met the other guests: a Swiss man, his Kenyan wife, and their sulky fifteen-year-old daughter. The rooms were designed using natural materials—shell curtains, raffia, and wood—decorated with treasures from the sea. We visited the Arabuko Sekoke Forest, where Col spoke to the local official before we drove on to the Gede ruins. They had changed since we were last there nineteen years ago; a lookout ledge had been built on a huge baobab. The ruins were fascinating, showing a distinct Muslim influence in their design. That evening, we joined Sonja, Papu, and the Swiss couple at the bar. Halfway through our chat, their daughter arrived and started talking as though a button had been switched on. She opined about everything, arguing with everyone as if she knew better. It was rather precocious. Papu was on top form, regaling us with stories from his past; he brought us all a round of Amarula and teased the opinionated youngster, too. 

Sun, Ruins, and Rare Finds 

The full moon lightened the dawn, and the sea gleamed like liquid mercury. Col left early for the Sekoke Forest without breakfast. I was still tired, but I decided to go for a walk. It was mildly cooler on the beach, and I wore my sandals to prevent cutting my feet on the exposed, jagged coral. I waded in until the water was up to my waist, then turned back. In our room, I glanced in the mirror and a beetroot with red eyes stared back—I was badly burned on my back where the sun cream hadn't reached. I was annoyed, knowing full well my complexion couldn't take the sun. The heavy, oppressive clouds that had built up suddenly opened, and the rain fell in torrents. Col and I walked the perimeter of Gede. The ruins are the remnants of a mysterious medieval Swahili civilisation, once a centre for trade along the East African coast. Set amongst overgrown indigenous woodland—filled with dancing butterflies, colourful birds, and Sykes’ monkeys—the narrow paths meander between excavated buildings. While walking, we came across the back end of an elephant shrew, and then, something very special. Thinking at first they were two large hares, we realised they were Ader's duikers—very rare creatures only found here and in Tanzania, once thought to be extinct in the Arabuko Sekoke Forest. Col returned for a lunch of calamari and garlic sauce, and then we left for Vipingo to celebrate New Year’s Eve with the family. It was a festive evening, and we returned just after midnight, bidding a fond farewell to the family who were leaving soon after. 

The Tsavo Trek and the Stolen Car 

Zero brought us tea around 4 a.m. It was a long drive over a tortuous, sandy, rutted road to our destination, Tsavo National Park. Once we turned off past Malindi, it took three hours to reach the gates. We nearly overturned the car several times as the road was full of dongas (deep ditches), and we had to swerve repeatedly to avoid killing a couple of guinea fowl. The park is quite big, and the terrain changes from sandy, open shrub to grassy bushland. We saw elephants, giraffes, zebras, waterbuck, and two pale-skinned lions with swollen bellies resting on an anthill. These two were related to the infamous ‘Man-Eaters of Tsavo’ that hunted humans while the railway was being built in the early 1900s, so we gave them a wide berth. We drove back at dusk and came across a caracal—slinking across the road in a pale crouch, just like a domestic kitty. It disappeared into the undergrowth, then peered through a bush at us before emerging again. Just as we tried to take a photo, the only other vehicle we had seen since leaving came rumbling along, startling the cat, which vanished for good. Zero woke us again with tea. We left late for Watamu and ‘Ocean Sports’, mostly because we were flying over speed bumps the entire way. We told the gate guards that Col was diving, so they let us park inside. I decided to go for a walk and needed to drop off my heavy backpack in the car. I walked straight to where I thought we had parked, but the car wasn't there. I panicked and looked wildly around. 
We tell the guards at the gate that Col is diving, so they let us park inside, past the booms. I decide to go for a walk, so need to drop off my heavy backpack in the car. I walk straight to where I thought we parked. And naturally, the car isn't here, so I panic and look wildly around for it. I march up to one of the guards and ask them where the car is.
‘ Where did you park it?’ asks the first guard.
‘ I parked it there, in front of you!’  I reply shrilly.

‘ I don't bloody know! It's a hired car. You need to help me find it. It's a dark colour, almost black.’ I pause for a few seconds,’ I think it's called a Scorpio.’ 
I glance down towards the car park and see just such a car pulling out and  start running after it.
‘Hi, I'm looking for a car that looks just like this!’ 
I announce breathlessly to the startled face of the Indian driver, who has recklessly wound down his window.
‘Well, this is my car,’ he says firmly. ‘Do you want to buy one?’
‘No, no, our hired car is stolen – it looks like this one,’ 
I break off and peer wildly into the back seat, trying to see if he has hidden our stuff. The driver looks annoyed and starts to reverse again. I cling on with my fingers.
 ‘This looks just like our car, where did you find it? I ask suspiciously, my fingers clutching at the window pane as I peer in. 
There’s a small crowd gathering now and they all gape at the unusual sight of a wild-haired woman clinging to a moving vehicle.
‘Leave us alone, this is my husband’s car!’ states the portly woman in a blue sari from the passenger seat. 
She is glaring at me in anger. The car picks up speed and my clawing, tenuous grasp slides off the window, leaving greasy finger marks. The man looks fearful as I lunge again at the moving car and he picks up speed. I stand helplessly, panting in fury, and turn to one of the guards who has joined the throng. I fling out an arm in the general direction of the disappearing vehicle,
'Look, there's our car, and he's driving off with it! He drove it right past you and you call yourselves security, well you wouldn't be able to look after a…' 
I break off when I hear the other guard, who has come onto the scene and is beckoning me. The small crowd’s eyes swivel as one to where he’s standing.
‘Madame, come!’ he calls. ‘ I think I have found your car.’
‘What?' I follow him around the corner and immediately see the car. It is exactly where we parked it earlier this morning.
‘Oh, well, how was I... Well, my husband must have moved it!’ I exclaim, a little unfairly. 
‘But isn't your husband diving?' asks the guard. His arms are now folded and his expression tells me his patience is wearing thin.
‘Well yes, he is, but he must have moved it when I was in the lounge.’ 
‘Aren't those the car keys in your hand?’ he asks, with a glance at my hands.
I look down at the bunch of keys I’m clutching. I know when I'm beaten. I mumble my apologies and start to walk towards the car.
‘Please, Madame, can you move this car to a better place over there?’
He waves his hand to another empty spot further up. I climb in and it auto-locks. Immediately, the heat builds up and sweat pours off me. I try to find the release button without success so I fumble with the gearstick. It doesn't budge. I put the gear into neutral and switch the engine off. The gearstick eases into first. I turn the engine on and try again – nothing! Drops of sweat fall onto the steering wheel and I cry out in irritation. The guard has been watching my red-faced, sweaty antics with growing alarm. He ambles over and watches me for a minute. I spend a further two minutes trying to unlock the door and open the window, each time sending an alarm beeping until finally, with my last breath, I manage to open the window and gasp at the guard that the sodding car is not drivable. 
‘ What is the problem?’ asks the patient guard. 
‘ The problem is, I can't get it into gear!’ I snarl. 
‘ Look!’  And again I fumble with the gears. 
The guard peers in to see what I’m doing, then straightens up. 
‘ Perhaps if Madame puts her foot on the clutch, it might work,’ he suggests calmly. 
I glance down. My foot is resting firmly on the brake pedal. I realise that in my agitated state my addled brain thought I was driving my automatic. The lack of oxygen hasn’t helped.
‘ Oh, ha ha. Silly me,’ I burble with a tremulous smile. 
‘ You see, I drive an automatic at home. Thank you so much!’ 
He smiles but says nothing and I feel my already glistening, puce face redden even more. I grate the gears into place and haltingly drive the vehicle to where the guard indicated. I hop out, grab my bag and sunglasses and head very quickly down to the beach. Bugger, bugger, bugger! Dumb, dumb, dumb, I mutter loudly, which does nothing to help relieve the illusion of insanity.
The beach is crowded with people; locals selling their wares and Italians walking up and down in skimpy bikinis, and I'm not just talking about youngsters. I’m reminded of old, brown, withered pieces of animated biltong. The younger ones are hanging onto their 'holiday Maasai’, a predilection the Italians seem inordinately fond of in these parts.The sand is full of seagrass in various stages of desiccation and it makes the edge of the beach uninviting. I am approached by three locals, a Maasai and an Italian en route to the other side. I blame the tourists for making lone women easy targets for selling their services as 'holiday companions’. I wander over to a stall selling colourful scarves and wraps and after fingering a few for softness, buy two and a small beaded bracelet. At two, the dive boat arrives back as the tide is slowly receding. Col emerges with his hat and plastic crate of camera gear. He and Steve join me on the deck and we have lunch while Col and Steve go into 'when we...' mode.
The lights in our room were faulty, so we had to shower in the dark. Then, like some weird silent movie, they flickered back on just as I was walking stark naked into the front room. I froze, caught like a rabbit in the headlights. Our room did not possess a door; it was completely open to the elements. The light went off again, and I continued padding over to find my clothes. Just as I reached for my trousers and top, the light flickered on again, and my hidden audience now had a perfect view of my pink bottom. I dashed behind the bedpost and dressed quickly. I found a lot of small shells and a dried-out pufferfish, which I carried back almost to the front of Midas Cove before dropping it somewhere near the sharp coral rocks. I showered and looked for my clean trousers—Zero told me the maid had taken them to dry properly. Later, dancers arrived with their drums and kit. Never one to miss an opportunity to make a spectacle of herself, I jumped in. I was given a tall headdress of white ostrich feathers and a grass hip skirt, and as soon as the drums rolled, I was shaking my booty with the best of them. We went a couple of rounds; Col took some photos which, thank heavens, were blurred. Everyone congratulated me on my efforts, claiming I had missed my true calling. I was summoned back into the circle so that the mzee (elder) could give me a local name. I am to be called ‘Mguu ya Shamba’, or ‘Legs of the Field’! Col found this hilarious—me, not so much—but I was honoured to have been given a local name. 

The Journey Home 

I couldn’t believe it when the tinkling of cups announced Zero and his tea a few minutes later. I stumbled out of bed, fumbling around to get dressed by torchlight. I felt a bit woozy, but considering the alcohol consumed only a few hours earlier, it wasn't too bad. We swallowed our last cup of Kenyan tea and aided by the ever-helpful Zero, packed the car. We said goodbye to him and his colleague; Zero had my email address and promised to write. I crossed his palm with a few hundred shillings and we set off down the windy, dark road, past the villages and over the humps twice, then onto the main road. The traffic picked up as we approached the Malindi area, and Col had to adopt some local driving ‘techniques’ to get us to the airport on time. We were excited to see Mount Kilimanjaro’s snow-covered peak again as we flew over. They dished out a tray of carbs—bread rolls and croissants with high-fructose fruit—which I ate hungrily with lots of butter. Imagine my annoyance when they came around with a poached-egg meal just as I was wiping the last crumb from my mouth. It is hard to think that only a few hours earlier, I was pounding the earth with my ‘shamba’ legs and shaking my feathers, Kenya-style! We both felt the holiday was too short—a true test of a good vacation.
