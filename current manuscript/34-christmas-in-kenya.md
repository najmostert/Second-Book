# Chapter 31: Christmas in Kenya

We were returning to Kenya after many years to spend Christmas with the family and made a split decision to send the luggage on to Nairobi. This was not a good idea, it turned out later, but at that moment it seemed the only way to ensure it got onto the plane as we only had an hour between flights.

The Cape Town to Johannesburg flight was uneventful. We made our way through customs and found a seat near a café. We ordered a drink and I glanced up at the flight information and nearly spilled my coffee. It said the flight to Kenya had been cancelled, and before I could relay this information to Col, the next flight was also cancelled. I hurried to the ‘Information’ kiosk under the mistaken idea they would provide some. Instead they were vague about the reasons for the cancellations. No one was prepared to accept responsibility and we were just sent from pillar to post. Eventually Col stood in the KQ airlines line and I decided to stand in first class. A good move it turned out, as I was asked by an official if he could help and I told him he could indeed. He sorted out the incompetent ticket official and we were given two tickets on the 2:30 flight which wasn’t even on the board yet, being as how it was only 9 a.m.

We were anxious to get to Nairobi in time to catch the next plane but this was not to be. Shortly after take-off, an air hostess came up to us and informed us that one of our pieces of luggage was missing. I was convinced it was my case but said to Col if it was his, he would at least have four changes of clothing. He looked at me with a puzzled expression and asked how I had worked that out. I told him that his trousers, which had removable zipper legs, could be worn four ways: full trousers, one leg removed, the other removed and lastly as shorts. We landed at four-twenty and rushed to get our luggage. Sure enough it was my case that was missing. I told Col he could keep his zippered trousers as I was the one who was going to wear my jeans and top four ways, mostly back to front.

The damp heat hit us like a wet sock as we walked around the small Nairobi airport looking for some answers, if not my actual suitcase. Col insisted that the authorities sign a lost luggage report as they wanted us to wait until we got to Mombasa. We had missed the connecting flight, needless to say, and ended up at a nearby hotel for the night.

I couldn’t believe it when the alarm startled me out of my sleep for the second time in two days. We trooped downstairs and had a quick breakfast before taking a taxi to the airport. Our plane was delayed but by ten we were on board the short flight to Mombasa. They had barely finished flinging peanuts at us before landing.

Col drove us, using Sonja’s directions, towards the place we were staying. It took us three hours and by the time we got to ‘Manfridays Midas Cove’, it was already three in the afternoon. A smiling Kenyan with lovely white teeth showed us to our room which was a lovely open-plan stone rondavel with a thatched roof, stone seating with cushions and a large mosquito netting-lined bed. Sonja, one of the owners, met us then we were driving to where Col’s family were staying for Christmas Eve. We shoved stuff in a bag, grabbed all the presents and left. It was another hour away so we were pretty exhausted when we arrived in Vipingo to a nice big house with a huge verandah. Col’s mum, sister, brother-in-law and children were all there. We hugged each other, had a drink and caught up.

Col and I left early after Christmas with the family. It took an hour to get back to Sonja’s place. Papu met us as we arrived and introduced himself as ‘the other half of the problem’. He had an American accent but was a Seychellois and Indian mix. Short, stocky and very welcoming. We had a cold shower and joined Sonja and Papu at the bar for wine and a chat. Later on we met the other group staying there, a Swiss man, his local Kenyan wife and later their sulky daughter of around fifteen years old.

The rooms were designed and built using natural materials — shell curtains, raffia and wood — and decorated with things from the sea. We were at the Sekoke Forest. Col spoke to the official in the forest about returning and we drove on to Gede. It had changed since we were last there nineteen years ago. They had a lookout ledge built on a huge baobab. The ruins were interesting and you could see the Muslim influence in the design.

We drove back to Manfridays and joined Sonja, Papu and the Swiss couple at the bar. Halfway through chatting, we were joined by their daughter who started chatting as though a button had been switched on. She opined about everything and argued with everyone as she appeared to know better. Rather precocious. Papu was on top form and regaled us with stories from his past. He brought us all a round of Amarula and teased the opinionated youngster as well.

The full moon lightened the dawn and the sea was gleaming like liquid mercury.

Col left early without breakfast for the Sekoke Forest. I was still tired but decided to get up and go for a walk as it was mildly cooler walking out at sea on the beach and I was wearing my sandals to prevent cutting my feet on the exposed jagged coral. I waded in up to my waist till I could go no further and turned back. Back at our room, I glanced in the mirror. A beetroot with red eyes stared back. I was badly burned on my back as well which the sun cream couldn’t get to. I was really annoyed as I was only too aware that my complexion couldn’t take too much sun. The heavy oppressive clouds that built up after I returned suddenly opened up and fell as heavy rain.

Col and I walked around the perimeter of Gede. There were many more ruins than we thought at first. The ruins are remnants of a mysterious medieval Swahili civilisation built from stone. Gede (or Gedi) was once a centre for trade along the East African coast and the remains of these stone structures and foundations were dotted throughout the forest. Set amongst overgrown indigenous woodland, with dancing butterflies, colourful birds and Sykes’ monkeys jumping in the thicket, the narrow paths meandered between the excavated buildings and were worth exploring. The ruins were a reminder of the decadent cross-continental trade and diverse cultures of East Africa.

While walking along the pathways we came across the back end of an elephant shrew. There were ruins of walls, wells and mosques everywhere. It must have been a large city. Then we saw something very special — thinking at first they were two large hares but they turned out to be Ader’s duikers which are very rare and only found here and in Tanzania, although it was once thought to be extinct in the Arabuko Sekoke Forest.

Col returned from the forest for lunch of calamari and garlic sauce, then we got ready to leave for Vipingo to celebrate New Year’s Eve with the family. It was a festive evening and we returned just after midnight after bidding a fond farewell to the family who were leaving soon after.

Zero brought us tea around 4 a.m. It was a long drive over a tortuous, sandy, rutted road to our destination; Tsavo Park and once we turned off past Malindi it took three hours in all to get to the gates of Tsavo. We drove in nearly overturning the car as the road was full of dongas (deep holes or ditches). We had to swerve several times to avoid killing a couple of guinea fowl.

The park is quite big and the terrain changes from sandy, open shrub to grassy bushland and we saw elephants, giraffe, zebra, waterbuck, and two pale-skinned lions with swollen bellies that rested on an anthill. These two were related to the infamous lion pair who ate humans while the railway was being built in the early 1900s so we gave them a wide berth. John Patterson was a military officer and hunter who was overseeing the construction of a bridge at the time. He shot the man-eater lion and later wrote about his experiences. We left the park after stopping for a quick cup of coffee at a tourist shop. The drive was long and difficult, given the state of the roads. We came across a caracal at dusk — slinking across the road in a pale crouch, just like a domestic kitty. It disappeared into the undergrowth then peered through a bush at us before coming out again. As we were trying to take a photo, sod’s law dictated that the only other vehicle we had seen since leaving came rumbling along right in our way, which naturally startled the caracal and it disappeared.

Zero woke us again with cups of very welcome tea. Col was rummaging around with his camera equipment and diving gear, so we left a little late but managed to get to Watamu and Ocean Sports by seven forty-five. This was mostly because we were flying over speed bumps most of the time.

We told the guards at the gate that Col was diving, so they let us park inside, past the booms. I decided to go for a walk, so needed to drop off my heavy backpack in the car. I walked straight to where I thought we had parked. And naturally, the car wasn’t there, so I panicked and looked wildly around for it. I marched up to one of the guards and asked them where the car was.

‘Where did you park it?’ asked the first guard.

‘I parked it there, in front of you!’ I replied shrilly.

‘And who was driving?’

‘What does it matter who was driving? It’s gone now! Right past your nose!’

‘What is the make and registration of the car?’ asked the second guard helpfully.

‘I don’t bloody know! It’s a hired car. You need to help me find it. It’s a dark colour, almost black.’ I paused for a few seconds. ‘I think it’s called a Scorpio.’

I glanced down towards the car park and saw just such a car pulling out and started running after it.

‘Hi, I’m looking for a car that looks just like this!’ I announced breathlessly to the startled face of the Indian driver, who had recklessly wound down his window.

‘Well, this is my car,’ he said firmly. ‘Do you want to buy one?’

‘No, no, our hired car is stolen — it looks like this one,’ I broke off and peered wildly into the back seat, trying to see if he had hidden our stuff. The driver looked annoyed and started to reverse again. I clung on with my fingers.

‘This looks just like our car, where did you find it?’ I asked suspiciously, my fingers clutching at the window pane as I peered in.

There was a small crowd gathering now and they all gaped at the unusual sight of a wild-haired woman clinging to a moving vehicle.

‘Leave us alone, this is my husband’s car!’ stated the portly woman in a blue sari from the passenger seat.

She was glaring at me in anger. The car picked up speed and my clawing, tenuous grasp slid off the window, leaving greasy finger marks. The man looked fearful as I lunged again at the moving car and he picked up speed. I stood helplessly, panting in fury, and turned to one of the guards who had joined the throng. I flung out an arm in the general direction of the disappearing vehicle.

‘Look, there’s our car, and he’s driving off with it! He drove it right past you and you call yourselves security, well you wouldn’t be able to look after a…’

I broke off when I heard the other guard, who had come onto the scene and was beckoning me. The small crowd’s eyes swivelled as one to where he was standing.

‘Madame, come!’ he called. ‘I think I have found your car.’

‘What?’ I followed him around the corner and immediately saw the car. It was exactly where we had parked it earlier that morning.

‘Oh, well, how was I… Well, my husband must have moved it!’ I exclaimed, a little unfairly.

‘But isn’t your husband diving?’ asked the guard. His arms were now folded and his expression told me his patience was wearing thin.

‘Well yes, he is, but he must have moved it when I was in the lounge.’

‘Aren’t those the car keys in your hand?’ he asked, with a glance at my hands.

I looked down at the bunch of keys I was clutching. I knew when I was beaten. I mumbled my apologies and started to walk towards the car.

‘Please, Madame, can you move this car to a better place over there?’

He waved his hand to another empty spot further up. I climbed in and it auto-locked. Immediately, the heat built up and sweat poured off me. I tried to find the release button without success so I fumbled with the gearstick. It didn’t budge. I put the gear into neutral and switched the engine off. The gearstick eased into first. I turned the engine on and tried again — nothing! Drops of sweat fell onto the steering wheel and I cried out in irritation. The guard had been watching my red-faced, sweaty antics with growing alarm. He ambled over and watched me for a minute. I spent a further two minutes trying to unlock the door and open the window, each time sending an alarm beeping until finally, with my last breath, I managed to open the window and gasp at the guard that the sodding car was not drivable.

‘What is the problem?’ asked the patient guard.

‘The problem is, I can’t get it into gear!’ I snarled.

‘Look!’ And again I fumbled with the gears.

The guard peered in to see what I was doing, then straightened up.

‘Perhaps if Madame puts her foot on the clutch, it might work,’ he suggested calmly.

I glanced down. My foot was resting firmly on the brake pedal. I realised that in my agitated state my addled brain thought I was driving my automatic. The lack of oxygen hadn’t helped.

‘Oh, ha ha. Silly me,’ I burbled with a tremulous smile.

‘You see, I drive an automatic at home. Thank you so much!’

He smiled but said nothing and I felt my already glistening, puce face redden even more. I grated the gears into place and haltingly drove the vehicle to where the guard indicated. I hopped out, grabbed my bag and sunglasses and headed very quickly down to the beach. Bugger, bugger, bugger! Dumb, dumb, dumb, I muttered loudly, which did nothing to help relieve the illusion of insanity.

The beach was crowded with people; locals selling their wares and Italians walking up and down in skimpy bikinis, and I wasn’t just talking about youngsters. I was reminded of old, brown, withered pieces of animated biltong. The younger ones were hanging onto their ‘holiday Maasai’, a predilection the Italians seem inordinately fond of in these parts. The sand was full of seagrass in various stages of desiccation and it made the edge of the beach uninviting. I was approached by three locals, a Maasai and an Italian en route to the other side. I blamed the tourists for making lone women easy targets for selling their services as ‘holiday companions’. I wandered over to a stall selling colourful scarves and wraps and after fingering a few for softness, bought two and a small beaded bracelet. I returned to Ocean Sports and ordered a cold Tusker and sat in the lounge again to cool off and voilà! There was Wi-Fi, so I immediately went on Facebook and chatted to Tim.

At two, the dive boat arrived back as the tide was slowly receding. Col emerged with his hat and plastic crate of camera gear. He and Steve joined me on the deck and we had lunch while Col and Steve went into ‘when we…’ mode.

The lights in our room were faulty so we had to shower in the dark. Then, like some weird silent movie, they came back on just as I was walking stark naked into the front room where I stopped, caught like a rabbit in the headlights. Our room did not possess a door. It was in fact completely open to the elements. The light went off again and I continued padding over to find my clothes. Just as I felt for my trousers and top, the light went on again and my hidden audience now had a good view of my pink bottom. I dashed behind the bedpost and dressed quickly. Just as well, as all the on-off lights had brought Sonja over. Col chose that moment of darkness to emerge naked from his shower and had just enough time to dash behind a narrow pillar as Sonja approached. I stifled my giggles and told her we were fine, but someone needed to look at the lights. This brought to her mind the memory of an amusing incident involving lights and Sonja retold her story at length while Col stood miserably buck-naked behind his pillar, waiting for her to leave. We later joined them (fully clothed) for a drink.

Later, Col returned to the forest to fetch his traps and to look for the golden shrews. I went for a long walk along the beach to find some shells. The beach on this side was also full of brown shreds of seagrass, like grated cabbage that had been left out too long. I found a lot of small shells and a dried-out pufferfish, which I carried back to almost the front of Midas Cove before dropping it somewhere near the sharp coral rocks. I had a shower and looked for my clean trousers — Zero said the maid took them to dry properly.

Later dancers arrived with their drums and kit and we watched the group of women; three fairly old women, do their thing, shaking and yodelling to the beat of the drums. After a while, they called us up to join them. Never one to miss an opportunity to make a spectacle of herself, I jumped in. I was given a tall headdress of white ostrich feathers and a grass hip skirt, and as soon as the drums rolled, I was shaking my booty with the best of them. We went a couple of rounds. Col took some photos which were blurred, thank heavens. Everyone congratulated me on my efforts and said I had missed my calling. I was summoned back into the circle so that the mzee (elder) could give me a local name. I was to be called ‘Mguu ya Shamba’, or ‘Legs of the Field’! Col found this hilarious — me, not so much, but I was still honoured to have been given a local name.

I could not believe it when the tinkling of cups announced Zero and the tea what felt like a few minutes later. I stumbled out of bed and fumbled around, trying to wake up and get dressed by torchlight. I felt a bit woozy, but considering the number of alcoholic beverages I had consumed only a few hours earlier, it wasn’t too bad.

We swallowed our last cup of Kenyan tea and aided by the ever-helpful Zero, packed the car. We said goodbye to him and his colleague. Zero had my email address, with a promise to write. I crossed his palm with a few hundred shillings and we set off down the windy, dark road, past the two villages and over the humps twice, then onto the main road. It started off quiet, but soon the traffic picked up as we approached the Malindi area. Col had to adopt some local driving ‘techniques’ as we had very little time to get to the airport.

We were excited to see Mount Kilimanjaro’s snow-covered peak again as we flew over and took some photos from the small window. A French woman asked Col to take a photo for her. They dished out a tray of carbs for lunch: bread rolls and croissants with some high-fructose fruit. I was annoyed but also hungry, so I ate them all with lots of butter. Imagine my further annoyance when they came around with a poached-egg meal just as I was wiping the last carb-laden crumb from my mouth. It was hard to think that only a few hours earlier, I was pounding the earth with my ‘shamba’ legs and shaking my feathers, Kenya style! We both felt the holiday was too short — a true test of a good vacation!
