# Chapter 31: Christmas in Kenya

We were returning to Kenya after many years to spend Christmas with the family and made a split decision to send the luggage on to Nairobi. This was not a good idea, it turned out later, but at that moment it seemed the only way to ensure it got onto the plane as we only had an hour between flights.

The Cape Town to Johannesburg flight was uneventful. We made our way through customs and found a seat near a café. We ordered a drink and I glanced up at the flight information and nearly spilled my coffee. It said the flight to Kenya had been cancelled and before I could relay this information to Col the next flight was also cancelled. I hurried to the ‘Information’ kiosk under the mistaken idea they would provide some. Instead they were vague about the reasons for the cancellations. No one was prepared to accept responsibility and I was  sent from pillar to post. Eventually Col stood in the KQ airlines line whilst I stood in first class. A good move it turned out as I was asked by an official if he could help and I told him he could indeed. He sorted out the incompetent ticket official and we were given two tickets on the 2:30 flight which wasn’t even on the board yet, being as how it was only 9 a.m.

We were anxious to get to Nairobi in time to catch the next plane but this was not to be. Shortly after take-off, an air hostess informed us that one of our pieces of luggage was missing. I was convinced it was my case but said to Col if it was his, he would at least have four changes of clothing. He looked at me with a puzzled expression and asked how I had worked that out. I told him that his trousers, which had removable zipper legs, could be worn four ways: full trousers, one leg removed, the other removed and lastly as shorts. He stared at me but said nothing. We landed  and rushed to get our luggage. Sure enough it was my case that was missing. I told Col he could keep his zippered trousers as I was the one who was going to wear my jeans and top four ways, mostly back to front.

The damp heat hit us like a wet sock as we walked around the small Nairobi airport looking for some answers, if not my actual suitcase. Col insisted the authorities sign a lost luggage report as they wanted us to wait until we got to Mombasa. We had missed the connecting flight needless to say and ended up at a nearby hotel for the night.

I couldn’t believe it when the alarm startled me out of my sleep for the second time in two days. We trooped downstairs and had a quick breakfast before taking a taxi to the airport. Our plane was delayed but by ten we were on board the short flight to Mombasa. They had barely finished flinging peanuts at us before landing.

Col drove in a hired car, using Sonja’s directions towards the place we were staying. It took us three hours and by the time we got to ‘Manfridays Midas Cove’, it was already three in the afternoon. A smiling Kenyan man with gleaming white teeth showed us to our room; an open-plan stone rondavel with a thatched roof, stone seating with cushions and a large mosquito netting-lined bed. We briefly met Sonja, one of the owners before driving to where Col’s family were staying for Christmas Eve. I shoved stuff in a bag and grabbed all the presents. It was an hour away so we were pretty exhausted when we arrived in Vipingo to a  big house with a huge verandah. Col’s mum, sister, brother-in-law and children were all there. We hugged each other, had a drink and caught up.

Col and I left early after Christmas with the family.  Papu met us as we arrived and introduced himself as ‘the other half of the problem’. He had an American accent but was a Seychellois and Indian mix. Short, stocky and very welcoming. We had a cold shower and joined blonde haired Sonja and Papu at the bar for wine and a chat. Later we met the other group staying there; a Swiss man, his local Kenyan wife and later their sulky daughter of around fifteen.

The rooms were designed and built using natural materials — shell curtains, raffia and wood — and decorated with items from the sea such as a curtain of abandoned flip-flops. Col drove us to Gede. It had changed since we were last there nineteen years before. 
The Gede (or Gedi) ruins are the remains of a once-prosperous Swahili coastal town near Watamu and Malindi, tucked into dense coastal forest.
Walking through, you can still see the stone skeleton of a real town: coral-built houses, narrow lanes, wells, several mosques, a palace-like complex and enclosing walls. The coral stone has weathered softly, and trees grow through and over the ruins, which makes the site feel less like a monument and more like a city being quietly reclaimed by the forest.
They had a lookout ledge built on a huge baobab tree. 

We drove back to Manfridays and joined Sonja, Papu and the Swiss couple at the bar. Halfway through chatting,  their daughter joined us and started chatting as though a button had been switched on. She opined about everything and argued with everyone as she appeared to know better. Rather precocious. Papu was on top form and regaled us with stories from his past. He brought us all a round of Amarula and teased the opinionated youngster as well.

The full moon lightened the dawn and the sea was gleaming like liquid mercury.
Col left early without breakfast for the Sekoke Forest. I was still tired but decided to go for a walk as it was mildly cooler walking out at sea on the beach. I wore sandals to prevent cutting my feet on the exposed jagged coral and waded in up to my waist till I could go no further and turned back. The heavy oppressive clouds that built up after I returned suddenly opened up and fell as heavy rain.

Col and I returned to Gede and walked around the perimeter. Gede (or Gedi) was once a centre for trade along the East African coast and the remains of these stone structures and foundations were dotted throughout the forest. Set amongst overgrown indigenous woodland, with dancing butterflies, colourful birds and Sykes’ monkeys jumping in the thicket, the narrow paths meandered between the excavated buildings and were a reminder of the decadent cross-continental trade and diverse cultures of East Africa.

Col returned from the forest for lunch of calamari and garlic sauce, then we prepared for Vipingo to celebrate New Year’s Eve with the family. It was a festive evening and we returned just after midnight after bidding a fond farewell to everyone who were leaving soon after.

Zero of the gleaming teeth brought us tea around 4 a.m. It was a long drive over a tortuous, sandy, rutted road to our destination; Tsavo Park and once we turned off past Malindi it took three hours in all to get to the gates of Tsavo. 

The park was large and the terrain changed from sandy, open shrub to grassy bushland where we came across elephants, giraffe, zebra, waterbuck and two pale-skinned lions with swollen bellies that rested on an anthill. These two were related to the infamous lion pair who ate humans while the railway was being built in the early 1900's, so we gave them wide berth. John Patterson was a military officer and hunter who was overseeing the construction of a bridge at the time. He shot the man-eater lion and later wrote about his experiences. 
We left the park after stopping for a quick cup of coffee at a tourist shop. The drive was long and difficult, given the state of the roads. A caracal at dusk was slinking across the road in a pale crouch, just like a domestic kitty. It disappeared into the undergrowth then peered through a bush at us before coming out again. As we were trying to take a photo, sod’s law dictated that the only other vehicle we had seen since leaving came rumbling past, starteling the caracal and it disappeared.

Zero woke us again with tea and we got to Watamu and Ocean Sports centre by seven forty-five. This was mostly because we were flying over speed bumps most of the way.

Col leaned out and informed the guard that he was diving so they let us park inside, at the booms. Col disapeared and I wanted to go for a walk, so needed to drop off my heavy backpack in the car. I walked back to where I thought we had parked but the car wasn’t there. I panicked and looked wildly around before marching up to one of the guards to ask them where the car was.

‘Where did you park it?’ asked the first guard.

‘I parked it there’ I replied shrilly pointing near the booms.

‘And who was driving?’

‘What does it matter who was driving? It’s gone now!’

‘What is the make and registration of the car?’ asked the second guard helpfully.

‘I don’t bloody know! It’s a hired car. You need to help me find it. It’s a dark colour, almost black.’ I paused for a few seconds. ‘I think it’s called a Scorpio.’

I glanced down towards the car park and saw just such a car pulling out and started running after it.

‘Hi, I’m looking for a car that looks just like this!’ I announced breathlessly to the startled face of the Indian driver who had recklessly wound down his window.

‘Well, this is my car,’ he said firmly. ‘Do you want to buy one?’

‘No, no, our hired car is stolen — it looks just like this one,’ I broke off and peered wildly into the back seat, trying to see if I could identify anything of ours. The driver looked annoyed and started to reverse again. I clung onto the door.

‘This looks just like our car, where did you find it?’ I asked suspiciously, my fingers clutching at the window pane as I peered in.
There was a small crowd gathering now and they all gaped at the sight of this insane woman clinging to a moving vehicle.

‘Leave us alone, this is my husband’s car!’ stated the portly woman in a blue sari from the passenger seat.
She was glaring at me in anger. The car picked up speed and my clawing, tenuous grasp slid off the window, leaving greasy finger marks. The man looked fearful as I lunged again at the moving car and he picked up speed. I stood helplessly, panting in fury and turned to one of the guards who had joined the throng. I flung out an arm in the general direction of the disappearing vehicle.

‘Look, there’s our car, and he’s driving off with it! What are we going to…’

I broke off when I heard the other guard, who had entered the scene and was beckoning me. The small crowd’s eyes swivelled as one to where he was standing.

‘Madame, come!’ he called. ‘I think I have found your car.’
‘What?’ I followed him around the corner and immediately saw the car. It was exactly where we had parked it earlier that morning.
‘Oh, well, how was I… Well, my husband must have moved it.’ I exclaimed, a little unfairly.
‘But isn’t your husband diving?’ asked the guard. His arms were now folded and his expression told me his patience was wearing thin.
‘Well yes, he is, but he must have moved it when I was in the lounge.’
‘Aren’t those the car keys in your hand?’ he asked, with a glance at my hands.
I looked down at the bunch of keys I was clutching. I knew when I was beaten and mumbled my apologies and stalked off towards the car. He wasn't finished with me however.
‘Please, Madame, can you move this car to a better place over there?’

He waved his hand to another empty spot further up. I climbed in and the car auto-locked. Immediately, the heat built up and sweat poured off me. I tried to find the release button without success so fumbled with the gearstick. It didn’t budge. I put the gear into neutral and switched the engine off. The gearstick eased into first. I turned the engine on and tried again — nothing! Drops of sweat fell onto the steering wheel and I cried out in frustration. The guard had been watching my red-faced, sweaty antics with growing alarm. He ambled over and watched me for a minute as I spent a further two minutes trying to unlock the door and open the window, each time sending an alarm beeping until finally with my last breath, I managed to open the window and gasp at the guard that the sodding car was not drivable.

‘What is the problem?’ asked the patient guard.

‘The problem is, I can’t get it into gear!’ I snarled.

‘Look!’ And again I fumbled with the gears.

The guard peered in to see what I was doing, then straightened up.

‘Perhaps if Madame puts her foot on the clutch, it might work,’ he suggested calmly.

I glanced down. My foot was resting firmly on the brake pedal. I realised that in my agitated state my addled brain thought I was driving my automatic. The lack of oxygen hadn’t helped.
‘Oh, ha ha. Silly me,’ I burbled with a tremulous smile.
‘You see, I drive an automatic at home. Thank you so much!’
He smiled but said nothing and I felt my already glistening, puce face redden even more. I grated the gears into place and haltingly drove the vehicle to where the guard indicated. I hopped out, grabbed my bag and sunglasses and headed very quickly down to the beach. Bugger, bugger, bugger! Dumb, dumb, dumb, I muttered loudly, which did nothing to help relieve the illusion of insanity.

The beach was crowded with people; locals selling their wares and Italian women walked up and down in skimpy bikinis and I'm not just talking about youngsters. I was reminded of old, brown, withered pieces of animated biltong. The younger ones were hanging onto their ‘holiday Maasai’, a predilection the Italians seemed inordinately fond of in those parts. The sand was full of seagrass in various stages of desiccation resulting in an uninviting beach. I was approached by three locals, a Maasai and an Italian en route to the other side of the beach. I blamed the tourists for making lone women easy targets for selling their services as ‘holiday companions’. I continued to wander over to a stall selling colourful scarves and wraps and after fingering a few for softness, bought two and a small beaded bracelet. I returned to Ocean Sports, ordered a cold Tusker and sat in the lounge to cool off and voilà! Wi-Fi was available, so I immediately went onto Facebook to message Tim.

The dive boat returned as the tide was slowly receding. Col emerged with his hat and plastic crate of camera gear. He and Steve joined me on the deck and we had lunch while Col and Steve went into ‘when we…’ mode.

The lights in our room at Manfriday's were faulty so we were showering in the dark. Like some weird silent movie, they came back on just as I was walking stark naked into the front room where I stopped, caught like a rabbit in the headlights. Our room did not possess a door. It was in fact completely open to the elements. The light went off again and I continued padding over to find my clothes. Just as I felt for my jeans and T-shirt, the light came on again and my hidden audience now had a good view of my pink bottom. I dashed behind the bedpost and dressed quickly. Just as well as all the on-off lights had brought Sonja over. Col chose that moment of darkness to emerge naked from his shower and had just enough time to dash behind a narrow pillar as Sonja approached. I stifled my giggles and told her we were fine, but someone needed to look at the lights. This brought to her mind the memory of an amusing incident involving lights and Sonja retold her story at length while Col stood miserably buck-naked behind his pillar waiting for her to leave. We later joined them (fully clothed) for a drink.

Later, Col returned to the forest to look for golden shrews while I took a long walk along the beach to find shells. The beach on this side was also full of brown shreds of seagrass, like grated cabbage that had been left out too long. I found some small shells and a dried-out pufferfish. 

Later that evening, dancers arrived with their drums and kit and we watched a group of women; three fairly old women, do their thing shaking and yodelling to the beat of the drums. After a while, they called us up to join them. Never one to miss an opportunity to make a spectacle of herself, I jumped in. I was given a tall headdress of white ostrich feathers and a grass hip skirt and as soon as the drums rolled, I was shaking my booty with the best of them. We went a couple of rounds. Col took some photos which were blurred thank heavens. Everyone congratulated me on my efforts and said I had missed my calling. I was summoned back into the circle so that the mzee (elder) could give me a local name. I was to be called ‘Mguu ya Shamba’, or ‘Legs of the Field’! Col found this hilarious — me, not so much, but I was honoured to have been given a local name. We said our farewell to our delightful hosts as we were to leave early next morning.

I could not believe it when the tinkling of cups announced Zero and tea after what felt like a few minutes later. I stumbled out of bed and fumbled around, trying to wake up and get dressed by torchlight. I felt a bit woozy but considering the number of alcoholic beverages I had consumed only a few hours earlier, it wasn’t too bad.

I swallowed my last cup of strong Kenyan tea and aided by the ever-helpful Zero, packed the car. Zero took my email address with a promise to write. I crossed his palm with a few hundred shillings and we set off down the windy, dark road, past two villages and over the humps twice then onto the main road. It started off quiet, but soon the traffic picked up as we approached the Malindi area. Col had to adopt some local driving ‘techniques’ as we had very little time to get to the airport.

We were excited to see Mount Kilimanjaro’s snow-covered peak again as we flew over and took some photos from the small plane window. They dished out a tray of carbs for lunch: bread rolls and croissants with some high-fructose fruit. I was annoyed but also hungry, so I ate them all with lots of butter. Imagine my further annoyance when they came around with a poached-egg meal just as I was wiping the last carb-laden crumb from my mouth. It was hard to think that only a few hours earlier, I was pounding the earth with my ‘shamba’ legs and shaking my feathers, Kenya style. We both felt the holiday had been too short — a true test of a good vacation!
