# Chapter 16: Namibia 2002: The Left-Hand-Drive Cruiser

The day dawned early with the knowledge that I still had a mountain of administrative work to complete for the Arabian Recruitment Agency—the newly formed placement agency I had set up since our return from Saudi Arabia. Despite the workload we were off to Namibia once more after an age to properly pack the left-hand-drive Toyota Land Cruiser that we had shipped from Saudi Arabia. I set the house alarm before locking the front door but we hadn’t gone far down the road when the security company phoned to say the alarms had just gone off. We were forced to turn back. We wanted to view the wild spring flowers in the West Coast National Park; a major global draw card for flower enthusiasts, with the Postberg section also offering stunning, undisturbed natural displays of wild flora. The best time—indeed, the only time—to view them is during the spring months of August and September, when colossal carpets of daisies, proteas (including the magnificent King Protea, the national flower), vygies, and wild orchids completely cover wide expanses along the West Coast. They bloom in unbelievable profusion of vibrant colours: brilliant white, deep red, orange, yellow, and purple. 
Supper at the overnight stop was served to us by a fourteen-year old boy—well let’s just say his voice hadn’t yet dropped. The next leg of the journey started in great anticipation but after an hour of driving and seeing very few Namaqua daisies actually on show, we decided to head on towards Nababeep via the village of Platlappies (I just love the old San names, the latter translating to ‘flat cloths’). There were plenty of beautiful March lilies lining the tracks along with a type of tumbleweed that looked quite pretty with its pink blossoms. As we drove further into the heart of Namaqualand, beautiful quiver trees began to dominate the horizon. Quiver trees, or kokerboom are tall, branching species of succulent aloe plants indigenous to the arid regions of Southern Africa. The historic name comes from the San practice of hollowing out the soft, spongy branches to construct quivers for holding hunting arrows. 
The surrounding road became ever more dry and less interesting as we turned onto a gravel dirt road that ran alongside Platlappies, passing by South Africa’s massive national nuclear waste repository site at Vaalputs. It was around this point that we realised we wouldn't make the border to Namibia before darkness fell so had to quickly formulate plan B. Plan B turned out to be the Kokerboom Motel, a few hours' drive shy of the boundary line. 

The Serial Number Standoff 

Dawn broke behind a curtain of driving, unforgiving rain. I noticed the dramatic change in our surroundings from dry-looking quiver trees and scrub to brownish, jagged rocky mountains that glistened like polished obsidian in the rain. At the border post we prepared ourselves mentally for the usual bureaucratic palaver and were not disappointed. The administration went fairly smoothly on the South African side of the gate, but on the Namibian side an incredibly slow border official whose understanding of the English language was slight at best took his time. While waiting, I sent some recruitment forms to my business partner back in Cape Town then returned to the counter where I had left Col twenty minutes before. He was meticulously declaring his one hundred individual pieces of camera equipment and tediously writing down every single factory serial number by hand on the customs forms. Even the weary Namibian official advised him to just write down a single line reading: ‘Camera and accessories’, to which Col replied somewhat darkly that he was going to write down every single individual item because he didn't want the guards accusing him of purchasing luxury gear over the border when we returned to South Africa. I received another urgent text message from my agency partner saying the nursing educator we had successfully placed in Saudi Arabia was not what the clients wanted. Apparently another four nursing staff members had suddenly left the hospital compound and needed to be replaced immediately. My partner told me a ‘Miss Finger’ had applied for the vacancy and I was in the process of typing a reply saying I hoped that didn't mean she was missing a digit, when my phone lost signal. 

The Dictaphone and the Erongo Rocks 

After stopping at a small roadside shop to purchase the last of their stale snacks we continued past Keetmanshoop and pulled over for a twenty-minute leg stretch. Tim was amusing himself in the back seat by farting into the portable dictaphone I had brought along for business and playing back his efforts. He was nine years old at this point and I reasoned there was only so long a pre-teen could remain interested in wild flowers and desert vistas. I made a mental note to wipe the machine's memory tape clean before my next corporate client meeting. Suddenly, our bumper accidentally hit a sandgrouse that had darted blindly into the road and Col stopped to take a look, when his eyes caught two interesting beetles in the sand. He captured the specimens inside one of our plastic water bottles; they were dead already so didn't feel too bad about the collection. Hardap Dam resort came into view a little after 6:30 p.m. 
I stopped to photograph a couple of rock hyraxes—locally known as dassies —as they were sunning themselves on a low stone wall en route to the breakfast hall. En route to Omaruru later that day we passed a hot springs resort named Gross Barmen and I wondered if they were. By afternoon both Col and I had developed a horrible case of the squits. We decided the waiter at the previous stop must have grated some minced caterpillar into our side salads and felt better for identifying the source of our liquid stomachs, before proceeding an extra six hundred kilometres out of our way just to see a rare francolin. The helmeted guinea fowl are completely different here from the ones we have in Cape Town; these desert specimens possess long, slender necks and a bright, vivid blue throat. Col turned the Cruiser down a rough dirt track and a small wooden signpost stated that the Erongo Wilderness Lodge was located nearby. We decided on a whim to stop and investigate, driving up the boulder-strewn edge of the mountain. It looked quaint; luxury thatched-roof tents built high against the solid Erongo mountain granite rock (which the manager proudly informed us was the oldest geological rock formation on Earth- he was wrong but at over 150 million years they’re still old). The manager and his wife were welcoming and we liked them immediately. It was fairly pricey for our budget though. Col asked them of our chances of spotting the rare Hartlaub’s francolin and the moment they reported that the birds were frequently seen scratching directly under the raised tents, the deal was sealed. What a spectacular, breathtaking view of the valley below! The sun was setting, casting long golden streaks across the mountain faces opposite the valley ridge. Our luxury tent was situated up some steep stone steps constructed high above the valley floor, offering a magnificent view. The tent featured an en-suite bathroom attached to the main room by a simple curtain of canvas and the shower recess was cut directly out of the natural rock face, with the toilet bowl nestling neatly between two massive boulders. This design lent a rustic, authentic air to the space. The porters brought an extra mattress in for Tim as we were unpacking our bags. The lodge dining room overlooked a floodlit waterhole and later that evening we watched a breeding herd of greater kudu treading quietly out of the surrounding scrub to enjoy a drink. Our supper was absolutely delicious: a smoked kudu and sweet melon starter garnished with fresh basil, followed by prime beef steak, potatoes, and buttered marrow with mint. 

Sammy the Boat Usurper 

This morning we prepared to take a marine boat trip out of Walvis Bay—a mournful-looking waiter had already laid the dining table, so we felt obliged have breakfast first. After purchasing the permits, Col drove thirty odd miles down to the coast to Walvis Bay. The weather was distinctly chilly and misty, the norm for the coast around Swakopmund. Our chartered boat was named Gambit and I muttered to Col that it could have been Armpit for all I cared as long as it had a working toilet on board. We were the only English-speaking passengers on board, the others being either Afrikaans or German-speaking. It was a lovely, calm day out on the water as we set off into the bay. I had just carefully placed my camera, dry bag and binoculars in the middle of the boat's storage bench and was minding my own business, when suddenly a five-ton bull fur seal exploded out of the sea and landed squarely on top of my bag and camera! I had to carefully extricate my expensive gear from beneath a ton of blubber before taking a few photos of the captain feeding the seal some frozen fish, mainly to test whether the mechanism of the camera still functioned (surprisingly, it did). Half an hour later, ‘Sammy’ the seal grew bored with the tourists and slid gently back over the fibreglass edge of the boat into the icy water. A few minutes later we spotted a Heaviside’s dolphin swimming gracefully in our wake. Our cetacean sightings weren’t limited to the Heaviside variety however, as a short while later, we encountered a school of dusky dolphins and then several massive bottlenose dolphins playing in the surf. As the boat returned toward the shoreline, the crew brought out platters of fresh coastal snacks: raw oysters and chilled champagne. Unfortunately, I find eating raw oysters akin to swallowing slimy, garden slugs, so luckily for Col, neither Tim nor I wanted our allocations and he scored an extra mountain of shellfish. Back on terra firma, we sat outside a local café for coffee and baked cheesecake to discuss our next move which was to reach the aptly named hamlet of Solitaire by evening, planning a route that cut straight through the local game reserve loops. 

Moose and the Crimson Dunes 

The surrounding landscape gradually transformed from coastal sand dunes to wide yellow grassland and massive granite outcrops. En route, we saw sandgrouse, Rüppell’s korhaan, Ludwig’s bustards, springbok, gemsbok, barn owls, a sweet family group of bat-eared foxes, black-backed jackals, wild ostriches and a magnificent spotted eagle-owl. It was an exceptional day for bird watching. As the sky darkened, the atmosphere streaked with brilliant red and gold from desert dust suspended in the air, and the surrounding dunes transformed from a pale pastel shade into a vibrant, glowing crimson colour- it was beautiful. We rumbled into Solitaire just after 6 p.m. The settlement consisted of a single petrol station that doubled as a basic motel and one-stop shop so we pulled over, booked a room for the night before joining the owner, Moose outside the communal braai area while he burned some meat over a huge coal fire. He was a tall, heavily built ex-Zambian expatriate and a true, larger-than-life character. He entertained us with wild bush stories as he cooked, taking copious, swigs of Windhoek Lager from the bottle as he turned over pieces of rapidly darkening meat on the grill. We ate our heavily cholesterol-laden meal and chatted well into the night before repairing to our hot, airless bedroom at midnight, where we tossed and turned like basting turkeys all night. The room was so uncomfortable that we lay on top of the bedspreads staring blankly at the ceiling while being continuously nibbled by insects. As I lay there sweating, I formulated a long, impassioned, eloquent speech in my head detailing exactly how I was going to tell Moose what a thoroughly miserable, uncomfortable night we’d endured due to his lack of air conditioning. So, what did I actually reply when he cheerfully asked us this morning how we’d slept? ‘Fine, thanks.’ Spineless, absolutely spineless, that’s me. 

The Shifted Clocks of Sossusvlei 

I sat outside on the bench where it was decidedly cooler, drinking a mug of strong coffee and munching on doorstep-sized sandwiches of Moose’s delicious homemade bread. Afterwards we packed the Cruiser and set off towards Sossusvlei. This famous desert area is renowned for its monumental, terracotta coloured sand dunes. When we arrived at the pan, the dunes were indeed impressive, copper-tinged pyramids of sand rising against the sky. The rich red colour deepened as the setting sun slid over the ridges. I desperately wanted the ubiquitous photograph of a gemsbok standing proud against this lovely backdrop but unfortunately, the dunes were too close to the track and there wasn't a single antelope in sight. Out of the direct range of the sun's rays, the colours of the sand became far more muted and pastel. It was a long drive across the desert and seemed ages before we reached any sign of civilisation, but we eventually pulled into a lodge named the ‘OK Corral’. Aside from persistent mosquitoes and a bright outside security light that shone directly through our window and onto my face FBI-style all night (curtains being an optional extra it seems), it wasn't a bad night’s sleep—which is British code for saying it was merely ‘OK’. The kitchen staff only appeared at 7 a.m. It was then that we heard that Namibia had officially turned its national clocks back by an hour last night; it was just a pity we hadn't got the memo. Judging from how long it had taken the cooks to fry some meat the previous evening, we wisely asked them for just toast and tea this morning. Loud, angry yelling suddenly emanated from behind the kitchen doors.
‘I’m glad we didn’t complicate things by asking for a cooked brekky’ I remarked to Col. The tea I requested arrived disguised as a mug of black coffee so I asked again for tea. I wasn’t at all sad to leave the OK Corral and head towards the South African border. A few kilometres out of town, a huge leguaan—rock monitor lizard—crossed the gravel road in front of our bumper, and Col slowed the Cruiser to let it pass safely into the scrub. After crossing the border back into South Africa, we headed onwards down the highway, passing Noheap, which sits not too far from Nababeep.
