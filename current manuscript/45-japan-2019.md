## Kanazawa

A thick, heavy blanket is draped over the frame to trap heat underneath and a tabletop sits on top of the blanket, completing the table’s surface. The heat source was traditionally a charcoal brazier but modern kotatsus have an electric heater mounted underneath the table frame, as with this one. ## Hiroshima

After making tea we set off early for the station to catch the train to Hiroshima. After taking a Shinkansen to the first stop, we transferred to the ‘Thunderbird’ — a limited express train. The train was very crowded and I had to keep my suitcases between my knees. A delightful little nine-year-old girl called Lily joined me for a while. We exchanged stories of our journey through Japan so far, and she asked me to ‘like’ her Instagram page. Tim and I delved into one of the two bento boxes we had brought along for lunch before the train pulled smoothly into Hiroshima at 1 p.m. and we grabbed a taxi to the hostel and the capsules that we had booked for the night. I was a bit nervous at first as I’m somewhat claustrophobic, but although they are small, they have curtains over the entrance, which seemed less ‘encapsulated’. They are just high enough to sit up in and have plugs for electronics, a small shelf, and a pillow with one blanket. We left our luggage locked up and explored Hiroshima. The Peace Memorial Park commemorates the aftermath of the nuclear bomb and we saw the only remains of a large building called the Hiroshima Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall (now known as the A-Bomb Dome). The only reason most of it remains standing post-bomb is that its reinforced concrete structure, located directly beneath the atomic bomb’s hypocentre, was hit by a more vertical, downward blast rather than a devastating horizontal force. The building’s solid design allowed its core frame to resist the explosion, while its location, only about 150 metres from the centre of the blast, meant the shockwave passed over it. An interesting T-shaped bridge (the Aioi Bridge) came into view which was (unintentionally) designed to withstand the blast. The slight damages meant it could be used until 1983 when extensive maintenance was undertaken. Later we went to a traditional Hiroshima-style okonomiyaki restaurant. Despite the queue, it was well worth it when we got a table. This comes with a long metal griddle tray in the centre of the table. The okonomiyaki is made at a much larger griddle in front of the smaller tables by pouring oil onto the hot surface, then adding chopped cabbage, egg, and a series of other toppings. The whole thing is quickly chopped, fried, and turned to make both sides crispy with either bacon or cheese. This is brought to your table and kept warm on the smaller griddle. An old black and white movie flickered on an overhead screen. It was a great way to end the day before heading back to the confines of our sleeping capsules. I accessed mine by climbing up a short set of wooden stairs and heaved myself into the capsule by shuffling backwards.

Tim purchased JR Ferry passes, so it was easy enough to board, show the passes, and find a seat upstairs for the ten-minute ride across the bay to the island of Miyajima and the famous red torii gate that is partially submerged in the sea. It’s a really pretty island with numerous ‘Nihonjika’ (Japanese deer), cherry blossoms, and red-flowered trees. Many shrines and a row of shops sit along the length of the beach.
It was a lovely, balmy day so many people had taken the opportunity of enjoying the improved weather. We tried a local delicacy of flattened squid sausage at one of the food stalls. They are fried on a low table with a series of small turning spits that makes watching the process akin to watching someone using a pinball machine. Another small cafe saw us munching on a cream bun with our coffees. Oyster clams are a popular treat here, but not for me. We stopped for lunch at the “Bambi” cafe for a curry. Tim, being the adventurous one, tried a large eel (Unagi).
We continued up the hill towards Daisho-in temple at the top, stopping briefly for a momiji manjū, which is a type of steamed bun: a small cake stuffed with sweetened bean paste. It is shaped like a Japanese maple leaf. I enjoyed the cream cheese filling the most. The first Prime Minister of Japan, Hirofumi Ito, was apparently strolling in Momiji Dani park, which is filled with maple trees, and was so entranced by the golden leaves that he asked his chef to make maple leaf cakes to have with green (matcha) tea. The idea caught on, and so the ‘Momiji Manjū’ cake was born. We had already eaten a few of these earlier that week.
After collecting my shoes, we continued hiking up the Hatsukaichi-Miyajimaguchi trail past rivers and streams and the ubiquitous Nihonjika deer. It was really beautiful. The Daisho-in temple complex is spread over a large area on a slope of the mountain with beautiful buildings on different levels. It was founded by the monk Kūkai in the year 806. Kūkai was one of the most famous monks in Japan and the founder of Shingon Buddhism. If you look on the left side, there are stone steps with hundreds of statues of guardian deity children. Sometimes they ‘wear’ knitted hats in red and white colours. The highest point of the temple is “Ichigandaishi”, where people believe that worshippers’ dreams will come true. It was well worth the climb and visit. We wound our way back to the harbour for the 4 p.m. ferry back to the mainland and took a train into town to find another Fuchu-yaki place (an okonomiyaki with soba that uses plenty of minced beef or pork and cabbage without adding bean sprouts), and I had one with cheese and noodle which was tasty. This was eaten while sipping a small bottle of sake. Back at the Capsule Hotel, which had become really busy and was bustling with tourists, I washed in the small, round basins and got ready for bed.

## Osaka

I was out of my capsule really early without injuring anyone’s head, joined Tim in the small breakfast room for toast and coffee before heading to the station for a train to Osaka. We were to meet up with Dan and friend in a cafe. The day was grey and drizzling, so we waited under an umbrella. A long, pink ‘Hello Kitty’ Shinkansen whizzed by in a pink blur. A man on the train had a cute brown rabbit on his lap. He had tied a red scarf around its neck and it sat quietly throughout the journey.
Osaka Aquarium Kaiyukan, which is one of Japan’s most spectacular aquariums, world-renowned for its size and presentation, is where we were headed that day. Kaiyukan displays the aquatic animals of the Pacific Rim by recreating their natural habitats. I booked a few months ago and it was a good thing I did because, being a Sunday, it was really crowded. Despite being a very large place, the crowds made it appear smaller with all the families and their hordes of bored and crying children. I was exhausted by the time we finished the tour, fed up with manoeuvring between the crowds. We returned to our hotel and checked in. The receptionist said our room was very ‘tight-ee’ after assessing Tim’s height. This proved to be true when we entered a very small room with two narrow beds and not much else. There were, however, the makings of tea so I made us each a cup of green tea, which helped.
Osaka Castle was once the largest castle in Japan in the 1580s. There’s an observation platform with great views of the city and a history museum that uses holograms, 3D pictures, and other technologies to describe the history of the castle. This five-storey building is a great example of the traditional architecture of the time. We only had time to stroll around for forty minutes or so before returning to the hotel, checking out, and dragging our luggage down the street to a Starbucks. I enjoyed the music they were playing — old and mellow. While Tim worked, I went for a short walk around Osaka town, taking photos of pretty flowers in window boxes and the view from the bridge across the Ōkawa river. The Sennichimae Line runs along one of Osaka’s bustling nighttime entertainment districts. This line is full of wonderful gourmet spots, including Kuromon Ichiba Market, where you can eat fresh seafood, meat, and fruits as you stroll. Hozenji Yokocho, with its narrow cobbled streets lined with traditional Japanese restaurants and kushikatsu spots brimming with an old-fashioned vibe in Tsuruhashi, the Osaka capital of Yakiniku barbecue. We were in search of The Design Pocket — a shop that sells sampuru (the aforementioned wax-moulded food items that are on display outside many restaurants in lieu of a menu).
I set about choosing a selection from the many on display. As they are not cheap, I decided on a very realistic fried-egg headband, a pair of mackerel earrings and a few other items that would serve as amusing presents. There are mobile phone covers made of ‘spaghetti’, rings made from ‘peas’, a ‘ham’ candle, tempura keychains, etc. I carefully packed away my ‘food’ products, and we headed for Dōtonbori, which is a long street full of lights and nighttime activities and found a small cafe on the banks of the river for supper. I brought out my fried-egg headband and fish earrings and donned both. It is probably the only place in the world where you can walk down the street wearing a fried egg on your head and no one bats an eyelid. I figured they’d go well with my chicken bag that I have at home. I’m at the age where eccentricity is accepted as normal and I’ve embraced the freedom it brings with vigour.

## Kyoto

At 9 a.m. we left for our morning cuppa joe, then boarded the train to Kyoto. There were still many cherry trees in full display which made for some gorgeous viewing through the window. Kyoto being a very traditional town, there are pretty women in strikingly beautiful coloured kimonos all over the streets.
The Fushimi Inari Shrine was next on the list of local attractions. This shrine is the largest dedicated to Inari, the Shinto God of rice and prosperity. It is famous for the large number of torii — red gates and takes an approximately two-hour ascent to reach the 233-metre summit which can be challenging but the views from the top are worth it.
It was quite a steepish climb to the top via many, many stone steps (around 12,000 altogether) and through as many red gates. It was quite lovely when we reached the top with all the colourful shrines, small green bushes, and wild flowers. There were a few signs warning us to ‘beware of the monkeys and bears’. We didn’t see any of either and I was rather disappointed. We stopped on the way down for a cup of refreshing green tea which came with a small steamed bun, as is the custom. There’s a large tree near the top called the ‘Root-up Pine Tree’ which looks like the lower body of a human, with one leg straight and the other bent. People believe this stump provides strong energy if you touch it to absorb its power, or walk under the roots to get a special blessing for your lower torso. In Japanese, it’s called “Neagari no Matsu.”
Each torii gate has been donated by a company or individual and the inscriptions on each gate provide the donor’s name with the date of the donation. There were quite a few stray cats playing around a small area with a fountain and a long-handled bamboo ladle. This is to wash your hands of impurities. After slurping a cappuccino each we started the walk back into the centre of Kyoto. As we strolled along the side of the Kamo River, we watched ducks, pigeons, cormorants, and a few grey herons. I asked a couple of kimono-clad women if I could take their photo and they happily obliged, giggling behind their colourful fans. We called in at our favourite shop for desserts — the 7-Eleven. These convenience (konbini) stores sell the most delightful desserts. Afterwards, we walked through the old geisha districts of Gion. This is Kyoto’s famous geisha district. Packed with bars, restaurants, and traditional teahouses, it comes to life in the early evening, when the lanterns are lit and geisha walk daintily on wooden geta through the streets on their way to their appointments. Geta are a combination between a flip-flop and a sandal with an elevated wooden base held onto the foot with a fabric thong to keep the foot well above the ground.
This is the best-known entertainment quarter in Kyoto. Originally its attraction was the Yasaka Shrine. It gradually developed into a town full of restaurants, including ryotei (traditional Japanese restaurants), and entertainment facilities. The Gion area in Kyoto is quite large. On the eastern end there’s Yasaka Shrine and in the opposite direction is the Kamo River on the western end. Tim pointed out a few geisha stepping gracefully out of a taxi or disappearing around a corner. A very pleasant day which ended, unfortunately, back in the ‘cabbage dump’ accommodation.

From the station we took a bus into Ukyō to see the well-known bamboo forest. Another lovely warm day ensured a very pleasant walk. Although the bamboo was not as prolific as I thought, it was still very pretty and one spot was ideal for a photo where the bamboo path meets in the distance to form a tunnel. Tim and I walked along the river until we came to a traditional restaurant where we sat watching small fishing boats sail by while eating our shrimp dish with an undercooked omelette. There were a lot of hand-driven rickshaws being pulled up and down the streets by men with well-muscled legs and grim determination. Our next delight came from a cafe selling delicious charcoal and cream cakes — they taste better than they sound. It rained a bit, but I went for an exploratory walk around Arashiyama, where the kimono-clad women walked and gossiped. I found ‘Kimono Forest’, which is a collection of gorgeous cylinder-shaped pillars framing the laneway to Randen tram station. The pillars are decorated in colourful kimono material. Tenryū-ji is the most important temple in Kyoto’s Arashiyama district. It is ranked first among the city’s five great Zen temples and is now registered as a world heritage site. It was built in 1339 by the ruling shogun Ashikaga Takauji. Takauji dedicated the temple to Emperor Go-Daigo, who had just passed away. The two important historic figures used to be allies until Takauji turned against the emperor in a struggle for supremacy over Japan. By building the temple, Takauji intended to appease the former emperor’s spirits. Unlike the temple buildings, Tenryū-ji’s garden survived the centuries in its original form. Created by Musō Soseki, the beautiful landscape garden features a central pond surrounded by rocks, pine trees and the forested Arashiyama mountains.
Kimono Forest was lit up as it was becoming dark by this time, creating a very pretty walkway. We took the train back and found a small side-road restaurant. It had no English menus, so Tim had to use his phone app to decipher and translate again. As usual, this brought up some interesting options:
‘Whimsical fried rice’, ‘Home made bacon and child’ (fruity and juicy!), ‘Mermaid in deep sea’, and my personal favourite; ‘Stir-fried wikipedia!’
We made the difficult decision to go with the item devoid of whimsy, children, mermaids, or flies and had a chicken and rice dish, which was a reasonably tasty, if safe, dish. A pamphlet at the counter asked the question, ‘What is sausage?’ but failed to provide a satisfactory answer, so we remained ignorant of any of the lesser-known characteristics and meandered slowly back to the cabbage room.
I donned my fried-egg headband and kipper earrings that morning and we set off for Himeji via Kobe. It was another lovely, balmy day as we walked up to Himeji Park and Castle. Sakura abounded and the park wore its pink mantle with pride. Carpets of fallen pink petals made for a fairy wonderland. Every blast of wind shook the trees of their dancing petals and they glided to the ground like floral snow.
Himeji Castle earned its name, “White Heron Castle,” due to its white plaster walls and appearance, which resemble a white heron taking flight. The castle dates back to the 13th century. Himeji Castle has seen its fair share of disasters. It was burned down during a war in 158. The tower and main building were rebuilt, after which it was abandoned, then lost to a large fire and in 193, the government started raising funds for reconstruction, going as far as selling clay to raise money. The rooftop of the castle was blown away by a typhoon in 1954 but continuing with its misfortune, was later reconstructed with wood that burned down completely. This unfortunate castle was registered as one of the first UNESCO World Heritage Sites in December 1993.
We walked through the outer building first — gleaming wooden floors and windows and came to the rooms where the princess used to sit. There were some rather steep steps between the rooms. Outside, the red-leafed trees clashed beautifully against the bright pink cherry blossoms. We exited the outer building and headed for the main part of the castle. The Himeji castle grounds are adorned with more than 1800 cherry blossom trees, planted around the main keep, walls, and moat. I later learnt that the castle was featured in the 1967 James Bond film, “You Only Live Twice.”
On the train to Osaka I was fascinated by a couple of young girls who spent the entire journey doing up each other’s faces. Eyebrows were carefully drawn in, then an intricate procedure involving tape was used to obtain a double-eyelid look. As one girl was transformed, the other was held to equal scrutiny and received a generous amount of face cream.
We stopped off at Kobe to find a restaurant to sample the famous Kobe beef. What a pretty town — especially at night. Buildings, trees and bushes shone and twinkled everywhere. A huge, gleaming white Ferris wheel added to the festive atmosphere. A restaurant in Higashikawasakicho overlooks the river and a full moon glimmered in the sky. The restaurant is Brazilian and serves what they call a ‘meat buffet’. Throughout the meal, waiters came up with yet another type of meat and carved thin slices to add to our plates and they were delicious and tender.
Kobe beef is a specific type of Japanese Wagyu beef that comes from the Tajima strain of Japanese Black cattle raised in the Hyōgo Prefecture. Dessert really pushed us way past satiety but was a really worthwhile experience. Afterwards, we waddled out of the restaurant and walked along the glittering pathways back to the train station.

## Nara Island

I wasn’t sorry to be leaving the ‘cabbage dump’ but had really enjoyed our time here. That day, we set off for Nara and discovered that the stations have lockers available for hourly rent so paid for two. My large suitcase would not fit no matter how much I tried, so I just left it there in the hope the country continued to show how safe and honourable its people had been so far (It did, and on our return four hours later, my suitcase had been lifted safely to the top of the lockers and remained intact). Owl Cafe en route to the park looked inviting with its cosy interior so we popped in for a coffee and an owl pat. It was a bit sad really, seeing all the owls sitting quietly (or so we surmised) at various points in the long room that serves as a cafe and observation area. They have quite a selection of owls, from the snowy owl to the huge eagle owl. The owl assistant took photos of us holding some owls on a huge leather glove that is provided for the purpose. I got my name printed in Japanese, and together with two owl key rings, we left the cafe and set off for Kōfuku-ji Temple and Nara Park. The deer are quite prolific here and walk in pairs or small groups all over the park. Various locals, seeing an opportunity to make some money, had set up simple stalls selling biscuits for the deer. This meant the deer knew that every visitor was a potential biscuit provider. I witnessed a woman bowing to a deer and the deer bowing back before receiving a treat. This Pavlovian response made for some cute photos. I wondered about the nutritional value of the biscuits and found out they are called Shika-senbei and are made from rice bran and grain. Some of the deer appeared a bit moth-eaten, but that could be a seasonal thing. From the park we walked a short distance to Tōdai-ji Temple to see the largest statue of Buddha in Japan. The Daibutsu (Great Buddha) is housed within the temple. Completed in 752, the statue stands 15 metres (49 ft) tall. It’s really huge as are the accessory statues. The big guy is made of bronze, so he really shone in the light of the day. A Shinkansen from Nara to Fuji was next. We changed trains in Yokohama to Odawara and had some really fantastic sights of Mt Fuji from the train window.

## Hakone

After a very comfortable night we set off for Hakone. Anyone imagining tatami mats uncomfortable, couldn’t be more wrong. They’re typically made with a core of compressed rice straw, covered with woven rush (igusa) straw. The edges of the mats are often reinforced with fabric borders called tatami-beri. Contemporary tatami mats might be made of compressed wood chip board or polystyrene foam for the core, instead of rice straw. In any case I slept very comfortably on mine whatever the grain.
The Lalique Museum, in Hakone, owns a collection of more than 1,500 of Lalique’s pieces, including over 200 precious jewellery items. The small garden area is also very pretty with a large mirror pond which reflects some cherry blossoms in its mercurial stillness. We walked to the harbour and boarded one of the large wooden ships for the Hakone Ropeway. These wooden ferries are replicas of old pirate ships, decorated with gold paint with wooden statues attached to the hull. Tim found a good spot for viewing near the top. The trip across to the gardens is only ten minutes long, but provides a lovely view of Lake Ashi. On the other side we wandered around the Hakone gardens and climbed some steep steps to a very pretty avenue of cypress trees. The view from the top was breathtaking with cherry blossoms wherever the eye could see. A local bus took us to the open-air museum. Huge exhibits are dotted around a large manicured garden and are absolutely stunning. There are statues of copper and steel, stone, ceramics with gold faces, a huge white head reclining in a pond with a small green bush serving as hair. Huge climbing frames, pipes, glass and chrome structures sit majestically along the open garden path. I really enjoyed the artwork and the entire experience. It took us a good two hours to look at everything. They also have a few of Picasso’s earlier works as well as a couple of his newer paintings.

## Mt Fuji

My last full night and day here in Japan and I was feeling really sad about that. We left all our luggage behind the washing line outside the room as we had to check out of our room (only in Japan can you do this and find everything as you left it on your return).
The ‘ropeway’ cable car took us up to the best view of the famous mountain. It slowly rose to 1000 metres, so you can enjoy the view en route. It was surprisingly warm at the top given the elevation. And as I turned round, in full view, was Mount Fuji in all her splendour. She wore a small hat of fluffy clouds around her brim, which also served as fluffy slippers at her feet. Many photos later, we wandered around and purchased a small packet of blackened, sulphur-steamed eggs. Clouds of steam dotted the rocky surface of the green sulphur mountain top. The black eggs are said to increase your life by seven years; although difficult to prove, many people were strolling round munching their boiled eggs with vigour, buoyed in the knowledge they had ensured a longer lifespan. Tim and I ate ours sitting at the top of the stairs with a great view of Fuji before taking the cable car back down with a group of American tourists. After walking around Hakone for a bit, we headed back to the washing line to fetch our luggage.
From here we took the next train back into Tokyo where we waited for our new host to fetch us from the station. A middle-aged man arrived in his car and took us to his traditional ryokan accommodation. Our room had the expected tatami mats, low wooden table and bamboo walls and shutters. I boiled some water for green tea before meeting his son and wife who told us in halting English that she had travelled a lot — mostly with her small terrier. The owner’s wife made us toasted muffin bread, eggs, bacon and great coffee that morning. There was an Italian couple staying here as well but they didn’t talk much to each other or to us. Tim and I had a good chat with the owner before setting off for Reno park. There was a man playing the pan flutes along the park pathway. It sounded like Peruvian music. A sweet old Japanese lady was dancing slowly and gracefully to his music, her eyes closed and her arms reaching out as if to feel the memories of her youth.
Reno Toshogo Peony park is full of the flowers of its name. There are so many varieties, sizes and colours! A feast for the eyes. Coloured umbrellas are dotted along the paths. Some of the peony varieties smell lovely too. A few shrines are covered with flowers. At Ueno Onsje park we walked along the river canal, stopping at ‘Buzz garden restaurant’ for lunch. They have a lovely spot marked ‘for dogs only’ which is nice for those with hounds. Then the time came to take my leave of my precious boy. I didn’t have long to wait for my plane to Dubai. My second trip to Japan has been fantastic; I really love the country with all its quirkiness and will miss the cleanliness, politeness and the beauty of the country and its people. Will definitely come back if I can. (Newsflash — I do!)
