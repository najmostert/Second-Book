# Chapter 46: JAPAN 2019

After sorting out the mix-up with my flight—and spending a bizarre night in a capsule hotel—I finally made it to Tokyo to reunite with Tim. The city felt wonderfully familiar with its narrow, busy streets, tangle of telephone wires, and tiny, leaning apartments. We stopped at a take-away coffee nook and sat under a bougainvillea bush to sip our coffee, before enjoying a traditional ramen meal of wheat noodles in a soy-and-miso broth, served overlooking the Nomikawa river, where the sakura (cherry blossoms) shimmered in shades of pink. In the Shinagawa Kamiosaki area, we found a cosy, compact pub. With the help of a friendly local named Jun, we braved a menu that was entirely in Japanese. I was pleasantly surprised to find that the raw chicken wing, slimy oyster bits, and raw fish I’d nervously ordered were actually quite tasty! We toasted the night with Sapporo beer and sake, served in the traditional style with a splash spilling over the side. 

Shinjuku and Urban Adventures 

Shinjuku was alive with neon lights and restaurants. We stopped for bubble tea—taro flavour for me—and visited a purikura shop, where we had fun in a photo booth, decorating our prints with "kawaii" (cute) effects. Later, at a VR gaming centre, we strapped on headsets to "punch" each other into oblivion. I lost badly, flailing my arms wildly, but it was hilarious to watch the replay later in the public area. We ended the night in a tiny karaoke bar, belting out English songs in a private booth and laughing at our terrible attempts. 

Akihabara and Beyond 

Akihabara was a whirlwind of anime culture. We visited a "Maid Café," Maid Dreaming, where the room was a sea of frothy pink lace. Our maid served our coffee with an earnest song and dance, complete with curtsies and hand-over-heart gestures. It was totally weird, but undeniably sweet! In a huge multi-level collectables store, Tim bought me a red-haired figurine of a "nurse" for my upcoming birthday. We enjoyed the beauty of Dempo-in Temple, where paper lanterns cast a golden glow against the cherry blossoms. One morning, we found a "garage" café in Minami-rokugo called Buckle, where the owner served us amazing coffee. A local patron told us about the annual Penis Festival (Kanamari Matsuri) happening that day. He shared the legend of a demon who hid in a woman’s "flower garden" and bit off her husband’s equipment, only to have its teeth shattered by a steel phallus crafted by a local blacksmith. It sounded like an unforgettable event, but unfortunately, it was in the opposite direction of our plans. 

Shibuya Crossing and Hachikō 

In Shibuya, we visited the famous statue of Hachikō, the Akita dog who famously waited at the train station for his deceased owner every day for nine years. I stroked his cool copper nose, touched by such profound canine devotion. We then braved the Shibuya Crossing—the busiest intersection in the world—before ducking into a Marvel Comic store, where we spent ages admiring the massive collection of anime and comic characters. Tokyo is a place where the deeply traditional and the wonderfully bizarre coexist perfectly, and I’ve been loving every second of it. We enjoyed the warm rays of the sun in a large department store, where a Peppa Pig robot greeted us with a repetitive welcome, mostly ignoring my silly questions! We walked to Yoyogi Park, a beautiful space filled with flamboyant sakura (cherry blossoms). It was a people-watcher’s paradise: a guy with bright orange hair and massive white wings, girls in maid uniforms, and a troupe of 1950s-style dancers jiving to Elvis songs. We even spotted a man who had passed out at the edge of the pond, his hand dangling in the water to "test the temperature." On our last night in Tokyo, it poured with rain. We ran to a restaurant in Roppongi—marked by a humongous metal spider exhibit outside—and enjoyed a warm Italian meal, where the waiter was delighted when I ordered the Japanese Maruki red wine (mostly because it was the most affordable option!). 

Nagano and the Snow Monkeys 

We headed for Nagoya, but in a classic travel mishap, we ended up at the wrong station, closer to the sea. We quickly hopped on a Shinkansen to Nagano instead, where our host, Takafumi, picked us up. He lived near the cold, crisp mountains in a traditional house with tatami mats and rice-paper walls. We visited a local onsen, stripping down to bathe in hot, salty mountain water before cooling off in an outdoor pool under a crescent moon. The next morning, we woke up to a magical surprise: the ground was covered in a fresh layer of snow. Takafumi drove us to the Jigokudani Snow Monkey Park. The walk through snow-laden trees and silver streams was delightful. When we reached the hot spring, we saw the macaques lounging in the steam, looking for all the world like ‘gin-swilling Englishmen,’ with their red faces and soaking fur. It was one of the highlights of the trip. 

Kanazawa We took the Shinkansen to Kanazawa, a city of cherry blossoms and neat gardens. We stumbled into a restaurant where the menu, translated by Tim's phone, provided endless entertainment. We ended up ordering ‘Cows skirt’ and ‘Chicken of the fried village,’ praying there were no ‘configurable gizzards’ in our meal. We explored Nagamachi, the old samurai district, where we learned about the austere lives of the samurai and the Ashigaru (foot soldiers). It was fascinating to see the Nomura family’s residence and learn about the Chugen—the servants who carried their master’s swords and food. The nearby Kanazawa Castle Park was equally beautiful, with its glittering ponds and flowering plants. 

Hiroshima 

After a final tea, we set off for the station to begin our journey to Hiroshima. We took a Shinkansen to our first stop and then transferred to the ‘Thunderbird’—a limited express train—to continue our trek through Japan. Hiroshima awaited, a city with a heavy, moving history that we were ready to learn more about as we left the cherry blossoms of the north behind. Our journey from Tokyo to Hiroshima took us through a whirlwind of experiences. After a crowded Shinkansen train ride—where I befriended a delightful nine-year-old named Lily—we arrived at our "capsule" hotel. I was initially nervous about my claustrophobia, but the curtained entrance made it feel manageable. We spent the day at the Peace Memorial Park, reflecting at the A-Bomb Dome, and ended the evening with Hiroshima-style okonomiyaki—a delicious, crispy cabbage-and-bacon pancake cooked on a giant griddle before our eyes which we eat while a small black and white TV flickers overhead. 

MIYAJIMA ISLAND 

I wake up with a full bladder, pull back the curtain and eye the short, narrow steps warily. I'm on the third level of capsules and it will be a precarious descent. Shuffling forward on my bottom, I make my way to the entrance, swing my legs out, and straight away come into contact with something soft and hairy. I withdraw my feet quickly and peer down. A young man with a very healthy head of hair is glaring up at me. ‘Sumemasen!’ I say apologising for my unintentional foot-to-head contact and wait until he shuffles down the narrow corridor towards the communal bathrooms before attempting to follow suit. This time narrowly missing Tim's head which is poking its way through his curtain on the first level. The next morning, I survived a precarious descent from my third-level capsule without any further foot-to-head contact with my neighbours. We took the ferry to Miyajima Island famous for its red torii gate partially submerged in the sea. The island was a picturesque dream of cherry blossoms, roaming Nihonjika deer, and winding forest paths. We climbed up to the Daisho-in temple complex, a sprawling site founded in 806. We ended the day back on the mainland with Fuchu-yaki (okonomiyaki with soba and minced meat) and a little sake before returning to the bustling capsule hotel. 

Osaka 

In Osaka, a drizzling Sunday greeted us. We visited the world-renowned Kaiyukan Aquarium, which was incredibly spectacular but, due to the weekend crowds, felt a bit claustrophobic. We stayed in a room so "tight-ee" that poor Tim barely fit! We spent time exploring Osaka Castle and browsing the Design Pocket shop, where I couldn't resist buying some sampuru (wax-moulded food items)—specifically a fried-egg headband and mackerel earrings. I wore them to supper in the Dōtonbori district, embracing the freedom of eccentricity; it’s the only place on earth where a fried egg on your head is just another Tuesday night! 

Kyoto 

Kyoto felt deeply traditional, with women in exquisite kimonos everywhere. We climbed the steep, seemingly endless steps of the Fushimi Inari Shrine, passing through thousands of vibrant red torii gates. We didn't spot any bears, but we did find the "Root-up Pine Tree," which is said to bless your lower torso if you walk under its roots! In the Gion district, we watched geisha daintily navigate the narrow streets on their elevated wooden geta sandals. We also visited the famous Arashiyama Bamboo Forest and the Tenryū-ji temple, a World Heritage site with a garden that has survived in its original form since 1339. After leaving our luggage locked up, we head for the subway en route to the ferry for the ten-minute ride across the bay to the island of Miyajima and the famous red torii gate that is partially submerged in the sea. It’s a really pretty island with numerous ‘Nihonjika’ (Japanese deer), cherry blossoms, and red-flowered trees. Many shrines and a row of shops sit along the length of the beach. As usual, Tim’s translation app provided the evening's entertainment. At a small side-road restaurant, the menu offered "Whimsical fried rice," "Homemade bacon and child," and my personal favourite, "Stir-fried Wikipedia!”

We make the difficult decision to go with the item devoid of whimsy, children, mermaids, or flies and have a chicken and rice dish, which is a reasonably tasty, if safe, dish. A pamphlet at the counter asks the question, ‘What is sausage?’ but fails to provide a satisfactory answer, so we remain ignorant of any of the lesser known characteristics and meander slowly back to the cabbage room.
I don my fried-egg headband and kipper earrings this morning and we set off for Himeji via Kobe. It’s another lovely, balmy day as we walk up to Himeji Park and Castle. Sakura abounds and the park wears its pink mantle with pride. Carpets of fallen pink petals make for a fairy wonderland. Every blast of wind shakes the trees of their dancing petals and they glide to the ground like floral snow.
Himeji Castle earned its name, "White Heron Castle," due to its white plaster walls and appearance, which resemble a white heron taking flight. The castle dates back to the 13th century. Himeji Castle has seen its fair share of disasters. It was burned down during a war in 158. The tower and main building were rebuilt, after which it was abandoned, then lost to a large fire and in 193, the government started raising funds for reconstruction, going as far as selling clay to raise money. The rooftop of the castle was blown away by a typhoon in 1954 but continuing with it’s misfortune, was later reconstructed with wood that burned down completely. This unfortunate castle was registered as one of the first UNESCO World Heritage Sites in December 1993. 
We walk through the outer building first—gleaming wooden floors and windows and come to the rooms where the princess used to sit. There are some rather steep steps between the rooms. Outside, the red-leafed trees clash beautifully against the bright pink cherry blossoms. We exit  the outer building and head for the main part of the castle. The Himeji castle grounds are adorned with more than 1800 cherry blossom trees, planted around the main keep, walls, and moat. I later learn that the castle was featured in the 1967 James Bond film, “You Only Live Twice.” 
On the train to Osaka I’m fascinated by a couple of young girls who spend the entire journey doing up each other's faces. Eyebrows are carefully drawn in, then an intricate procedure involving tape is used to obtain a double-eyelid look. As one girl is transformed, the other is held to equal scrutiny and receives a generous amount of face cream.
We stop off at Kobe to find a restaurant to sample the famous Kobe beef. What a pretty town—especially at night. Buildings, trees and bushes shine and twinkle everywhere. A huge, gleaming white Ferris wheel adds to the festive atmosphere. A restaurant in Higashikawaskicho overlooks the river and a full moon glimmers in the sky. The restaurant is Brazilian and serves what they call a ‘meat buffet’. Throughout the meal, waiters come up with yet another type of meat and carve thin slices to add to our plates and are delicious and tender.
Kobe beef is a specific type of Japanese Wagyu beef that comes from the Tajima strain of Japanese Black cattle raised in the Hyōgo Prefecture. Dessert really pushes us way past satiety but is a really worthwhile experience. Afterwards, we waddle out of the restaurant and walk along the glittering pathways back to the train station.
NARA ISLAND
I’m not sorry to be leaving the ‘cabbage dump' but have really enjoyed our time here. Today, we set off for Nara and discover that the stations have lockers available for hourly rent so pay for two. My large suitcase will not fit no matter how much I try, so I just leave it there in the hope the country continues to show how safe and honourable its people have been so far (It does, and on our return four hours later, my suitcase has been lifted safely to the top of the lockers and remains intact). Owl Cafe en route to the park looks inviting with its cosy interior so we pop in for a coffee and an owl pat. It is a bit sad really, seeing all the owls sitting quietly (or so we surmise) at various points in the long room that serves as a cafe and observation area. They have quite a selection of owls, from the snowy owl to the huge eagle owl. The owl assistant takes photos of us holding some owls on a huge leather glove that is provided for the purpose. I get my name printed in Japanese, and together with two owl key rings, we leave the cafe and set off for Kōfuku-ji Temple and Nara Park. The deer are quite prolific here and walk in pairs or small groups all over the park. Various locals, seeing an opportunity to make some money, have set up simple stalls selling biscuits for the deer. This means the deers know that every visitor is a potential biscuit provider. I witness a woman bowing to a deer and the deer bowing back before receiving a treat. This Pavlovian response makes for some cute photos. I wonder about the nutritional value of the biscuits and find out they are called Shika-senbei and are made from rice bran and grain. Some of the deer appear a bit moth-eaten, but that could be a seasonal thing. From the park we walk a short distance to Tōdai-ji Temple to see the largest statue of Buddha in Japan. The Daibutsu (Great Buddha) is housed within the temple. Completed in 752, the statue stands 15 metres (49 ft) tall. It’s really huge as are the accessory statues. The big guy is made of bronze, so he really shines in the light of the day. A Shinkansen from Nara to Fuji is next. We change trains in Yokohama to Odawara and have some really fantastic sights of Mt Fuji from the train window.
HAKONE
After a very comfortable night we set off for Hakone. Anyone imagining tatami mats uncomfortable, couldn’t be more wrong. They’re typically made with a core of compressed rice straw, covered with woven rush (igusa) straw. The edges of the mats are often reinforced with fabric borders called tatami-beri. Contemporary tatami mats might be made of compressed wood chip board or polystyrene foam for the core, instead of rice straw. In any case I slept very comfortably on mine whatever the grain.
The Lalique Museum, in Hakone, owns a collection of more than 1,500 of Lalique's pieces, including over 200 precious jewellery items. The small garden area is also very pretty with a large mirror pond which reflects some cherry blossoms in it’s mercurial stillness. We walk to the harbour and board one of the large wooden ships for the Hakone Ropeway. These wooden ferries are replicas of old pirate ships, decorated with gold paint with wooden statues attached to the hull. Tim finds a good spot for viewing near the top. The trip across to the gardens is only ten minutes long, but provides a lovely view of Lake Ashi. On the other side we wander around the Hakone gardens and climb some steep steps to a very pretty avenue of cypress trees. The view from the top is breathtaking with cherry blossoms wherever the eye can see. A local bus takes us to the open-air museum. Huge exhibits are dotted around a large manicured garden and are absolutely stunning. There are statues of copper and steel, stone, ceramics with gold faces, a huge white head reclining in a pond with a small green bush serving as hair. Huge climbing frames, pipes, glass and chrome structures sit majestically along the open garden path. I really enjoy the artwork and the entire experience. It takes us a good two hours to look at everything. They also have a few of Picasso’s earlier works as well as a couple of his newer paintings.
Mt FUJI
My last full night and day here in Japan and I'm feeling really sad about that. We leave all our luggage behind the washing line outside the room as we have to check out of our room (only in Japan can you do this and find everything as you left it on your return).
The ‘ropeway’ cable car takes us up to the best view of the famous mountain. It slowly rises to 1000 meters, so you can enjoy the view en route. It is surprisingly warm at the top given the elevation. And as I turn round, in full view, is Mount Fuji in all her splendour. She wears a small hat of fluffy clouds around her brim, which also serve as fluffy slippers at her feet. Many photos later, we wander around and purchase a small packet of blackened, sulphur-steamed eggs. Clouds of steam dot the rocky surface of the green sulphur mountain top. The black eggs are said to increase your life by seven years; although difficult to prove, many people are strolling round munching their boiled eggs with vigour, buoyed in the knowledge they have ensured a longer lifespan. Tim and I eat ours sitting at the top of the stairs with a great view of Fuji before taking the cable car back down with a group of American tourists.  After walking around Hakone for a bit, we head back to the washing line to fetch our luggage. 
From here we take the next train back into Tokyo where we wait for our new host to fetch us from the station. A middle aged man arrives in his car and takes us to his traditional ryokan accommodation. Our room has the expected tatami mats, low wooden table and bamboo walls and shutters. I boil some water for green tea before meeting his son and wife who tells us in halting English that she has travelled a lot- mostly with her small terrier. The owner’s wife makes us toasted muffin bread, eggs, bacon and great coffee this morning. There’s an Italian couple staying here as well but they don’t talk much to each other or to us. Tim and I have a good chat with the owner before setting off for Reno park. There’s a man playing the pan flutes along the park pathway. It sounds like Peruvian music. A sweet old Japanese lady is dancing slowly and gracefully to his music, her eyes are closed and her arms reach out as if to feel the memories of her youth.
Reno Toshogo Peony park is full of the flowers of its name. There are so many varieties, sizes and colours! A feast for the eyes. Coloured umbrellas are dotted along the paths. Some of the peony varieties smell lovely too. A few shrines are covered with flowers. At Ueno Onsje park we walk along the river canal, stopping at ‘Buzz garden restaurant’  for lunch. They have a lovely spot marked ‘for dogs only’ which is nice for those with hounds. Then the time comes to take my leave of my precious boy. I don’t have long to wait for my plane to Dubai. My second trip to Japan has been fantastic; I really love the country with all its quirkiness and will miss the cleanliness, politeness and the beauty of the country and its people. Will definitely come back if I can. ( Newsflash- I do!)
