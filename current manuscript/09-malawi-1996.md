# Chapter 8: Malawi 1996

Malawi is a landlocked country in southeastern Africa, defined by its highlands, split dramatically by the Great Rift Valley and its enormous lake. We’re heading there for a few weeks of exploration. I was glad Nanny had agreed to stay in our house while we were away as two weeks is a long time for a property to stand empty. There was a prominent ‘Fierce Dog’ notice hanging on our front gate and I joked that Nanny could always bark if the need arose. Col, having embarked on a prophylactic course of Lariam—or ‘delirium’ as we preferred to call the anti-malaria medication—woke up in the early hours of the morning convinced that a wild boar was standing directly over our bed. It took me several minutes of bleary-eyed, reassurance to convince him that the beast wasn't actually there. As Col insisted on taking the wheel this morning, I asked him to inform me timeously should any such apparitions appear to him on the road. It looked like it was going to be a very long journey. Did I say a long journey? Perhaps I meant a short night, consisting of about three hours of sleep. After finally arriving in Winburg at midnight, Col had to ask for directions to the Winburg Hotel. By the time we located the building, the staff had all gone to bed. While the security guard went off to rouse the lady of the house I’d hastily shoved Tim behind a large potted plant in the lobby as he didn't have any shoes on, his shorts were visibly torn and he had red dust caked all over his left leg. I was absolutely not about to forgo a warm bed because the hotel’s ‘rights of admission’ were reserved. The lady in question turned out to be very nice — or perhaps local business was slow. In any event she kindly offered us a pot of tea along with the promise of a room. We refused the beverage, took the key and managed to sleepwalk Tim up the stairs and straight into bed. Col and I were overtired. We lay in our comfortable bed, arms propped behind our heads, staring companionably up at the high ceiling and chatted about what we were going to do once we reached Malawi. Col had visited the country many years prior and said it was a fantastic holiday destination. He was keen to dive in Lake Malawi and photograph the cichlids—the hundreds of species of uniquely colourful freshwater fish that reside in the lake ecosystem.

The next day at the airport terminal, they called our flight for boarding at the exact same moment Col decided he desperately needed a new collared shirt for the trip (I had completely forgotten to pack one—so sue me). He announced that he needed a good pair of walking shoes as well and dashed into a Nike store after purchasing an expensive blue shirt at three times the price he would normally pay. I decided to join him. Clutching four pieces of hand luggage and holding Tim’s hand, I strolled casually towards the shop at the far end of the terminal to meet Col. His first words to me were, ‘Where’s my camera bag?’ I felt the welcoming smile instantly leave my face as I simultaneously dropped all the luggage on the floor, turned around and sprinted back to the row of seats we had vacated minutes before. Sod’s Law saw fit to let me off lightly this time and I located the heavy camera bag nestled safely among the potted plants where Col had left it ‘in my care’. Dripping with sweat and filled with foreboding—if this was how our holiday was to commence?—I rejoined a thoroughly relieved Col and we marched towards the departure gate just as the final call for our flight to Malawi echoed over the loudspeakers. Boarding the Air Malawi aircraft was another classic experience in ‘travel moments one can do without’. A buxom, officious-looking woman stood squarely at the entrance to the plane wearing a tight blue uniform that stretched precariously over her ample proportions. She was looking intently at the dimensions of everyone’s hand luggage as they struggled up the rickety mobile stairs towards the cabin door. Right in front of us, a large gentleman had his suitcase aggressively removed from his hands. 
‘This is much too large for hand luggage,’ she stated flatly. ‘We will have it placed in the hold immediately.’ Col slipped quickly behind my back and in the general melee of passengers all trying to force their way on board at the same time, she failed to observe that we were carrying the equivalent of our combined body weight as hand luggage. We sat down thankfully in our allocated seats and breathed a collective sigh of relief. Tim and Col had originally been allocated seats across the central aisle, but a kind lady travelling from Nairobi offered to swap with Tim so he could sit next to the window. We kept dodging heads of other passengers as I tried to converse with Col across the row, so eventually, the passenger sitting next to me indicated with a curt wave of his hand that we should simply swap seats with the lady. We had just completed this seat reshuffle and our safety belts were firmly clipped into position when we were approached by a beaming air hostess who had clearly been to ‘smile school’. She politely asked us to move again. An American family seated in front of us had a young boy and civil aviation regulations dictated that passengers with young children were strictly prohibited from sitting next to the emergency exit rows. So, like a game of musical chairs in slow motion, we changed seats yet again. The air hostesses assigned to our section—poverty class— reluctantly and with incredibly poor grace served anyone rash enough to ask for a second beer or extra snacks, with nary a welcoming smile in sight (she’d definitively not attended smile school, I thought sourly). One unfortunate passenger had a packet of peanuts thrown onto his lap after timidly enquiring if he could have a second bag. As we commenced our final descent, I glanced out of the porthole window to see the long, shimmering blue snake that is Lake Malawi stretching out along the coastline and felt a frisson of excitement. This was the second-deepest lake in Africa, nestled within a tiny country wedged between Zambia, Tanzania and Mozambique. Another young flight hostess was kept busy taking photographs of the passengers—mostly the male ones, I noticed. She really should have spent more time warning us to fasten our safety belts as the actual touchdown on the runway was both sudden and spine-jarring. We landed into a staggering temperature of 36°C and 100% humidity. Col discovered at that exact moment that the Nike shop at the terminal had accidentally sold him £40 worth of matching left shoes which threw him straight into a fume. We ran into an old acquaintance of his at the terminal— Dave; an affable, bearded chap—and chatted to him whilst waiting in the sweltering queue for the small connecting plane from Lilongwe to arrive. The passengers were all standing outside on the hot tarmac, visibly wilting in the unaccustomed heat when suddenly a short, khaki-clad official appeared and pompously ushered us en masse back inside the terminal building again. It seemed the ticket controller wanted a neat queue inside so she could visually inspect our tickets, only to turn around and send us all back onto the tarmac again. Only in Africa, I thought grimly. The connecting plane was a tiny twenty-seater twin-propeller aircraft. We climbed aboard and found some vacant seats right at the back. The lack of cabin assistance was a bit daunting—I wondered silently how many other safety corners were being cut during this regional flight and surreptitiously checked the rivet lines on the wings for signs of structural wear and tear. I chatted nervously to Dave while the crew prepared for take-off. The partition door separating the cockpit from the passenger cabin kept swinging wide open revealing the pilot and co-pilot chatting in desultory fashion. One of them appeared to be casually eating what looked like a cheese and ham sandwich. I had never sat close enough to an active cockpit before to have made such an intimate observation and it unsettled me. A chap sitting behind us located a crate of soda water near his feet and cheerfully handed bottles around to the passengers. I decided to take detailed footage of the entire take-off sequence with my video camera, reasoning if we didn't make it, the authorities would at least have sufficient evidence of the pilot’s incompetence after our collective demise.
The aerial view of the lake was magnificent as we swooped low towards the runway. We landed smoothly enough and descended from the aircraft some twenty minutes later. The humidity in the air hit our lungs like a warm, wet blanket but discover an unexpected, luxurious reception waiting at the edge of the tarmac. Glasses of frosted, ice-cold fruit drinks were immediately thrust into our clammy hands and traditional straw boaters decorated with fresh bougainvillea flowers placed upon our heads. I suspected that we had inadvertently joined the official Club Makokola welcoming committee line and this suspicion was confirmed with the sudden arrival of Oliver, the co-manager of Chembe Lodge —the rustic camp where we were actually spending the next ten days. Our fancy hats and cold glasses were quickly and unceremoniously removed by the resort staff the moment they realised our mistake and I was left nibbling the slice of decorative orange. Oliver was Australian—short, blonde, deeply tanned with a capable air about him. He shook hands firmly before leading our group towards his white Toyota van. We were joined by four other passengers on the bumpy ride to the edge of Lake Malawi. The other couple were Afrikaans-speaking; the boss and his secretary in the middle of a heavy, illicit affair, we surmised (without much evidence it must be admitted other than entirely different surnames paired with a number of shared, intimate looks of the sort that rapidly disappear the moment children are introduced to a marriage), alongside two single men carrying large backpacks. After bumping our way over gargantuan potholes, the vehicle came to an abrupt stop at a roadside curio stand somewhere off the beaten track. Instantly the van was surrounded by local merchants displaying their wares and within minutes of intense bartering, I was the proud owner of two carved wooden apples, five beaded bracelets and a matching necklace—all for the price of an exceptionally lurid pair of pink trousers and a sequinned top. I always loved a good barter and the procedure had been both rapid and intense. In the sweltering heat I felt at times as though I were physically drowning in a humid soup of faces and thrust-out hands. Several small woven toy vehicles—different makes of cars and buses carefully constructed from local raffia—were offered for sale. They were beautifully made but Col, who felt it was too early in the holiday to be spending cash said, ‘Not today, sorry,’ several times before the hawkers turned their attention to Tim. Tim, of course, turned on his charm and Daddy simply couldn't resist. He purchased a cleverly constructed raffia Toyota 4x4. Tim was absolutely delighted. Oliver drove onwards until we finally reached the camp, chatting occasionally and throwing out snippets of geographical information regarding the Malawian countryside. It was now pitch dark and still incredibly humid. We were unprepared for the dense swarms of mosquitoes that greeted us the minute the van stopped and hoped that Tim had not been bitten. The camp was built right on the water's edge of the southern portion of the lake—a massive, long body of fresh water that runs almost the entire length of Malawi. There were a number of large granite rocks and boulders dotted along the shoreline. 
The Malawian Kwacha is officially divided into 100 Tambala. The word Kwacha translates literally to ‘chicken’, as it was historically considered that a certain number of chickens’ worth of trade value constituted a Kwacha. We were to part with a lot of chickens in the days to come.
Our accommodation comprised traditional safari tents set up around the clearing on the hard sand. I felt a bit ratty by this stage especially when discovering that the lodge took the term ‘rustic’ a bit too literally. I could find no soap, no towels, no insect spray and no mosquito net in the tent which necessitated a frantic, sweating search by candlelight to locate our own supplies in the depths of the luggage. I stood under a cold shower to cool off—no towel was actually needed as the intense heat meant I dried in about five minutes anyway. A sudden, rhythmic drumming announced that supper was served. The nearby thatch-roofed verandah featured several wooden benches and long trestle tables where our food awaited. It was so stiflingly hot that we didn’t eat much and Tim refused to touch anything at all. Col chatted to Oliver about the possibilities of a scuba dive. The following morning we arrived expectantly to the dining table under the thatch and asked the waiter for the breakfast menu. This request was met with a slightly puzzled expression by the waiter-cum-drum-beater who simply asked us how we wanted our eggs. It turned out that eggs were the only item, aside from rubbery toast, on the menu— no butter provided so the toast had to be eaten dry. They also didn't possess any filtered drinking water leaving us with a daily breakfast choice of hot tea, Fanta, Coca-Cola, or Sprite—or dying of thirst. We opted for the tea and a Coke. I dislike Coca-Cola but thirst won over personal preference. Col later walked down to the local fishing village about a kilometre away to secure a crate of bottled sodas. He lugged them back to camp and stored them safely under our bed. By 10 a.m. I felt as though I had already been awake for an entire day then Tim complained too feeling cold and wanted a heavy blanket, even though it was 37°C in the shade. It could only mean one thing of course so I took his temperature and sure enough, it was running high. I looked around the camp for Col but he had already left on a boat dive. I remembered meeting a rather irritating medical doctor at breakfast who had bored the shirt off everyone at the table with endless tales of his emergency casualty exploits. I tracked him down on the beach where he was regaling a fresh, impressionable group of bikini-clad women with his medical heroism. I asked him if he would write me a standard prescription for an antibiotic for Tim, but he flatly refused, stating that his American medical script would not be legally recognised in Malawi. Useless bugger. Whilst I was explaining the situation to Col an hour later outside our tent, I saw a long, bright green Cape grass snake draping itself sinuously over our canvas tent flap. It was a true mark of how the oppressive humidity and general exhaustion had addled my brain, as I merely pointed it out to Col and continued the conversation without pause. 
I discovered this morning that the kitchen staff had finally brought in a fresh supply of bottled water and immediately purchased a few two-litre bottles. The stuff was liquid gold around camp—I would sooner have left my expensive video camera unattended on a bench than leave our precious water bottle out of my sight. Ah, they’re playing our song again. ‘Hello Safaris’ possessed exactly one African cassette tape which the bar staff played over and over again on a continuous loop. One particular song amused me (when it wasn’t driving me insane); it featured a chorus sung in English that went something like: ‘Hol’ me in your yams, baby, yeah, yeah...’ repeated five times in succession. Col arrived back from Otter Point.The dive had gone splendidly and he’d utilised two full rolls of film and his legs were quite sunburned. Tim enthusiastically banged the camp drums tonight to announce dinner was served and he enjoyed a little dance with the waiter behind the bar; I think he was finally enjoying himself more now that his fever had broken. Supper consisted of fresh Chambo fish and chips. Tim and I secured a lift from Alison—otherwise known as ‘Haircut’, the guy who worked behind the bar—riding in the back of his beat-up old truck. We’d nicknamed him Haircut because he sported a very strange, intricate hairstyle that was shorn completely bald around his ears but stuck up like a thick, rigid hairnet across the top of his skull. The fact that his actual name was Alison should have been strange enough though. It was fascinating travelling through the rural village with its mud-and-wattle huts and scatterings of chickens scratching the dirt paths. Haircut appeared to be the main man-about-town; he made several stops along the track where a certain amount of intense negotiating took place. Negotiating about what exactly we never learned, but we were reasonably certain it wasn't legal. There were a number of beautiful, crimson flamboyant trees dotted around the village. A gentleman named Mr Stevens had originally started the very first lodge at Cape Maclear back in the 1970s. It was aptly named ‘Mr Stevens' Guest House’, or simply ‘Stevens’. He was a visionary who saw that Cape Maclear possessed immense potential for international tourism rather than remaining an undeveloped fishing village. He was personally responsible for establishing the first lodge, the first local taxi service (transferring overland backpackers between here and Monkey Bay) and the first boat taxi, thereby single-handedly encouraging tourism to the region. Golden Sands was another quaint beach camp we passed. It featured many broken and disused brick buildings, towering palm trees and pristine white sands. A troop of chacma baboons had taken up permanent residence in the nearby trees and we watched their antics for a while before purchasing a Fanta at the tiny kiosk store. The shop window was dusty and bare, save for a pair of tiny, rolled-up red curtains hanging inside. A green wooden dartboard on the wall bore the legend Malawi's Best Beer in faded gold lettering though there was none available for us to sample. Tim proudly showed the man behind the counter his plastic toy dinosaur, stating firmly, ‘Dinosaur.’ The man stared at the small plastic monster and declared it to be a chameleon. A brief argument ensued with Tim emerging the victor by default; he firmly declared the creature to be a ‘Pterosaurus’ and marched triumphantly out of the store before his taxonomy could be disputed again. We walked in the intense midday heat towards Otter Point, eventually arriving at a small, secluded cove flanked by granite rocks and the most beautiful, glittering, crystal-clear pool of water. The fish were visible through the turquoise surface of the lake and I was amazed by the sheer variety of colours. They were indigenous cichlids, some featuring incredibly elaborate patterns. Tim was led safely into the shallow water’s edge by Col, to feed the fish dry biscuit crumbs which they attacked with a voracious appetite. I tried on the diving goggles and leaned forward to immerse my face in the water, presenting an interesting picture as I balanced precariously over the jagged rocks. Col thought about photographing my perching posterior but wisely decided against it. It was far too hot to remain exposed for longer than an hour, so we made our way back to the campsite where we purchased more sugary sodas. I passed the hot afternoon slowly, gulping down the odd mopane bee and lethargically swatting at them in the stifling heat. I later engaged in a long conversation with one of the local villagers. By the close of chat, he had formally promised us accommodation ‘anytime’ with his grandmother and her chickens. Food would be fully provided—specifically banana loaf at 20 Kwacha a slice—and any amount of wilderness activities would be personally arranged, he concluded. I agreed to let him know should any of the above arrangements become necessary. 
We waited for Wayne to arrive at the appointed hour and fervently hoped he wasn't operating on ‘Africa time’. Wayne was a bit laid-back; in fact, he was damn near horizontal at times. At 3:25 p.m. his inflatable rubber dinghy finally came into view—and why does the word ‘rubber’ always sound so inherently unsafe when applied to a boat? Wayne greeted us with a cheerful wave and soon we were all on board with his assistant and zooming off across the water towards ‘The Aquarium’, so-called because of its crystal-clear depths. Shortly after dropping anchor over the dive site, Wayne casually informed us that two days prior, some individual had removed a vital piece of the boat’s rubber drainage plug. He added, not very reassuringly, that the hull currently possessed a slow, continuous leak and they were lucky they hadn't all drowned on the crossing. I quickly asked him if the problem had been fixed before we left the beach. 
‘Oh, no, not yet anyhow,’ he replied vaguely. He looked at the horizon as if to discover the reason for this dilatoriness, then promptly dived into the water to join Col, leaving me, Tim, and the local boatman bobbing nervously up and down on this inflatable toy while the water level inside rose at a steady rate. I observed the rising level quietly for some time before pointing it out to the assistant. He looked mildly interested; no, he hadn't noticed that the boat was actively sinking. Did I happen to know what he could do to help the situation? ‘Um, we could use this’ I suggested offering him a plastic mug. The offer was met with an alacrity that was far from reassuring. Thus passed the hour with our vessel sinking slowly into the lake, Tim happily sitting in the pilot's driving seat and the local frantically bailing out cupfuls of water over the side. Just as my panic level was nearing boiling point, Wayne and Col finally resurfaced. Wayne, shaking water all over my video camera lens, announced cheerfully that he would allow Tim to steer us back to camp and not to worry about the leak. My smile was somewhat fixed by now and, as Tim’s erratic driving increased, so did my anxiety levels. We were almost submerged in water by the time we made it safely back to the shore. Shakily, I clambered out of the dinghy and resisted a powerful urge to simultaneously bend down, kiss terra firma and box Wayne’s ears. The intense heat which had been greatly diminished while out in the centre of the open lake, increased tenfold the moment we hit the sand and sweated our way back for a cold shower and a couple of beers—they had run out of bottled water again. I think we may have been a bit salt-depleted from sweat. I tried to secure some salt from the kitchen to eat with the ubiquitous boiled egg that constituted our daily breakfast. 
A huge sandstorm broke out this afternoon. The water was choppy, the clouds formed huge grey mounds above us and chairs were blowing around. I was sure this heralded a storm and awaited the subsequent downpour eagerly. It was a pleasant evening before going off to the hot, sticky, airless tent. The blasted chicken with laryngitis crowed us awake at 2:30, and I could have cheerfully wrung its neck if I’d had the energy. Oliver wanted us gone by mid-morning so I finished packing and dropped Tim in the lake (not literally, of course)—he was warm again, so I slipped half an aspirin into his juice. I was just about to lean under the bed for a soda when a mouse stopped me in my tracks. I gave my usual ‘Oooh’ cry and stepped back onto Col’s tender toes. He fell against the camp bed, yelling and it collapsed. A real comedy team. We set off for Club Makokola in Oliver’s van, dodging huge potholes—in fact, the roads here were mainly giant potholes of murram with the odd bit of tar holding them together. Oliver suddenly swerved to miss another deep dip in the road and nearly knocked a man off his bicycle in the process. We arrived at the pretty campsite of Club Makokola and met up with Dave before heading for the airport. 
After a body search involving gloves and a lot of ineffectual prodding by a hefty female security guard, we boarded Air Malawi. They only had three planes in Air ‘Where-are-we’, so I think we must have been on all three by now. Tim sat next to Dave who kindly gave him his breakfast. He’d been staying at a nicer camp that actually fed its guests a proper breakfast and they also had a great supply of drinking water according to Dave. Col hired a car at the airport. The chassis seemed low for the roads here. Both of us were feeling a bit queasy, so we stopped at a coffee shop for some tea. (Shouldn’t that be, we stopped at a tea shop for some coffee?) It came weak and milky, just how I dislike it. We were going to do some shopping then head for the Zomba Plateau, approximately 250 km away. The plateau, also known as the Zomba Massif, is a mountain of the Shire Highlands in southern Malawi. It was now 10 a.m. and we were waiting for our brunch. The first request was met with a laconic, ‘Sorry, there are no chips, no bread, no lunch. On the menu’ But, the waiter added (pencil and pad at the ready), we might order toasted sandwiches. We tried to work that one out then told him we’d also like juice. He said there’s a bottle of Mazoe orange concentrate in the bar, but it was locked up and they couldn't find the key; he dashed off to try and find one. Col’s food arrived. They spent twenty minutes apologising because there were no chips. He said it was OK; it really didn’t matter. They apologised again. Then the waiter appeared ten minutes later with a plate of toasted buns and apologised because they had no bread. We were happy to accept the buns as being another form of bread, so Col repeated that it didn’t matter, slightly alarmed at the anxiety an apparent lack of ingredients was causing the staff. We ate up. Forty-five minutes later, Col received a plate of toasted cheese AND chips. We had given up trying to fathom the availability of food that was both available and not. A sort of Schrödinger’s meal, if you will. We set off in the intense heat and Tim started boiling up again. I had popped an analgesic in his Coke this morning but with the relentless heat and dust he just got worse. He vomited twenty minutes outside of town all over the back seat of the hired car, so we tried to find a place to clean him up. Eventually, stopping outside a mud hut and asked an old man who was sitting there if we could have some amanzi (water). He fetched me a cracked basin and carefully filled it with water from a jug. I gabbled my thanks, explaining that my toto (young child) was sick. I washed Tim and the car seat as best I could with the meagre water and an old T-shirt. I thanked the old man and pressed a few hundred Kwacha into his calloused palm before continuing for the plateau. Tim’s temperature remained obstinately around 40°C. We arrived at the Zomba Plateau at around 4:30 p.m. It was getting dark so didn’t have much time to set up camp at the top of the plateau. The campsite was very basic: an ablution block with a toilet, a shower and an open wood fire pit. We pitched the tent and laid out the basics for supper after cleaning Tim at the tap. I laid him down in the tent and began rushing around trying to make something edible. Tim was getting hotter and by morning I felt hopeless with worry. I told Col we should take him to hospital. As a last desperate measure, Col and I bundled him into the car and drove around the plantation looking for some natural cool forest; most of it was pine, which was disappointing. Thankfully and unexpectedly Tim started improving as he lay across the back seat. We stopped to ask a local man for directions to a lookout area that gave a wonderful view of the valley. He offered to show us himself and hopped in the back; then our self-appointed guide with the unlikely name of ‘Junkness’, proceeded to guide us through the tortuous, dusty roads to the viewpoint. The hired car had such a low chassis that manoeuvring through the thick, dusty potholes was nearly impossible and we got stuck a few times. The view of the Rift Valley was magnificent when we eventually arrived. Junkness jumped out and showed us a deep ravine on the side of the lookout where it was said that Dr Banda had sent various members of the populace whom he had taken a dislike to over the edge. The usual enterprising local was selling crystals to passing tourists. Junkness took us a little way into the forest then we made our way back, crossing Junkness’s palm with a few crumpled notes at the bottom of the road before making our way back to camp. This morning Col disappeared to catch some flutties and I tidied up in the camp and bought some gooseberries from a man wearing sandals made from old car tyres who was passing the camp. He kept the berries in a cracked enamel bowl. I picked up a handful of berries and considered I hadn’t seen many birds yet, though there were supposed to be quite a variety here. Later we set off for the KuChawe Inn, a nearby five-star hotel, but all we wanted was a little lunch in a pleasant setting. We asked if we could change some dollars at the reception. ‘Sorry, we can’t do today,’ was the short reply. After sitting outside on the verandah (red cement floor and Victorian white-painted railings), Col asked the waiter if we could have supper and pay in dollars. The waiter threw up his hands in anguish and disappeared. Perplexed, we waited to see if he would reappear or if we had ruined his day with our request. He returned and said, no deal on the payment in dollars, but we could have supper. We pointed out that they didn’t appear to have cash to give us and they didn’t accept dollars, so we couldn’t pay. He disappeared again and returned a moment later—no deal, according to management. Luckily for us, the table of three Malawian businessmen behind us had been eavesdropping on this exchange and one of the men (a Mr Banda, it turned out) had a quick word or two with the waiter, who disappeared again. He returned beaming—the office was now suddenly open for business and if we would just step this way, we could change some of our dollars to Kwacha. This we did with a quick but sincere thanks to our benefactors. Col changed just enough to see us through the week and we ordered toasted sandwiches.

September the 5th—Gunpowder Plot day elsewhere but thankfully quiet here. We all slept well. Tim was also feeling better so I made a fire for the tea and crushed half an antibiotic into his juice, then fretted about how long I could keep this up as he always finds out. We had a bite to eat—literally, as supplies were running very low —then packed our rucksacks and headed for one of the waterfalls along the forest. It was beautiful here with forest trees and vegetation, as yet untouched by human hand. Tim and I caught some tadpoles in a plastic cup, then played at dropping leaves over the edge of the waterfall; watching them float off down the river. All in all a pleasant way to pass a few hours. Up early next morning to start with the tea-making process in the cool, smoky dawn. I took Tim to see the nearby trout farm. We paid 50 Tambala each and for that we had the privilege of staring into the murky depths of several concrete ponds, looking at trout. This five-minute process entitled us to sign a dusty, well-thumbed exercise book with our comments. I was hard-pressed to come up with anything more original than, ‘The trout were interesting,’ and that was a lie as the water was so murky you could hardly see them. Last night I glimpsed a Livingstone’s lourie as it flew over the low trees at camp. This lourie, or turaco, is a beautiful green, very shy forest bird with a distinctive white-tipped, pointed crest, mostly found in evergreen forests, where it feeds on buds, fruits, and flowers. I noticed my diary pages getting thinner as Col keeps on tearing out pages to make ‘envelopes’ for his butterflies. I have just enough pages left to finish the holiday. Col drove us to Blantyre for supplies. I noticed a hawker selling tomatoes at the side of the road, so we stopped and bought a handful. Suddenly, a runaway tractor came careening up behind us at a tremendous rate of knots. From the look of absolute horror on the driver’s face, it was obvious the brakes had failed. Col had only enough time to release the handbrake and zoom sideways onto the side of the road, squashing three tomatoes and spraying me with pips in the process. We watched with morbid fascination as the tractor zoomed down the hill, picking up as much speed as its weight would allow. It disappeared around a corner. I tried to wipe pips and tomato puree off my face with my T-shirt. Scores of people were riding or pushing their bikes along the road with what seemed like half the forest on their backs—at this rate, there will be no forest left. The law here states that no one may chop down trees unless the wood is dead, so being the inventive people they are, they cut off branches anyway, then burn them and say it’s dead wood if asked. They remind me of termites, always taking bits of wood away from the forest, slowly but surely destroying it. 
A notice at the bank, written in pen and taped to the dusty window informed us that it was not changing money today. Changing money seems to be bent on a whim in Malawi. 

Our friend John Wilson had commented before we left, that camping facilities were scarce. Col had waved them away with, ‘Oh, that won’t be a problem; Jan can handle all that.’ I muttered that he’d probably have me on my hands and knees rubbing flint stones together to make a fire. Col, having taken another Larium last night, had taken to suddenly stopping the car, taking out his wallet whilst muttering, ‘Keep calm, stay calm’ after checking and double-checking that the contents of his wallet were in order. He would then drive on, stop again suddenly and let go of the steering wheel to ‘check the alignment’ before venturing on. Exhausting stuff and I was worried his manic behaviour would worsen with each ‘delirium’ tablet. 

On our way out of Blantyre, we saw a sign outside a petrol station stating in bold: PETROL SERVICE STATION. OPEN 24 HOURS EXCEPT FOR FUEL—Huh? 
As we arrived at one of the huge tea estates that Wilson had told us about, there were rolling, manicured lawns and vast tea bushes as far as the eye could see. Col stopped the car and walked up to the front door of the elaborate house. An old geezer came out and was most unhelpful, uncharacteristically rude, and abrupt (maybe he was on Larium too). He said, no, we couldn’t camp on his estate, we should have booked, he had no houseboy, etc. He suggested we try the next estate before closing the door in Col’s face. We continued driving up a long, winding road, pot-holed as is the norm here, eventually arriving at a farm building and factory with a long, low stone wall surrounding it. Col parked outside under a tree and ambled off hopefully to see if we’d have any better luck in Thyolo. We stopped at the entrance to a large white-walled building where we sought a ‘Rita Maria’ or ‘Maria Rita’—we weren’t sure which, so tried a few variations. In any event she was here; an attractive, slim, dark-haired Italian woman with a ready smile. We sat outside her office on low wooden benches and she offered us tea (would I ever say no to that?). There was a slight problem as we hadn’t actually booked, but Rita was sorting that out. We were so dirty and dusty after weeks of travel that Col sent me to sit in a dark corner so as not to sabotage the booking. Cheeky! His own clothes had not seen laundry detergent in weeks either. One of the farm workers showed us to the bungalow where we were to stay. A huge, single-storey bungalow set in exquisite surroundings with mountains and forest all around. I was thrilled; it was quite a treat after living it rough for the last few weeks to have such luxury to ourselves, even if it was only for the night. We were introduced to Stone, the caretaker-cum-cook. He was an old man with grizzled grey hair but shook our hands firmly and told us he’d been here for years. I was mildly irritated when he followed me around watching my every move, especially when in ‘his’ kitchen. Col dashed off in the car to explore the forest, leaving me to look round (with Stone not far behind), the huge house and grounds. The long, wide verandah opened out over a beautiful garden. There were six bedrooms and several bathrooms in this vast building. We chose the bedroom at the end for ourselves; Tim, despite many choices at his disposal, wanted to sleep in our room. He was only two years old and scary sleeping in a big room by himself, so I made up a mattress on the floor next to our bed. I felt like some tea and wandered into the kitchen, nearly stepping on Stone, who was hovering nearby. He said there was tea in the storeroom for us. He insisted on putting only two teaspoons of tea leaves into the pot but I told him I liked my tea strong and made him put in another two. The result was a brick-red tea, so strong you could stand your spoon straight up in it. Stone just looked amused. It was flavourful, though—and we were in a tea estate, after all. I sat outside on the wide, colonial-style verandah, sipping my brew and writing up my diary. Tim played with his toys nearby, his bottom red from the cement floor polish. Col returned at around 6 p.m. I made supper under the watchful eye of Stone who’d offered to make it himself, but I declined due to our embarrassing lack of provisions. Tim fell asleep outside on the verandah, so I carried him to the mattress in our room. Col and I sat on the verandah steps, chatting and sipping wine. It was very pleasant and we were sorry it was only for one night. It was wonderful to be able to just switch on a kettle again as my fire-making abilities were certainly not up to par. Stone washed up the few dishes and left for the night. I was pleased because I desperately wanted to recharge my camera battery. The only way I could do this was to unscrew the square plug from the rusty old refrigerator and attach it to my camera charger. I left it until the camera was fully charged, then changed the plugs back before Stone could notice.
We were both so glad that Tim was better; the rest of the holiday might now be ‘saved’. It was still difficult trying to get his antibiotic and chloroquine into him twice a day, a procedure that both Tim and I hated in equal measure. I had to write out a cake recipe for Stone this morning. He had some event or other coming up and as chief cook (and bottle-washer), he had been allocated this task. I was entirely the wrong person to ask, as my cakes are legendary—and not in a good way —but I did my best. Col came in at lunchtime having successfully spotted the rare green-headed oriole. Stone helped with lunch while I washed the car again. Lunch consisted of cabbage and bacon bits, washed down with a final cup of ‘Thyolo tea’. We bid farewell to Stone with an exchange of addresses and promises to write. It was sweltering again as we drove to the office to hand back the key before heading slowly towards Liwonde National Park. 
We arrived at Warthogs Wallow that afternoon and were given a traditional rondavel equipped with a fridge, a small kitchenette and two mosquito-netted beds. I noticed that the paintings on the wall were done by the owner of Warthogs Wallow, David Kelly who was married to a frail, pale-looking Scottish woman in a wheelchair. We suspected she might be suffering from multiple sclerosis. His paintings were exceptionally good, mostly depicting Malawian wildlife. They had a sweet little girl of about six years old. Yesterday we had been served by Stone; today Glass pandered to our needs—who knows, perhaps tomorrow Rock would make us breakfast. That afternoon, we embarked on an hour’s boat trip along the Shire River, skippered by Maxton. He had a few bottles of ‘Greens’ (the local Carlsberg beer) and lemonade on board. We saw hippos, crocodiles, elephants, and beautiful malachite kingfishers. According to Maxton, some of the elephants were very uneasy with our presence due to recent poaching in the area. I am always deeply saddened to hear of our beautiful wildlife being endangered in this way. 
I awoke at around 2:30 a.m.to Col moving around frantically in the bed next to me so I peered under his mosquito net to ask what was wrong and received a face-full of pyrethrin spray for my pains. 
‘I’m riddled with mozzie bites’ He snapped. I swigged some water to try and remove the foul taste of the insect spray and settled down again. 
This morning, we set off in the semi-dark towards Lilongwe. Our first roadblock was positioned just outside town. There was a low barrier across the road and several gun-toting, uniformed men flagged us down. One of them had obviously been hitting the ‘Greens’ all night and his overly hearty greeting sent a massive blast of alcoholic fumes through the car window. He interrogated Col on our reasons for travelling and demanded to know where we were from. This line of questioning rapidly became very uncomfortable. He wanted to know why we were driving a Malawi-registered car if we didn’t live in the country. Colin remained calm and polite which was just as well as I was actively restraining myself from ripping the man's head off. After some further questioning he leaned even further into the vehicle, grinning evilly as he sent another alcoholic blast throughout the cabin. ‘Today you are my friends,’ he said and the implication was clear. Col drove off quickly before he could change his mind. 
The packed breakfast we’d received from Glass was the direct result of some careful, intense negotiation. He had been incredibly sullen at first, but after gifting him a kilo of rice, a pair of shoes, some salt and a few rolls of loo paper, his mood transformed. Suddenly, he couldn't do enough for us, wishing us a safe journey and a speedy return. It was amazing, considering that an hour prior, I didn't even know he could speak English. On reflection, I didn't find the locals we encountered to be as friendly as we had been led to believe. They were often rather sullen, in fact, but perhaps the oppressive weather or some underlying political situation was the cause. Economically, a lot changed for the Malawian people after President Banda was overthrown. 
Amusing shop names popped up en route, such as ‘Aunt Chris wait’ and the ‘Last warning Restaurant’. David had told us previously that he once employed a man legally named ‘Serious Mosquito’. We made it to the airport and the usual long, drawn-out process of queuing, paying airport departure tax, declaring photographic equipment and weighing heavy luggage commenced. Suddenly, the tannoy crackled to life—not to call a flight but to request that a Dr Colin ‘Masstert’ come to the enquiry desk to identify his luggage. Inwardly groaning, Col set off to see what the problem was. He returned twenty minutes later and said they’d pointed to our big suitcase and demanded he open it. The tent poles had apparently looked like weapons on the scanner and the small wooden apple carving that contained our tea leaves needed to be physically tasted by officials to prove it wasn't anything illegal or smoke-worthy! I tried my best to look as though my hand luggage weighed only a few pounds when in reality about forty kilos of wooden carvings were digging a permanent, painful groove into my shoulder blade. We boarded our flight on Air ‘Where-are-we’. The pilot joked over the radio that red tape was delaying take-off—I only hoped he didn't mean that literally, though you never really knew with these guys. We were served lunch and Tim ate his share as well which was a wonderful sign that he was fully recovered. We were no sooner handed our tea when a steward suddenly wandered down the aisle, carelessly squirting some foul anti-malarial spray into the atmosphere and directly into my beverage. We landed back in Johannesburg with a distinct bump. Col had to traipse all the way up to the transit lounge to secure permission to exchange his ‘two left feet’ shoes. Meanwhile, I lost my money in a vending machine and nearly strained my wrist violently banging the darn thing. I was discovering itchy mosquito bites I didn't even know I had—one right at the bottom of my eyebrow and another on my bottom. Col eventually fetched our vehicle from the garage, and we began the long, familiar journey home.
