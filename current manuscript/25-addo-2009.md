# Chapter 22: Addo 2009

The three dogs, sensing our impending departure, were getting underfoot or slinking off to lie somewhere (usually a newly planted flower bed) to look miserable. And off we went, stopping off at one of those ‘rest stops’ for the ‘bathroom’ and a Steers burger. I pondered the use of the word bathroom as a coy replacement for toilet. I mean it’s not as though there’s even a bath, and if there were, would travellers suddenly decide to have a bath?

We were on our way to Addo National Elephant Park in the Eastern Cape province, known as South Africa’s third largest park.

We made it to the Wilderness in good time as the ancient old Land Cruiser that we had bought from Saudi was still going well. It still had the distinctive smell of Saudi sand coming through the vents when the engine first started. Tim slotted in the back, half hidden in duvets, blankets and pillows. I dispensed nibbles en route till we stopped again in Harkerville, where we found our small orange-coloured cottage on the edge of the forest. Tim and I had a game of snooker but we were both so bad, we ended up chucking the balls into the pockets manually which kind of defeats the purpose.

After stopping at a restaurant with a strong nautical flavour, I did some people watching. A handsome young couple sat in front of us. He looked like he’d stepped out of an advert for men’s shampoo and when he shook his head, his finely quaffed hair swung over one eye, while she was model-slim, brown with a long blonde mane of hair in which nestled a huge fake hibiscus, which Col likened to a bird bath. They took turns photographing each other and the full moon with their digital camera. (If this had occurred a few years later, it would definitely have landed on the pages of Instagram.) They both disappeared for some length of time and we were thinking they’d scarpered without paying the bill, but they eventually returned and ordered another meal which got photographed for so long, I was convinced it was now cold and inedible.

After packing the cruiser, we set off towards Port Elizabeth, a small coastal city a little over two hours away, and stopped at a small café for lunch. I found Cape St Francis thanks to my little old cell phone navigator (this was pre-smartphone). The resort where we were going to spend a few days was quite large and situated right next to the sea.

A number of people windsurfed on the beach as we walked along the blustery, wind-blown sand towards the lighthouse.

I bumped into our neighbour, who had asked the day before if we were the noisy type. I had replied that, yes, we were — very! Her husband smiled nervously and said,

‘Ah OK good, that’s fine then.’

Seeing them that morning, I asked her if we had been noisy enough. She laughed and said we’d been great. The journey continued towards Port Elizabeth via Jeffreys Bay, with a brief stop to watch the surfers. The Dung Beetle Lodge where we would be staying was after the Super Tube, a hot spot for waves in the surfing world. We arrived and stared out at a flat sea with nary a ripple in sight. The beach had a pinkish tinge near the salt mines, which was quite pretty.

Our hosts at the Dung Beetle welcomed us to the area and showed us to our rooms. They were decorated in animal themes. Our room was called ‘The Big 5’ and Tim was in ‘Lion’. With hardly any time to unpack and have a cuppa tea we set off to fill up with petrol and head towards the entrance to Addo Park. It was a 16-kilometre stretch from the first gate to the second and it was along here that we saw kudu bulls and a few females, warthogs, and a leopard tortoise. The game drive was good after that, and we encountered more animals; kudu, vervet monkeys, a different tortoise, many elephants, a black-backed jackal, warthogs, and secretary birds. The elephant experience was wonderful. At least four herds gathered at the water hole and some walked right next to the car while crossing the road. They were so close that you could almost lean out and touch their leathery hides (should you be so inclined).

We had the usual rush to get back to the gate by 6 p.m. Why do game parks insist on closing the gates just as the nocturnal animals are starting to wander around? Back at The Dung Beetle, our host advised us where to go for a meal, so we had a quick shower, changed, and walked to the nearby Grunters restaurant. It was the local pub and probably the only place in the area to eat. The steaks were great — cooked to perfection.

That day’s game drive was after 10 a.m. while it was still relatively cool and we found an albino guinea fowl on the side of the road. We were the first through the gate, and it was quiet on the road. We saw our first dung beetle once we passed through the second gate. Later, a few more beetles appeared on a fresh piece of elephant dung. These insects are fascinating to watch as one pushes itself up over the dung, which must seem like a large hill to it.

Fun fact: dung beetles can bury one metric tonne of dung a year and play an important ecological role by removing waste and destroying parasites and pests, thereby returning nutrients to the soil. After collecting their prize, dung beetles always roll the dung in a straight line, regardless of any obstacles in the path.

Male and female kudu were on view (the males are typically larger, with huge twisted horns and fine white stripes over their backs), and warthogs with babies (often nicknamed ‘sucklets’). After a significant amount of driving, the decision was made to stop in the main camp for a drink and to book an evening drive.

Our hostess kindly phoned to tell us where the lions had been sighted, so we sped over (stopping very briefly to look at some buffalo) and managed to have five minutes of ‘Kalahari’, the lion with the huge mane and sore paw that we had seen earlier, to ourselves. We learned that these lions were introduced to Addo from the Kalahari National Park, as they are the closest to the prides that inhabited Addo in the 1890s.

After supper we set off to join Jonathan, our guide at the truck for the night drive. Others included a group of Swedes, an Irish girl, a South African couple, and ourselves. Jonathan was an entertaining and witty raconteur, bellowing titbits of information at intervals through his megaphone as we trundled slowly through the early evening light. We saw some white cranes while it was still light enough, but Jonathan soon traded his megaphone for a huge torch.

‘Kalahari’ the lion made another brief appearance along with more kudu and spring hares, but otherwise the drive wasn’t very eventful.

The transformation from a small refuge for 11 elephants to South Africa’s third-largest national park is amazing and heartening for wildlife conservation. Today, Addo is the only park in the world to house the ‘Big 7’: elephant, rhino, lion, buffalo, leopard, plus great white sharks and southern right whales in its marine section. It’s one of my favourite game parks and although the closest to Cape Town, it’s still a good eight-hour drive.

After the long weekend it was time to start heading back along the coast to Cape Town.
