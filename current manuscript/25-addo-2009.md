# Chapter 22: Addo 2009

The three dogs, sensing our impending departure, were getting underfoot or slinking off to lie somewhere (usually a newly planted flower bed) to look miserable. We left, stopping only at one of those service stations for the ‘bathroom’ and a Steers burger. I pondered the use of the word bathroom as a coy replacement for toilet. I mean it’s not as though there’s even a bath, and if there were, would travellers suddenly decide to have one?

We're on our way to Addo National Elephant Park in the Eastern Cape province, known as South Africa’s third largest park.

We made it to the Wilderness in good time as the ancient Land Cruiser that we'd brought from Saudi was still going well. It still had the distinctive smell of Saudi sand coming through the vents when the engine first started. Tim slotted in the back, half hidden in duvets, blankets and pillows. I dispensed nibbles en route till we stopped again in Harkerville, where we found our small orange-coloured cottage on the edge of the forest. Later Tim and I had a game of snooker but we were both so bad, we ended up chucking the balls into the pockets manually which kind of defeats the purpose.

After stopping at a restaurant with a strong nautical flavour, I did some people watching. A handsome young couple sat in front of us. He looked like he’d stepped out of an advert for men’s shampoo and when he shook his head, his finely coiffed hair swung over one eye, while she was model-slim, brown with a long blonde mane of hair in which nestled a huge fake hibiscus, which Col likened to a bird bath. They took turns photographing each other and the full moon with their digital camera. (If this had occurred a few years later, it would definitely have landed on the pages of Instagram.) They both disappeared for some length of time and we were thinking they’d scarpered without paying the bill, but they eventually returned and ordered another meal which got photographed for so long, I was convinced it was now cold and inedible.

After packing the cruiser we set off towards Port Elizabeth, a small coastal city a little over two hours away. I found Cape St Francis thanks to my little old cellphone navigator (this was pre-smartphone). The resort where we were going to spend a few days was quite large and situated right next to the sea.

A number of people were windsurfing on the beach as we walked along the blustery, wind-blown sand towards the lighthouse.

I bumped into our neighbour, who'd earlier asked if we were the noisy type. I had replied that, yes, we were — very! Her husband had smiled nervously and said,

‘Ah OK good, that’s fine then.’

Seeing them now, I asked her if we'd been noisy enough. She laughed and said we’d been great. The journey continued towards Port Elizabeth via Jeffreys Bay with a brief stop to watch the surfers. The Dung Beetle Lodge where we'd be staying was after the Super Tube, a hot spot for waves in the surfing world. We stared out at a flat sea with nary a ripple in sight. The beach had a pinkish tinge near the salt mines.

Our hosts at the Dung Beetle welcomed us to the area and showed us to our rooms, decorated in animal themes. Our room was called ‘The Big 5’ and Tim was in ‘Lion’. With hardly any time to unpack we set off to fill up with petrol and head towards the entrance to Addo Park. It was a 16-kilometre stretch from the first gate to the second and it was along here that we saw kudu bulls a few females, warthogs and a leopard tortoise. The game drive was good after that; kudu, vervet monkeys, a different tortoise, many elephants, a black-backed jackal, warthogs, and secretary birds. The elephant experience was wonderful. At least four herds gathered at the water hole and a few walked right next to the car while crossing the road. They were so close that you could almost lean out and touch their leathery hides.

We had the usual rush to get back to the gate by 6 p.m. I wondered why game parks insist on closing the gates just as the nocturnal animals are starting to wander around.


Fun fact: dung beetles can bury one metric tonne of dung a year and play an important ecological role by removing waste and destroying parasites and pests, thereby returning nutrients to the soil. After collecting their prize, dung beetles always roll the dung in a straight line, regardless of any obstacles in the path. I saw one on the side of the road and watched it pushing its ball of dung for a while.


Our hostess kindly phoned me on a game drive to tell us where the lions had been sighted, so we sped over (stopping very briefly to look at some buffalo) and managed to have five minutes of ‘Kalahari’, the lion with the huge mane and sore paw that we had seen earlier, to ourselves. We learned that these lions were introduced to Addo from the Kalahari National Park, as they are the closest to the prides that inhabited Addo in the 1890s.

After supper we set off to join Jonathan, our guide at the truck for a night drive. Others included a group of Swedes, an Irish girl, and a South African couple. Jonathan was an entertaining and witty raconteur, bellowing titbits of information at intervals through his megaphone as we trundled slowly through the early evening light. We saw white cranes while it was still light enough before Jonathan traded his megaphone for a huge torch.

‘Kalahari’ the lion made another brief appearance along with more kudu and spring hares otherwise the drive wasn’t very eventful.

The transformation from a small refuge for 11 elephants to South Africa’s third-largest national park is amazing and heartening for wildlife conservation. Today, Addo is the only park in the world to house the ‘Big 7’: elephant, rhino, lion, buffalo, leopard, plus great white sharks and southern right whales in its marine section. It’s one of my favourite game parks and although the closest to Cape Town, it’s still a good eight-hour drive.

After the long weekend it was time to start heading back along the coast to Cape Town.
