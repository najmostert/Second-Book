# Chapter 22: ADDO 2009

The three dogs, sensing our impending departure, are getting underfoot or slinking off to lie somewhere (usually a newly planted flower bed) to look miserable. And off we go, stopping off at one of those ‘rest stops’ for the ‘bathroom’ and a Steers burger. I ponder the use of the word bathroom as a coy replacement for toilet. I mean it’s not as though there’s even a bath and if there were would travellers suddenly decide to have a bath? We’re on our way to Addo National Elephant Park in the Eastern Cape province, known as South Africa’s third largest park. We make it to the Wilderness in good time as the ancient old land cruiser that we bought from Saudi is still going well. It still has the distinctive smell of Saudi sand coming through the vents when the engine first starts. Tim slots in the back, half hidden in duvets, blankets and pillows. I dispense nibbles en route till we stop again in Harkerville. We find our small orange-coloured cottage on the edge of the forest in Harkerville. Tim and I have a game of snooker but we’re both so bad, we end up chucking the balls into the pockets manually which kind of defeats the purpose.After stopping at a restaurant with a strong nautical flavour, I do some people watching. A handsome young couple sit in front of us. He looks like he’s stepped out of an advert for mens shampoo and when he shakes his head, his finely quaffed hair swings over one eye while she is model-slim, brown with a long blonde mane of hair in which nestles a huge fake hibiscus, which Col likens to a bird bath. They take turns photographing each other and the full moon with their digital camera. (If this occurred a few years later, it would definitely have landed on the pages of Instagram). They both disappear for some length of time and we are thinking they’ve scarpered without paying the bill, but they eventually return and order another meal which gets photographed for so long, I’m convinced it’s now cold and inedible. After packing the cruiser, we set off towards Port Elizabeth, a small coastal city a little over two hours away and stop at a small café for lunch. I find Cape St Francis thanks to my little old cell phone navigator (this is pre smart phones). The resort where we are going to spend a few days is quite large and is situated right next to the sea. A number of people windsurf on the beach as we’re walking along the blustery wind blown sand toward the lighthouse. I bump into our neighbour, who asked yesterday if we were the noisy type. I replied that, yes, we are— very! Her husband smiled nervously and said, ‘Ah OK good, that’s fine then.’ Seeing them this morning, I ask her if we were noisy enough. She laughs and says we’ve been great. The journey continues towards Port Elizabeth via Jeffreys Bay, with a brief stop to watch the surfers. The Dung Beetle Lodge where we’ll be staying is after the Super Tube; a hot spot for waves in the surfing world. We arrive and stare out at a flat sea with nary a ripple in sight. The beach has a pinkish tinge near the salt mines, which is quite pretty. Our hosts at the Dung Beetle welcome us to the area and show us to our rooms. They are decorated in animal themes. Our room is called "The Big 5," and Tim is in "Lion," With hardly any time to unpack and have a cuppa tea we set off to fill up with petrol and head toward the entrance to Addo Park. It’s a 16-kilometre stretch from the first gate to the second and it’s along here that we see kudu bulls and a few females, warthogs, and a leopard tortoise. The game drive is good after that, and we encounter more animals; kudu, vervet monkeys, a different tortoise, many elephants, a black-backed jackal, warthogs, and secretary birds. The elephant experience is wonderful. At least four herds gather at the water hole and some walk right next to the car while crossing the road. They are so close that you can almost lean out and touch their leathery hides (should you be so inclined). We have the usual rush to get back to the gate by 6 PM. Why do game parks insist on closing the gates just as the nocturnal animals are starting to wander around? Back at The Dung Beetle, our host advises us where to go for a meal, so we have a quick shower, change, and walk to the nearby Grunters restaurant. It is the local pub and probably the only place in the area to eat. The steaks are great— cooked to perfection. Today’s game drive occurs after 10 AM while it is still relatively cool and we find an albino guinea fowl on the side of the road. We're the first through the gate, and it’s quiet on the road. We see our first dung beetle once we pass through the second gate. Later, a few more beetles appear on a fresh piece of elephant dung. These insects are fascinating to watch as one pushes itself up over the dung, which must seem like a large hill to it. Fun fact: Dung beetles can bury one metric ton of dung a year and play an important ecological role by removing waste and destroying parasites and pests, thereby returning nutrients to the soil. After collecting their prize, dung beetles always roll the dung in a straight line, regardless of any obstacles in the path. Male and female kudu are on view (the males are typically larger, with huge twisted horns and fine white stripes over their backs). warthogs with babies (often nicknamed "sucklets"), After a significant amount of driving, the decision is made to stop in the main camp for a drink and to book an evening drive. Our hostess kindly phones to tell us where the lions have been sighted, so we speed over (stopping very briefly to look at some buffalo) and manage to have five minutes of 'Kalahari,' the lion with the huge mane and sore paw that we saw earlier to ourselves. We learn that these lions were introduced to Addo from the Kalahari National Park, as they are the closest to the prides that inhabited Addo in the 1890s. After supper we set off to join Jonathan, our guide at the truck for the night drive. Others include a group of Swedes, an Irish girl, a South African couple, and ourselves. Jonathan is an entertaining and witty raconteur, bellowing titbits of information at intervals through his megaphone as we trundle slowly through the early evening light. We see some white cranes while it is still light enough, but Jonathan soon trades his megaphone for a huge flashlight. The 'Kalahari' the lion makes another brief appearance along with more kudu and spring hares, but otherwise, the drive isn’t very eventful. The transformation from a small refuge for 11 elephants to South Africa's third-largest national park is amazing and heartening for wild life conservation.Today, Addo is the only park in the world to house the "Big 7": elephant, rhino, lion, buffalo, leopard, plus great white sharks and southern right whales in its marine section. It’s one of my favourite game parks and although the closest to Cape Town, it’s still a good eight hour drive. After the long weekend it’s time to start heading back along the coast to Cape Town. .
