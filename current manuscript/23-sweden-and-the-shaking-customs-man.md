# Chapter 20: Sweden and the Shaking Customs Man

After virtually no preparation to speak of back home, we found ourselves propelling across Cape Town towards the airport terminal, en route for Sweden once more. In true, historic Mostert tradition, our procedure towards actually reaching the departure lounge was fraught with excess baggage fees and the extensive declaration of many and varied pieces of photographic equipment. The printed notice board above the bored South African customs official read: Declare all photographic equipment, so Col confidently approached the desk, extended his hand and shook the official's hand firmly, indicating both his absolute integrity and his trustworthiness. The man looked thoroughly confused, shook his head, held out his empty hand again, and said flatly, ‘Pen.’ I immediately intervened and thrust my Arabella Sheraton pen straight into his palm. ‘I got it for nothing, so you may as well keep it!’ I beamed at him with holiday goodwill and magnanimous generosity. Col commenced the laborious, tedious process of reading out all the factory serial numbers of his many lenses and bodies. He read out the fifteenth long serial number, and the official sighed audibly, repositioned his new pen, and wrote it down on the manifest. I was growing deeply worried we would miss our flight at this rate. I phoned my mother quickly on my mobile. ‘We’re stuck in customs, and I declare I’m entirely fed up!’ I told her over the static. We lurched towards the check-in counter, where a very sour-faced airline official completely omitted eye contact but immediately joined the hand-thrust club. ‘Passport. Boarding pass,’ she barked curtly at Col with a big sniff. She turned her face and proceeded to blast a million virulent flu bugs straight onto his startled body, all without looking up from her screen or offering a single word of apology. Col retreated behind my shoulder, frantically asking me to hand her the passports while he flapped fruitlessly at his clothes with a microfiber camera lens cloth. She returned our documents with another large, wet sniff, and Col leaped back a foot. ‘Have a nice flight with KLM,’ she monotoned to the counter. I had a strong feeling she was not being totally sincere. 

Shovelling Cake at Seven Minutes 

Col’s heavy camera bag strap was biting deeply into his neck, threatening to sever a major artery on the spot, so we hurriedly looked for a place in the terminal to grab a quick bite to eat. I ordered prego rolls, chocolate cake, and French toast with precisely twenty minutes to spare before our boarding call. The kitchen preparation took some time, and the waitress eventually returned to inform us that the restaurant manager wished to remind our table that we now had exactly seven minutes left to scoff the entire order! He obviously derived some form of sadistic pleasure from this announcement, and the portions delivered were absolutely enormous. Tim and I recalled a movie we had watched recently featuring a character named Doodah, and we cackled merrily at the memory while attempting to desperately shovel the equivalent of a heavy three-course meal into our mouths with five minutes to boarding. Suddenly, none of us were actually hungry. ‘Why on earth are we having a heavy supper when they’ll just feed us again on the plane?’ asked Tim reasonably between messy slobbers of thick chocolate cake. ‘Eric says KLM food is absolutely terrible,’ mumbles Col, cramming half a loaf of prego bread into his mouth and chewing furiously. I was tempted, very briefly, to tip the entire remaining contents of my plate straight into my handbag, but I was saved from this social folly by the loudspeaker announcement of our final call to board the aircraft. We all heaved ourselves into an upright position—an act somewhat impeded by our so-called hand luggage, the weight of which was more than double our allocated amount, as usual. I cast a quick glance out at the underbelly cargo hold of the Boeing 777 and declared it structurally intact. It was freezing cold outside on the tarmac, and Tim looked exactly like a stereotypical German tourist, dressed in his shorts, thick socks, and walking sandals. We boarded the cabin slowly and located our rows. Col kept several passengers waiting impatiently in the narrow aisle while he frantically rearranged his overweight camera bags inside overhead bins that were already packed full. The crew offered us hot soup and a cold pasta salad around 3 a.m., which the three of us declined, as we were still actively digesting our massive earlier intake of terminal calories. Six hours later, we were offered a choice between ‘sweet or savoury omelettes’ for breakfast. Tim and I unwisely opted for the sweet version, and a soggy, bizarre custard affair appeared on our trays. Col, having chosen the savoury option, announced loudly that his eggs smelled exactly like wee; nevertheless, he ate the entire portion anyway. After disembarking an hour later into the warm, slightly humid airport in Denmark, we had very little connection time to rush from one end of the terminal to the other where our small connecting plane for Sweden was scheduled to take off. Having reached the gate just in the nick of time, the desk agents announced a delay of forty minutes. Airports! 

Lost Luggage in Stockholm 

We finally arrived at 2:20 p.m. to a thoroughly wet Sweden. After watching the luggage carousel go round for the umpteenth time with nary a sign of any of our cases appearing, we came to the reluctant conclusion that, while our bodies were definitely in Sweden, the exact same could not be said for our clothes. It seems we were not the only passengers to stand in the depressing lost luggage line. We filled out numerous carbon forms, and the airline official said he ‘hoped’ to locate our suitcases by tomorrow. I was not at all happy with his choice of the word ‘hope’, and felt he should have utilized words with far more professional conviction —such as ‘I’m positive’ or ‘They will definitely arrive’. Many abandoned suitcases were still revolving forlornly around the rubber carousel, which meant another lot of frustrated passengers were currently waiting in entirely the wrong place. Col’s mother and stepfather met our family at the barriers and drove us straight to their well-situated apartment in Stockholm. We caught up on family news, and enjoyed a short walk through the nearby fir forest at 10:30 p.m. Tim marvelled at how incredibly light it still was outside despite the late hour; back home in Cape Town, it had already been pitch dark for six hours. The next morning, Col went for a long bicycle ride, so Tim and I walked downtown together to look at the buildings. We both thoroughly enjoy old architecture, and Stockholm possesses many historic treasures. After lunch, we met up with Col’s sister, Annie, and caught the metro train down to the harbour where our ship, The Victoria, had arrived at the quay. It was a large, double-storey vessel. After boarding the deck, we set sail on the beautiful Mälaren lake. The weather was cool, crisp, and pleasant. Chugging a cold beer each, we watched the passing scenery slide by: many small islets, a green tree-lined coastline, and stately wooden buildings. 

The Barefoot Viking of Birka 

We eventually disembarked at Birka—the only true historic Viking town on the Scandinavian peninsula, situated on the island of Björkö. Birka was established back in the 8th century and served as a crucial, bustling trading market town on the western side of the island for over two hundred years. The island's boundaries were a lot smaller during the Viking Age due to the Baltic sea level being roughly four to five metres higher back then than it is today. We wandered over to a local harbour restaurant to enjoy a traditional ‘Fisk och lök’ (fish and onion) roll served with hot coffee. Our tour guide, Lové (pronounced ‘Loo-veh’), was a blond-haired Swedish lad dressed in authentic Viking attire consisting of a leather waistcoat and baggy brown trousers that ended abruptly above the ankle. He took our group on a walking tour of the small island entirely barefoot because, he informed us with a grin, their seasonal supply of ‘Viking shoes’ had not yet arrived from the supplier! We followed his bare feet up to the nearby earthen fort, then down some narrow gravel tracks, some completely overgrown with grass, where once ancient Birka lay. Beautiful birch trees grew between the massive burial mounds, of which there are roughly 1,600 individual graves remaining on the island. The absolute highest point of the ridge was dedicated with a large stone cross monument. The cross, rising above the island like a beacon, had been constructed to honour the Benedictine monk Ansgar, who brought Christianity to the country back in 830 A.D. Lové told our group that the modern village of Björkö consists of only two functional farms that historically date back to the 750s. We chatted with him while leaning our backs against the old stone wall of the former hill fort. There was a small museum on the island where I purchased some dried hops along with a traditional recipe to brew my own mead the moment I returned home. (I eventually found the packet of hops five years later, hidden behind a bottle of Chinese five-spice in my kitchen cupboard, completely full of weevils.) The ancient burial mounds featured small stones arranged around their perimeters in the shape of perfect circles. Lové explained that the famous Hjalmar Stolpe excavations originally took place here back in 1871. While searching the soil for valuable amber, Stolpe had kept a meticulous diary documenting his daily excursions. Further on down the track, there was a specific grave site known as ‘The Elk Man’, where human remains had been discovered buried alongside his iron weapons and half an elk antler lying perfectly on the left side of his skull. Lové told our family of how the islanders historically took all matters of legal concern straight to the King's court, accompanied by expensive gifts. Before the implementation of Christianity, a democratic system of the ‘Ting’ existed in Norse society. Ting assemblies were an early Scandinavian system of administration, governance, and justice during the Viking era. Local disputes were resolved and political decisions made at regional assemblies, which were represented in a strict hierarchy. The tings also functioned as sacred religious sites and open markets for commerce. I was just waiting for him to turn around and say, ‘And another ting...’ After a thoroughly fascinating few hours exploring the island we returned to The Victoria and enjoyed our supper on board. This evening’s culinary choice was poached salmon served with fresh new potatoes and a green garden salad. I watched with amusement a small group of rather jaded-looking girls celebrating a hen party at the corner table. Back at the apartment, we had to keep our window blanket pinned at a sharp angle to prevent the powerful, bright morning sun from entering our room at 4 a.m. and waking us up. Gröna Lund amusement park was on our itinerary today, so we departed immediately after breakfast. It was exceptionally crowded at the gates, as it happened to be the first official day of the Swedish summer holidays. I was unfortunately persuaded by the boys to go on one of the high ‘fun rides’—a complete misnomer if I ever heard one! I regretted my decision the exact second the iron safety bar was strapped across my chest, and I kept my eyes tightly shut for the entire way up and down, completely defeating the structural purpose of the view. The commercial photograph that we purchased afterwards showed my eyes tightly closed while I wore an expression of absolute, abject terror. Afterwards, Annie, Tim, and I decided to try the ‘Crazy House’ attraction, navigating its lopsided stairs, uneven floors, and rooms that mechanically ‘moved’. We followed this adventure with a trip through the 'Haunted House', clutching each other tightly as we walked slowly through the dark, spider-webbed corridors, waiting to be scared shitless at every turn. Just the psychological idea that something or someone is about to pounce out at you from the dark is all it takes, and I find myself shrieking before anything has even attempted to scare us. After using every bit of adrenaline in our collective bodies, we decide it’s lunch time. The restaurant overlooks the harbour below and is quite pretty with many window boxes of brightly coloured flowers. After a tasty respite, Annie and Tim head off to be scared some more on the long jump. The one where they lift you in your seats to enormous heights and suddenly drop you so that you feel as though you’ve lost a few body organs in the process. No thank you. There are a few tightrope artistes to watch as well so I decide that will be kinder to my adrenaline-depleted body. Afterwards we take a bus back to Sturegatan where I pack for a trip down to Karlskrona tomorrow. Around 3am Tim crawls his way in his sleeping bag to the darker corridor to avoid the bright light. We’re all a bit snappy this morning due to a lack of sleep and walk down to the metro to buy tickets for our train cabin after which we climb aboard and settle down for a slight nod. I doze fitfully and awaken ready for some caffeine so waddle down the rocking corridor to the food carriage to buy us all a coffee. We have to change trains three hours into the journey. Once aboard the second train, we try to find out where our new seats are but there’s no one around to ask so eventually we just find three empty seats and sit there. I brought my take away salad with me from the first train and try to open the packet without stabbing anyone in the ear with my plastic knife. Harder than you think. We arrive in Karlskrona after 3pm and the Mostert’s are there to meet us. For some reason Mark, Col’s brother, is waving a fluffy blue wig. The reason is never provided and is accepted as being normal behaviour. Mark and Julie have given us one of their cars so we can do some sight seeing ourselves while they’re at work. All three of their boys take up the back of one car. We drive to their house - a colourful dolls house with lace curtains and flowers everywhere. It’s very pretty. There are two garages and a big garden. Ideal for the kids. Mark prepares a BBQ in the garden and cooks up an amazing array of meats and potatoes with crusty bread. The ferry is full but we can move around and look at the surrounding islands as we pass. We return to the ‘gingerbread house’ then race over to the train station just in time to pile ourselves and our luggage onto the train before the whistle blows. The same fiasco as last time occurs with changing carriages and we find ourselves dragging tons of luggage behind us as we wander down the track looking for our seats. In the end we just haul ourselves on board and I lift a 25kg case over a dog kennel, complete with dog. I’m still swearing as we enter the ‘Tst Afdeling’ - the ‘quiet carriage’ and receive a few frowns. Back in Stockholm, Anne Sofie says she’s treating us to a short cruise to Finland on the Viking Lines, The Arabella. It has a huge black and red funnel and looks quite sleek. I’m quite excited at the prospect of visiting another Nordic country. It’s quite exciting for all of us, especially for Tim who hasn’t been on a liner before. The Arabella is technically a ferry but it loses non of its majestic splendour for that. Someone takes photos of the passengers as we are boarding. I later walk around to find the photos of us that have been displayed on a large white board. I recognise Tim’s T-shirt and have a closer look. In the photograph, I have turned round to say something to Col. My eyes are half closed, mouth is open mid sentence and the overall look is of the intellectually disabled so I quickly return to the others and say I can’t find our photo. After locating our cabins which are close to each other on the 6th level,Tim and I go exploring while Col and Tim’s farmör sit on deck. A movie is showing that we haven’t seen, so at 5:30 Tim and I buy some popcorn and sit watching ‘What happened in Vegas’. There are only a handful of other viewers so we can whisper loudly to each other and munch popcorn. Col and his mum are sitting in a bar next to a salt encrusted window. We order a beer. I ask the barman what he recommends and he says the local ‘Lamar Kultar’ is good so that’s what I have. Supper is a huge buffet so I pile my plate with shrimp, salmon and salad. I’ve never eaten so much salmon since arriving in Sweden. Its great! There’s ‘free’ wine as well (actually there’s no such thing as ‘free’ in the context of a cruise). After a look around the duty free shop where I buy some items with my ‘rubber expanding’ credit card (not!), we repair to our cabins to read and sleep to the gentle rocking of the boat.
