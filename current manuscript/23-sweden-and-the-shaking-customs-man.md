# Chapter 20: Sweden and the Shaking Customs Man

After virtually no preparation to speak of, we were propelling ourselves across Cape Town towards the airport en route for Sweden. In true Mostert tradition the procedure towards actually getting to the departure lounge was fraught with excess baggage and the declaration of many and varied photographic equipment. The notice above the bored customs official read, ‘Declare all photographic equipment’, so Col approached the official’s outstretched hand and shook it firmly, indicating both his integrity and trustworthiness. The man shook his head, held out his hand again and said,

‘Pen.’

I intervened and thrust my Arabella Sheraton pen into his palm.

‘I got it for nothing so you may as well keep it!’

I beamed at him with goodwill and ‘magnanimous’ generosity.

Col began the laborious process of reading all the serial numbers of his many pieces of equipment. He read out the fifteenth serial number and the official sighed audibly, repositioned his pen and wrote it down. I was worried we would miss our flight at this rate. I phoned Mum quickly.

‘We’re in customs and I declare I’m fed up!’ I told her.

We lurched towards check-in where a very sour-faced official omitted eye contact but joined the hand-thrust club.

‘Passport. Boarding pass.’ she barked curtly at Col with a big sniff.

She turned to blast a million virulent bugs onto his startled body, all without looking up or apologising. Col quickly retreated behind me and asked me to give her the passports while he flapped fruitlessly at his person with a camera lens cloth. She returned the passports with another large sniff and Col leapt back again.

‘Haveaniceflightwithklm.’ she monotoned.

I had a feeling she was not being totally sincere.

Shovelling Cake at Seven Minutes

Col’s camera bag strap was biting into his neck and threatening to sever an artery so we hurriedly looked for a place to grab a bite to eat. I ordered prego rolls, chocolate cake and French toast with twenty minutes to spare before boarding. Preparation took some time and the waitress informed us that the manager wished to remind us we now had seven minutes left to scoff the lot.

He obviously derived some sadistic pleasure in this and the portions were enormous.

Tim and I recalled a movie we had seen recently with a character called Doodah and we cackled merrily at this while attempting to shovel the equivalent of a three-course meal into our mouths with five minutes to boarding.

Suddenly none of us were hungry.

‘Why are we having supper when they’ll feed us again on the plane?’ asked Tim reasonably between slobbers of thick chocolate cake.

‘Eric says KLM food is terrible.’ mumbled Col, cramming half a loaf of bread into his mouth and chewing furiously.

I was tempted very briefly to tip the contents of my plate into my bag but was saved from this folly by the announcement of our call to board the plane.

We all heaved ourselves into an upright position, an act somewhat impeded by our so-called hand luggage, the weight of which was more than double our allocated amount as usual. I cast a quick glance at the underbelly of the 777 and declared it intact. It was freezing outside and Tim looked like a German tourist in his shorts, socks and sandals.

 Col kept several passengers waiting impatiently in the aisle while he rearranged his overweight camera bags in overhead bins that were already full.

We were offered soup and cold pasta salad around 3 a.m. which we all declined as we were still digesting our earlier intake of calories. Six hours later we were offered a choice between ‘sweet or savoury omelettes’ for breakfast. Tim and I unwisely went for the sweet, and a soggy custard affair appeared. Col, having chosen savoury, said the eggs smelled like wee. Nevertheless he ate it all.

After disembarking an hour later into the warm, slightly humid airport in Denmark we had very little time to rush from one end of the airport to the other where our small plane for Sweden would take off. Having got there just in time, they announced a delay of forty minutes. Airports!

Lost Luggage in Stockholm

We arrived to a cloudy wet Sweden. After watching the luggage carousel go round for the umpteenth time with nary a sign of any of our cases, we came to the reluctant conclusion that, while we were definitely in Sweden, the same could not be said for our luggage. It seemed we were not the only ones to stand in the lost luggage line. We filled in numerous forms and the official said they hoped to locate our luggage by the next day. I was not happy with the word ‘hope’ and felt he should have used words with more conviction, such as ‘I’m positive’ or, ‘They will definitely’.

Many suitcases were still revolving forlornly around the carousel which meant another lot of passengers were waiting in the wrong place.

Col’s mum and stepfather met us and took us to their well-situated apartment in Stockholm. We caught up and had a short walk through the nearby fir forest at 10:30 p.m. Tim marvelled at how light it was despite the late hour. It had already been dark for six hours at home.

Col went for a bike ride so Tim and I walked downtown and looked at buildings. We both enjoy old architecture and Stockholm has many. After lunch we waited for Col’s sister Annie and took the metro to the harbour where the ship, ‘The Victoria’, had arrived. It was a large double-storey vessel. We set sail on the Mälaren lake. The weather was cool and pleasant. Chugging a beer each we watched the passing scenery; many islets and green tree-lined coastline, and stately wooden buildings.

The Barefoot Viking of Birka

We disembarked at Birka, the only true Viking town on the Scandinavian peninsula of Björkö which was, until a thousand years ago, a busy market town on the western side of the island. It was a lot smaller in the Viking age due to the Baltic being roughly four to five metres higher than it is today. We wandered over to a restaurant for a ‘Fisk och lök’ roll with coffee (fish and onion).

Lové (pronounced ‘Louverh’) was a blond-haired Swedish lad dressed in Viking attire of leather waistcoat and baggy brown trousers that ended above the ankle. He took us on a tour of the small island barefoot because, he informed us, their supply of ‘Viking shoes’ had not yet arrived. We followed him to the nearby fort then down some narrow gravel tracks overgrown with grass, where ‘once ancient Björkö lay’, he informed us in his slightly accented English. Birch trees grew between the large burial mounds of which there are roughly 1,600 on the island. The highest point was dedicated with a large stone cross that rose above the island like a beacon, erected to honour the Benedictine monk Ansgar.

Lové told us that the town of Björkö consists of only two farms that date back to the 750's.

We chatted to him while leaning against the old stone wall of the former fort. There was a small museum on the island and I bought some hops with a recipe to make mead as soon as I got home. (I found the packet of hops five years later, full of weavils, hidden behind the ‘Chinese seven spice’ bottle.)

The burial mounds had small stones around them in the shape of circles. The Hjalmar Stolpe excavation took place in 1871. Further on was a grave site called ‘The Elk Man’ where his remains were discovered with his weapons and half an elk antler lying on the left side of his head.

Lové told us how people took all matters of concern to the King’s court accompanied by gifts. Before Christianity, a system of ‘Ting’ existed in court. Ting assemblies were an early system of administration and justice during the Viking times. Local disputes were resolved and political decisions made at regional tings, while local tings were represented at higher-level tings in a hierarchy. Tings also functioned as religious sites and meeting places for commerce. I was waiting for him to say,

‘And another ting…’

After a fascinating few hours on the island we returned to the Victoria and had supper on board. The evening’s choice was poached salmon, new potatoes and a salad. I watched a small group of rather jaded-looking hen party girls cavorting the harbour in dubious flimsy attire.

Gröna Lund was on the cards the following day so we left after breakfast. It was very crowded, as it was also the first official day of the Swedish summer. I was persuaded to go on one of the ‘fun rides’, a misnomer if I ever heard one. I regretted it as soon as I was strapped in and kept my eyes closed the entire way up and down, defeating the purpose somewhat. The photo that we received afterwards showed my eyes tightly shut and wearing an expression of abject terror.

Annie, Tim and I tried the ‘Crazy house’ with its lopsided stairs and uneven floors and rooms that ‘moved’. We followed this with a trip through the ‘haunted house’ and clutched each other as we walked slowly through the dark spider-webbed rooms waiting to be scared shitless at every turn. Just the idea that something or someone is going to pounce on you is all it takes and I found myself shrieking before anything had even attempted to scare us.

After using every bit of adrenaline in our collective bodies we decided it was lunch time. The restaurant overlooked the harbour below and was quite pretty with window boxes of brightly coloured flowers. After a tasty lunch, Annie and Tim headed off to be scared some more on the long jump. The one where they lift you in your seats to enormous heights and suddenly drop you so that you feel as though you’ve lost a few body organs in the process. No thank you. There were a few tightrope artistes so I decided that would be kinder to my adrenaline-depleted body.

Around 3 a.m. Tim crawled his way in his sleeping bag to the darker corridor to avoid the bright light. We were all a bit snappy that morning due to a lack of sleep and walked down to the metro, climbed aboard and settled down for a slight nod.

I dozed fitfully and awoke ready for some caffeine so waddled down the rocking corridor to the food carriage to buy us all coffee. We had to change trains three hours into the journey. Once aboard the second train, we just found three empty seats and sat there. I had brought my takeaway salad with me from the first train and tried to open the packet without stabbing anyone in the ear with my plastic knife. Harder than you think.

We arrived in Karlskrona after 3 p.m. and the Mosterts were there to meet us. For some reason Mark, Col’s brother, was waving a fluffy blue wig. The reason was never provided and was accepted as normal behaviour. Mark and Julie had lent us one of their cars for sightseeing while they were at work. All three of their boys took up the back of one car. We drove to their house — a colourful doll’s house with lace curtains and flowers everywhere. It was very pretty wth two garages and a big garden. Ideal for the kids. Mark prepared a BBQ in the garden and cooked up an amazing array of meats and potatoes with crusty bread.

The ferry was full but we could move around and look at the surrounding islands as we passed. We returned to the ‘gingerbread house’ then raced over to the train station just in time to pile ourselves and our luggage onto the train before the whistle blew.

The same fiasco as last time occurred with changing carriages and we found ourselves dragging tons of luggage behind us as we wandered down the track looking for our seats. In the end we just hauled ourselves on board and I lifted a 25 kg case over a dog kennel, complete with dog. I was still swearing as we entered the ‘Tst Afdeling’ — the ‘quiet carriage’ — and received a few frowns.

Back in Stockholm, Anne Sofie said she was treating us to a short cruise to Finland on the Viking Lines, The Arabella. It had a huge black and red funnel and looked quite sleek. I was quite excited at the prospect of visiting another Nordic country. It was quite exciting for all of us, especially for Tim who hadn’t been on a liner before. The Arabella is technically a ferry but it loses none of its majestic splendour for that.

Someone took photos of the passengers as we were boarding. I later walked around to find the photos of us that had been displayed on a large white board. I recognised Tim’s T-shirt and had a closer look. In the photograph, I had turned round to say something to Col. My eyes were half closed, mouth open mid-sentence and the overall look was of the intellectually disabled, so I quickly returned to the others and said I couldn’t find our photo.

After locating our cabins, which were close to each other on the 6th level, Tim and I went exploring while Col and Tim’s farmor sat on deck. A movie was showing that we hadn’t seen, so at 5:30 Tim and I bought some popcorn and sat watching ‘What happened in Vegas’. There were only a handful of other viewers so we could whisper loudly to each other and munch popcorn.

Col and his mum were sitting in a bar next to a salt-encrusted window. We ordered a beer. I asked the barman what he recommended and he said the local ‘Lamar Kultar’ was good so that’s what I had. Supper was a huge buffet so I piled my plate with shrimp, salmon and salad. I’d never eaten so much salmon since arriving in Sweden. It was great! There was ‘free’ wine as well (actually there’s no such thing as ‘free’ in the context of a cruise).

After a look around the duty-free shop where I bought some items with my ‘rubber expanding’ credit card (not!), we repaired to our cabins to read and sleep to the gentle rocking of the boat.
