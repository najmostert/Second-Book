# Chapter 35: Paris 2015

## Monday

It had been an ‘annus horribilis’, to quote the Queen and Tim was in Europe, so I planned a trip to France with him. Two old geezers sat behind me, chatting the entire flight about cars, prolapses and urinary issues — specifically their wonky prostates.

We arrived mid-morning and I waited at the carousel for my cling-wrapped bag before taking the train from Charles de Gaulle Airport into the city. An impromptu Michael Jackson impersonator sang a few songs in the aisle. He wasn’t very good.

Tim was at the station to meet me. He’d been travelling for two years as a digital nomad and was planning on being in Paris the same time as me. Our hostel was okay but the lift wasn’t working and we were on the 5th floor. We walked up the nearby hill to the Sacré-Cœur Basilica. Since its completion it had been a place of perpetual adoration — a constant presence of the faithful praying before the Blessed Sacrament day and night — and several figures were bowed in prayer.

Buskers earned a few euros with various acts. An old man wearing a cloth cap played the accordion. Another had converted a shopping trolley into a music centre; whilst a record played, he accompanied the music on a flute, adding to the happy holiday atmosphere. A large bronze statue of a woman sat in the plaza where tradition held that rubbing one of her breasts brought good luck. I noticed that her breasts were the only part of the statue gleaming a bright copper. In need of some luck, I rubbed enthusiastically.

Later, we walked into the city via a boulangerie for a croissant before taking the metro to the Jardin des Tuileries and Starbucks for coffee. We tried to get into the Louvre, but it was too full with long queues snaking down the entire courtyard — the downside to being there in summer — so we looked at sculptures in the garden, including a rather ornately carved archway before walking back along the Quai Conti. At Saint-Germain-des-Prés, we lay down next to the canal wall.

The Eiffel Tower at the Champ de Mars looked different from how I imagined it — bigger somehow, but no less impressive. It was hard to get the full perspective unless you were a fair distance away, but we walked around the base and took photos before heading along Port de la Bourdonnais to Trocadéro and Palais de Chaillot; an Art Deco building built in 1937 for the World’s Fair with curved wings, a large esplanade and stunning views of the tower. The metro took us to the Champs-Élysées to see the Arc de Triomphe. I had a lovely moment looking at one of the world’s best-known symbols of French national identity. At its base stood a torch. Every evening at 6:30 p.m. it was re-lit and veterans laid wreaths near its flickering flame, recalling an unknown French soldier who gave his life in World War I — a lovely sentiment, if a huge task. We tried the Louvre again at 5 p.m., thinking we’d have a better chance of getting in — and we did. It was fascinating to walk around looking at famous statues such as the Venus de Milo. The Mona Lisa was surprisingly small — I was sure I wasn’t alone in thinking it was a huge painting. It was still somewhat surreal gazing at it in the flesh, so to speak.

The train took us to Pigalle from Crimée. Humidity wrapped itself round my head as we walked to the ‘red-light district’ for our morning coffee. The café overlooked the famous Moulin Rouge where, the story goes, in 1899 a young Englishman called Christian became infatuated with Satine, a singer there. She was promised by the manager to a Duke in return for funding his next production. As the young lovers continued to meet in secret, Satine’s wedding day drew closer but she had hidden the fact she had tuberculosis and later died in her lover’s arms. Très tragique.

Tim and I caught a high-speed train to Mont Saint-Michel. Our hotel was eco-friendly and focused on recycling, something I was keen on pursuing at home. It was also cooler than Paris. We dumped our cases and decided to  walk the mount straight away.

It was set in a pretty, open, rural area dating back to the early 8th century. Aubert, bishop of nearby Avranches, claimed that the Archangel Michael appeared before him and told him to build a church on the island just out to sea — which he subsequently did.

The fun part was getting there; you could walk right up to the entrance at low tide or take a small boat when the tide was high. Mont Saint-Michel is a real French commune that boasts a mayor, a priest, a postman, municipal policemen and waste services. In total there were around twenty residents, including a number of cats.

I was pleased to note the tide was out. All around us was open grassland in golden hues in the setting sun; cows grazed and pretty flowers dotted the area with clumps of bright colour. Our first proper sighting of the mount was of a dark, towering piece of rock with a church at its peak. It was a majestic sight and I quickened my pace. The entrance was easily found and we continued up the narrow, cobbled street, passing shops, restaurants and a hotel as we climbed. A stone wall covered the back of the island. The views were amazing. It had the look of a quaint medieval town with all the buildings nestled together in a cluster of grey stone.

Tim had read about the famous ‘omelette de la mère Poulard’, developed by Anne Boutiaut Poulard in the 19th century on the island and still served at her restaurant, 'La Mère Poulard'. I wanted to try it, so entered the cafe hoping for a table with a magnificent view but it was  busy with a busload of Japanese tourists and excitable tour guides. I grabbed a small table inside and ordered the famous omelette with cheese while Tim had the fresh oysters. Service was very slow but when the omelette arrived I could see why it was so famous — light, creamy and fluffy all at once. Delicious.

After supper we continued climbing to the top for a good look over the surrounding countryside, which gleamed  burnished gold in the aftermath of the setting sun. The tide was slowly coming in, so we hurried down and strolled around the small, dark grey-sand beach. The last of the light cast a beautiful golden glow on the village. An old boat lay on its side in the damp sand and I took photos in various languishing poses before heading back along the pathway to the hotel. By now the sky was strewn with pastel colours. 

The day began infinitely busier as busload after busload of tourists spilled into the car park. Again the tide was out so we walked up through the town.
Access to the Gothic Abbey via the Grand Degré, a wide staircase of about 350 steps, once blocked by a swinging door guarded by a watchman. The buildings all wrapped around the top of the rock like decorations pressed into a chocolate cake. At its highest, the abbey church rested on crypts built into the rock — a masterpiece of Gothic architecture from the beginning of the 13th century as the dark, ornate buildings clawed at the blue sky. From the church and stairways were panoramic views. 
We'd asked the hotel receptionist where to wait for the bus back to Paris and she pointed across the road. After an hour and no sign of a bus, Tim asked the station master who informed us that we’d missed it. The information we should have been given was to catch the local bus to Ponterson first and another to Rennes. We were now stranded, tired and irritable having stood in the sun for so long. I ordered a taxi which cost muchos euros. He dropped us off at Hotel Florin and after checking in, went in search of a drink at a nearby restaurant. It was too late to order anything to eat so after a glass of wine we returned to our hot airless room. We were heading for Spain the following day.
