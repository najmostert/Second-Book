# Chapter 35: Paris 2015

## Monday

It had been an ‘annus horribilis’, to quote the Queen, and Tim was in Europe, so I had planned a trip to France with him. I watched two episodes of Miranda on the screen. Two old geezers sat behind me, chatting the entire flight about cars, prolapses, and urinary issues — their wonky prostates.

After boarding the small plane for France from Dubai, I walked to my window seat. A Korean girl was sitting between me and another man. I looked out of the window, watching the earth fly by as we took off.

We arrived mid-morning and I waited at the carousel for my cling-wrapped bag before taking the train from Charles de Gaulle Airport into the city. An impromptu Michael Jackson impersonator sang a few songs in the aisle. He wasn’t very good.

Tim was at the station to meet me. He’d been travelling for two years as a digital nomad and was planning on being in Paris. The hostel was quite nice, but the lift wasn’t working and we were on the 5th floor. Later, we took a train to Montmartre, which was quite a pretty place. We walked up the nearby hill to the Sacré-Cœur Basilica. Since its completion it has been a place of perpetual adoration — a constant presence of the faithful praying before the Blessed Sacrament day and night — and I did notice several figures bowed in prayer.

Quite a few buskers were earning a few euros around the area. An old man wearing a cloth cap played the accordion. Another had converted a shopping trolley into a music centre; while a record played, he accompanied it on a flute, adding to the general holiday atmosphere. A large bronze statue of a woman sat in the plaza where tradition holds that rubbing one of her breasts brings good luck. I noticed that her breasts were the only part of the statue gleaming a bright copper. In need of some luck, I rubbed away enthusiastically.

Later, we walked into the city, stopping at a boulangerie for a croissant before taking the metro to the Jardin des Tuileries and Starbucks for coffee. We tried to get into the Louvre, but it was too full, with long queues snaking down the entire courtyard — that’s the downside to coming here in summer — so we looked at the sculptures in the garden, including a rather ornately carved archway, and walked back along the Quai Conti. At Saint-Germain-des-Prés, we lay down next to the canal wall to catch up.

The Eiffel Tower at the Champ de Mars looked different from how I imagined it — bigger somehow, but no less impressive. It was hard to get the whole thing into view unless you were a fair distance away, but we walked around the base and took photos before heading along Port de la Bourdonnais to Trocadéro and the Palais de Chaillot, an Art Deco building built in 1937 for the World’s Fair, with curved wings, a large esplanade, and stunning views of the tower. The metro took us to the Champs-Élysées, where we saw the Arc de Triomphe. It was a lovely moment, looking at one of the world’s best-known symbols of French national identity, which took thirty years to build. At its base stands a torch. Every evening at 6:30 p.m. it is re-lit and veterans lay wreaths near its flickering flame, recalling an unknown French soldier who gave his life in World War I — a lovely sentiment, if a huge task. We decided to try for the Louvre again at 5 p.m., thinking we’d have a better chance of getting in — and we did. It was fascinating to walk around looking at famous statues like the Venus de Milo. The Mona Lisa was surprisingly small — I’m sure I am not alone in thinking it was a huge painting. It was still somewhat surreal gazing at it in the flesh, so to speak.

This morning, we took a train to Pigalle from Crimée. It was already humid walking down to the ‘red-light district’ for our morning coffee tipple. The café overlooked the famous Moulin Rouge where, the story goes, in 1899 a young Englishman called Christian became infatuated with Satine, a singer there. She was promised by the manager to a Duke in return for funding his next production. As the young lovers continued to meet in secret, Satine’s wedding day drew closer, but she hid the fact she had tuberculosis and later died in her lover’s arms. It’s très tragique.

Tim and I caught a high-speed train to Mont Saint-Michel. The hotel was eco-friendly and focused on recycling, something I’m keen on pursuing at home. It was also a lot cooler here than it had been in Paris. We dumped our cases and decided to go straight to see the mount.

It’s set in a pretty, open, rural area dating back to the early 8th century, when Aubert, bishop of nearby Avranches, claimed that the Archangel Michael appeared before him and told him to build a church on the island just out to sea — which he subsequently did.

The fun part is getting there; you can walk right up to the entrance at low tide or take a small boat when the tide is high. Mont Saint-Michel is a real French commune that boasts a mayor, a priest, a postman, municipal policemen and waste services. In total there are around twenty residents, including a number of cats.

As we walked towards it, I was pleased to note that the tide was out. All around us was open grassland in golden hues in the setting sun; cows grazed and pretty flowers dotted the area with clumps of bright colour. Our first real sighting of the mount was of a dark, towering piece of rock with a church at its top. It was a majestic sight and I quickened my pace. The entrance was easily found so we continued up the narrow, cobbled street, passing shops, restaurants and a hotel as we climbed. A stone wall covered the back of the island. The views were amazing. It had the look of a quaint medieval town, with all the buildings nestled together in a cluster of grey stone.

Tim had read about the famous ‘omelette de la mère Poulard’, developed by Anne Boutiaut Poulard in the 19th century on the island and still served at her restaurant, La Mère Poulard, and many others. I wanted to try it, so entered hoping for a table with a magnificent view, but it was very busy with a busload of Japanese tourists and excitable tour guides. There was a small table inside, so I ordered the famous omelette with cheese while Tim had the fresh oysters. The service was very slow, but when the omelette arrived I could see why it is so famous — light, creamy and fluffy all at once. Delicious.

After supper we continued climbing the many stairs to the top and took a good look over the surrounding countryside, which gleamed a burnished gold in the aftermath of the setting sun. The tide was slowly coming in, so we hurried down and strolled around the small, dark grey sand beach. The last of the light cast a beautiful golden glow on the village. There was an old boat lying on its side in the damp sand and we took photos in various lounging poses before heading back along the pathway to the hotel. By now the sky was strewn with pastel colours. It was quite a walk and we arrived back at 10:30 p.m.

It was another lovely day; hotter than yesterday and infinitely busier as busload after busload of tourists spilled into the car park. Again the tide was out, so we walked up through the town.

Access to the Gothic Abbey is via the Grand Degré, a wide staircase of about 350 steps, once blocked by a swinging door guarded by a watchman. The buildings all wrap around the top of the rock like decorations pressed into a chocolate cake. At the top, the abbey church rests on crypts built into the rock — a masterpiece of Gothic architecture from the beginning of the 13th century, the dark ornate buildings clawing at the blue sky. From the church and other rooms and stairways there were great panoramic views. We had earlier asked the hotel receptionist where to wait for the bus back to Paris and she pointed in the general direction, so we waited as directed across the road.
