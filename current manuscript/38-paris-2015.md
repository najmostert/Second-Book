# Chapter 35: Paris 2015

It had been an annus horribilis, as the Queen would say, so I planned a trip to France with Tim, who was already in Europe. I watched two episodes of Miranda on the flight while two old geezers behind me chatted incessantly about cars, prolapses, and their wonky prostates. We arrived mid-morning, and after taking the train from Charles de Gaulle, I met Tim at the station. He had been travelling as a digital nomad for two years. Our hostel was nice, though the lift was broken and we were on the fifth floor. Later, we took a train to Montmartre and hiked up to the Sacré-Coeur Basilica. Since its completion, it has been a place of perpetual adoration, and sure enough, several figures were bowed in prayer. The area was alive with buskers. An old man played the accordion, while another had converted a shopping trolley into a music centre, playing records while he accompanied them on a flute. I also found a large bronze statue of a woman in the plaza; tradition holds that rubbing her breast brings good luck. As she was the only part of the statue gleaming bright copper, I rubbed away enthusiastically. We strolled to a boulangerie for croissants before taking the metro to the Jardin des Tuileries. The Louvre was too full, so we spent time looking at sculptures in the garden and walking along the Quay Conti. The Eiffel Tower was more impressive than I imagined—bigger, somehow. We walked around the base, then continued to the Palais de Chaillot for stunning views. At the Arc de Triomphe, we watched the symbolic flame being tended—a lovely sentiment for the unknown soldier. We tried the Louvre again at 5 p.m. and finally got in; the Venus de Milo was fascinating, though the Mona Lisa was surprisingly small. The next morning, we walked through Pigalle for coffee, overlooking the Moulin Rouge. I recounted the tragic tale of Christian and Satine—très tragique! 

Mont Saint-Michel 

Tim and I caught a high-speed train to Mont Saint-Michel. The hotel was eco-friendly and focused on recycling—something I’m keen to adopt at home. It was much cooler here than in Paris. Mont Saint-Michel dates back to the early 8th century, when Aubert, the bishop of Avranches, claimed the Archangel Michael appeared and told him to build a church at the top of the island. You can walk right up to the entrance at low tide or take a boat when the tide is high. It’s a real French commune with around twenty residents, including a number of cats. As we walked toward it, the tide was out. The surrounding grassland glowed in golden hues as cows grazed and colourful flowers dotted the landscape. Our first real sighting of the mount was a dark, towering piece of rock with a church at the top. The narrow, cobbled streets were packed with shops, restaurants, and hotels, and the views from the ancient stone walls were absolutely amazing. It looked for all the world like a quaint medieval cluster of grey stone, nestling together in the sea. Tim had read about the famous ‘omelette de la mère Poulard,’ developed by Anne Boutiaut Poulard in the 19th century. It is served at her original restaurant, La Mère Poulard, and many others on the island. I was keen to try it and entered the restaurant hoping for a table with a magnificent view of the mount, but it was packed with Japanese tourists and excitable guides. We found a small table inside, where I ordered the famous cheese omelette while Tim opted for fresh oysters. The service was slow, but when the omelette arrived, I understood the hype; it was light, creamy, and fluffy all at once. Delicious. After supper, we continued climbing the many stairs to the summit, taking in the countryside, which gleamed a burnished gold in the setting sun. As the tide was coming in, we hurried down and strolled along the dark-grey sand beach. We took photos in various lounging poses by an old boat lying on its side, heading back along the pathway just as the sky filled with pastel colours. We finally reached our hotel at 10:30 p.m. The next day was hotter and infinitely busier, with busload after busload of tourists spilling into the car park. Again, the tide was out, so we hiked up through the town. Access to the Gothic Abbey is via the Grand Degré, a wide staircase of about 350 steps, once guarded by a swinging door. The buildings wrap around the rock like decorations pressed into a chocolate cake. At the top, the abbey church rests on crypts built directly into the rock. The monks constructed this masterpiece of Gothic architecture at the beginning of the 13th century; the dark, ornate buildings seem to claw at the blue sky. After taking in the panoramic views, we found the bus stop and made our way back to Paris.
