# Chapter 32: Greece 2013: The Journey Begins

Sod's law dictates that the day before leaving for our long-awaited trip to Greece should be as chaotic as possible! Tim had broken his left leg in a car accident just over six weeks prior. I almost cancelled the holiday—which included my sisters, Jax and Jo, as well as Tim and me—but his surgeon assured us he was okay to go gallivanting in the Greek isles as long as he kept his leg elevated, used crutches, and took his painkillers. Jax, Dan, Tim, and I visited Mum and my brother, John, for tea. Joseph, John’s so-called carer, decided this happy gathering was the perfect time to announce his resignation, effective July 1st. Mum didn't take the news well, with all of us about to go away. I reminded Joseph of his contractual obligations—at least a month's notice or a replacement—but that was where we had to leave it. John is wheelchair-bound after a diving accident and has been through so many ‘carers’ that he has threatened to write a book on the subject. Tim seemed to be managing on his crutches. We had our suitcases wrapped in cling film to prevent theft en route. I had paid for Tim to fly Business Class, so he and I boarded before my sisters. Once on board, I settled Tim into his seat then returned to ‘cattle class’ to join Jax and Jo. After some turbulence, I lurched up to Business Class to check on him; he was doing fine, enjoying a glass of bubbly and watching a big-screen movie. I stayed until mealtime, when I was politely asked to return to my eight-inch seat. I returned to Tim later, ostensibly to check on him but also to score some chocolate or champagne, but the steward muttered that Tim was old enough to manage by himself. I ignored him and sat in the neighbouring empty seat, where I promptly fell asleep. I woke half an hour later to find the steward hovering over me, asking me to return to my inferior seat. A wheelchair was arranged for the terminal, and we were whizzed through security. Jo beeped and had to be body-searched, and they were very suspicious of Tim’s Exogen contraption—a device that delivers low-intensity pulsed ultrasound to stimulate bone healing. I had to demonstrate how it worked, which involved Tim obligingly hoisting his knee and an unhealthy amount of conductive gel. After using the business lounge, I went in search of my sisters and realised my cell phone was missing. I rummaged frantically through all the bags like a madwoman before running to report it lost. They told me they had a white iPhone, but not my black one. I was miserable, knowing I’d need it to keep in contact with Mum and John. We looked like a bunch of refugees—Jax hijacking a small trolley piled high with gear, Jo clutching her oversized pillow from home —as we trundled toward the gate. Jo and Jax spent their time systematically gathering free perfume samples until they had accumulated almost a whole bottle's worth. 

Arrival in Athens 

Tim and I boarded the plane via a hoist lift—a small forklift-style truck. Once settled, I fretted about the wisdom of travelling with him so soon after surgery; he looked so pale. My own eyes were red and gritty, and my calves were swelling unbecomingly over my stockings like an undercooked soufflé. We touched down in 34-degree heat. The ultra-efficient porter wheeled Tim at a considerable pace, and we struggled to keep up. We were whisked through the Diplomatic Desk and straight to the luggage carousel to reunite with our ‘pupaed’ luggage. We were soon squeezed into a taxi with Spiros, who drove with a velocity reminiscent of the track at Kyalami. He informed us he had worked in Port Elizabeth in 1973; Jo mumbled that perhaps he had better keep his eyes on the road. He deposited us down a quaint side street with cobblestones and little shops. First observation: stairs! Lots of them. And a lift that didn't work. Tim’s room was down five steps, through a computer room, across a courtyard, and up five more into a compact, untidy room with bunk beds. It was shortly after our arrival that Dimitris accosted us on the street. He was a short, swarthy Greek who insisted his restaurant—also called The Plaka—offered free wine and ice cream to anyone staying at our inn. We returned to his restaurant that evening. Andreas served us, whisking away the tablecloth for a paper one for us ‘dribbling plebs’. We feasted on lamb shank, Greek salad, olives, and bread. Later, I injected Tim with Clephane to prevent clotting; a roommate walked in just as I was brandishing the needle, causing him to back out of the room before I could explain that I wasn't injecting anything illegal. The next day, we met Georgios (‘Call me George’), who insisted on taking us to his tourist office for strong coffee. He gazed soulfully into Jo's eyes while inquiring about our budget. Jax briskly informed him we were taking the ferry to the island of Ios tomorrow and that our cash flow was meagre. George quickly withdrew his caressing fingers. He offered to drive us to the ferry dock at 6:15 a.m. for €25, which we agreed to, shaking on the deal with a kiss on the cheek. Next thing I knew, it was 5:20 a.m. We dragged our luggage out to the pavement to wait for George’s taxi. The receptionist told us the fare was too much—it should be €16–20—so we challenged George when he arrived. He merely shrugged, stepped on the accelerator, and squealed around corners to Piraeus Harbour. The woman behind the desk was snappy, telling us we had to be at Gate Nine and that the ferry would leave in ten minutes. I grabbed my glasses off her nose and ran, leaving Jax, Jo, and Tim to struggle with seven bags and the crutches. I made it to the gangplank just as the official blew his whistle. ‘My sisters and son are coming! Please wait!’ I bellowed. The official ignored me. Jax spotted a man on a moped, hopped on the back, and roared at him to fetch Tim. The official proceeded to raise the gangplank. In desperation, I lunged for it and clung to the rail as it was winched up, while hundreds of passengers stared at the spectacle. A moment later, the moped engine roared up, and the official, knowing he was beaten, lowered the gangplank again. We were ushered unceremoniously on board, stumbling over luggage and tyres, looking for seats. Tim’s leg began to swell, so I looked for seats where he could elevate it. A small family of three occupied the space opposite a pair of empty seats. I smiled and mimed our predicament, but the woman just stared blankly. Jo asked the bartender to speak to her in Greek, but it turned out she was Bulgarian. I stood up and through a series of intricate mimes, attempted to explain that my son had broken his leg. I thumped my own swollen limb and made swinging movements with my arms, causing the couple to shrink into their seats in terror. 'He needs to raise his leg,’ I continue. I try to raise my own leg and nearly fall over. ‘So, you see, after the plane journey,’ a quick whirling motion of my arms to illustrate a plane, 'He needs to sit on this chair, I point to the empty seat behind me, 'And put his leg on this seat!’ I point to the occupied seat in front. This story proves a huge challenge for me and my arms and legs flail around wildly as I pantomime the events leading up to this desperate request. Their combined look of bewilderment has turned to actual fear as I reach the climax of my explanation. I’m lifting my leg up and down next to the young man, who is practically sitting in his mothers lap in an attempt to remove himself from this mad woman. It’s at this point that Jo returns with Tim in tow. Tim has time to witness his mother swivelling her leg over a terrified family of three who are all cowering into the corner. Tim smiled wanly at the terrified audience, then pointed to his swollen limb, complete with its surgical scars. He calmly eased himself into one of the seats and lifted his leg onto the recently vacated chair; the woman visibly relaxed, smiled, and nodded in sudden comprehension. I sighed in relief, Jo grinned, and we all spent a moment nodding and smiling—the crisis was averted. In truth, I was simply plain exhausted and dry-mouthed. Order restored, I left Tim to find some water and joined my sisters, who had found empty seats in a much nicer part of the ferry where a gentle breeze cooled our sweating brows. We enjoyed the comfort of these bow-seats until we docked at the first island where three people suddenly loomed over us, pointing to the seats. We had to move again. I was thoroughly annoyed—we had actually booked tickets, after all, and were not merely a bunch of squatting pirates! Being thirsty and desperate for a ‘wee’, I went in search of a toilet. The ferry loo consisted of a small, cramped cubicle on the side of the upper deck. I entered and sat down, but the door suddenly swung open; a young girl with a green complexion stood swaying, her hand over her mouth. I was in immediate danger of being puked on, as well as being exposed to the elements and casual passers-by. With one fluid motion, I was up, my clothing replaced, and I flung myself swiftly past the girl, narrowly missing the first jet of projectile vomit. Shakily, I made my way back to my seat and sat there quietly until we docked at the next island. The morning’s shenanigans had utterly exhausted me. I took a look over the side of the ferry as we docked briefly; the sea was a beautiful, deep azure blue—the kind only found in the Mediterranean. More seat-hopping ensued, so we decided to just stand near Tim until we finally docked at Ios, which looked beautiful in its bright white-and-blue colours. As I disembarked, I noticed a man holding a sign that read, ‘Francesco’s’. This was our accommodation, so we joined him gratefully. He introduced himself, and we piled ourselves and our luggage into his 4x4, setting off upwards on narrow, steep, dusty streets towards the top of the island. On arrival, we were greeted warmly by Francesco himself and handed glasses of cold water, which we sipped while gazing down at the blue sea below. The accommodation had whitewashed walls, white-painted stone paths, and bright blue shutters—typically Greek. Bursts of colour from bougainvillea bushes broke up the stark white. We were introduced to Captain ‘Haddock’ (I never did get his real name, but he certainly looked the part) and his wife, Maria, along with their daughter and a woman named Beth, who was warm and friendly. After unpacking, we trooped downstairs to find a cool breeze, sitting at a large wooden table overlooking the harbour and the silver sea. Maria regaled us with the story of her broken arm, which she sustained while dancing on a table after a few too many glasses of ouzo. Jax, Jo, and I went for a little look around the village, only five minutes away, wandering through twisting, whitewashed stone paths and alleyways snaking around blue-and-white boutiques. That afternoon, Jax gave us an impromptu Pilates lesson on the verandah while we lay on towels under the hot sun. I kept falling over and eventually gave up when the smell of bacon wafted by while I was trying to maintain a stiff-legged pose. Tim chatted to Larry, an English guy who had recently fallen on one of the cobbled paths while drunk; he had two purple eyes and a bandaged nose. It was incredibly relaxing sipping our drinks, watching the scenery, and seeing new guests arrive. An hour later, we found a small restaurant tucked away from the busy area called L'Olio (The Olive Place). The decor followed the name: a soft olive colour with green leaf decorations on the walls. It was bright and cosy. Harries, the owner, showed us to a window seat and we ordered a carafe of red wine and a salad with cranberries, oranges, olive oil, and walnuts—it was delicious. After our sumptuous repast, we wended our way up the cobbled labyrinth of streets back to Francesco’s. 

Windmills and Homer’s Tomb

My sisters joined us for a traditional breakfast, after which we walked into the town square to hire a car. Jax chose a Nissan Micra. We piled in and drove towards the windmills—twelve in total, overlooking the small church of Agios Nikolaos. Two of the windmills had been restored; historically, they were used to grind grain, contributing to the island's agricultural economy. After gazing at the windmills, we set off for the south side of the island passing the popular Mylopotas beach. Jax drove all over the island stopping for photos. Oleander bushes abounded, and the deepest blue sea shimmered on either side of us. Jax stopped at Homer’s Tomb; the poet is said to have died on Ios and is buried on Plakoto, on a hill at the northernmost part of the island. A long pathway and stone cairns led us to the tomb. Tim struggled a bit on his crutches but made it to the top. I met a French couple descending and exchanged a brief ‘Bonjour’. Homer’s marble tomb had been desecrated a few years prior, which was disappointing, but it was still fascinating to imagine his early life here. It was still raining when we left and continued up the cliffside road, passing many small church-like buildings with crosses, looking like islands ‘floating’ in the sky. I discovered I had been wandering around with two long streaks of dried toothpaste on my cheek all morning; naturally, no one had bothered to tell me! A trip down to the beach was on the cards, as it was already hot by 9 a.m. The sea was gorgeous and inviting. At reception, we bumped into Larry, who proved to be a regular mine of unsolicited information. He told us he once met Cat Stevens, whose father apparently lived on Ios. He also spun a yarn about a huge ocean liner that turned into the harbour, forgot to stop, and crashed into several boats before running aground on the beach. Larry rattled this off without drawing breath, and while some of it was interesting, I felt exhausted just listening to it all. After a quick wash, we met in reception to watch the sunset, joined by Larry and his friend Mark from the UK. Mark works on the railways and paints in watercolours; he was kind enough to give us each a miniature painting. Captain Haddock joined us briefly, and Larry told us he had great standing on the island. He also mentioned that when the Turks occupied the island and banned the Greek flag, locals painted their windows with the flag's colours— which is why the houses have their distinctive appearance today. He claimed old ladies were sent to greet incoming ships; if they didn't return, the locals knew it was a pirate ship and prepared for an attack. When he told us that the island of Santorini caused a tsunami and the parting of the Red Sea, I knew he was past the point of no return with his storytelling. (I heard later that a significant tsunami was triggered by a large earthquake in 1956, causing waves up to 25 metres high on nearby islands, so he wasn't entirely making it up, though I’m still not sure about the Red Sea!) I woke up several times during the night to the sound of great revelry. Hateful, hedonistic youth. A more appealing sound of church bells followed this morning. I got ready so quickly I didn't even have time to draw on my eyebrows before leaving for the harbour. Jax jogged there, and I walked down with Jo after stopping for coffee at the ‘Pleasure’ restaurant. I was watching a young guy nearby with crutches when, mid-sip, I glanced down and spied a disembodied hand under the table, jerking away in surprise and spilling half my coffee. The very able-bodied guy approached us holding the crutches and explained that the hand belonged to his friend, Jack, who ‘wasn't feeling very well’—a euphemism for ‘blind drunk’. His Aussie friend tried unsuccessfully to revive him until the restaurant owner stepped in and poured a glass of very cold water over his head. Jack sat up, groaning, and his friend helped him stagger across the road with his trousers and underpants at half-mast. The pool water was a lovely blue and warm to the touch. I cautiously lowered myself in, but when Jo glided over and tried to get me to float, I wasn't having any of it; I made some vague, half-swim movements, chin elevated and one toe firmly on the bottom. After hoisting myself out, we had lunch at the Moonlight restaurant —a beautiful day, with a deep blue sea and a matching sky. That evening, Tim and I sat at a large wooden table overlooking the harbour to gaze at the sunset. The Lord Byron is nestled between a few pubs in a small courtyard; some large, striped umbrellas attempted to keep pigeon droppings from decorating our food. Our pretty waitress from Morocco was quite bossy, barking orders to all and sundry. When Jax tried to help her pin down the napkins, the waitress snippily asked if she wanted a job there, and when Tim asked for no tomatoes, she flatly refused. Her tip was diminishing by the minute! The two stooges, Marc and Larry, arrived and were warmly greeted by ‘Helga the Horrible’. Jo and I watched a lone diner eat a ginormous plate of spaghetti; he had brought his own colourful napkin, which he tied under his chin before carefully winding mounds of pasta around his fork and slurping the lot in one go. It was fascinating. Our repast ended with huge bowls of crème brûlée and glasses of raki—a strong, milky-white Turkish beverage often called ‘lion’s milk’. The next day, we took a quad bike back to Mylopotas beach—Jax managed to get it started—and stopped at the Petradi Hotel for breakfast. Before we left Ios, Maria gave Tim and me a jar of their own honey, and we sat in the square at Cafe Tropicana drinking milkshakes. 

Santorini: The Black Sand Beach 

I dragged my big case to reception and took the small bus down to the harbour with Francesco. He hugged each of us before depositing us at the café. At 10 a.m., our ferry—a huge green vessel of the Hellenic Seaways line—arrived. Like a floating hotel, it was spacious and comfortable. From First Class, I watched the approach to Santorini; white limestone stalactites clung to the volcanic rock above a navy sea. We docked, and I spotted a short, grey-haired man holding a sign reading ‘Anna Hostel’. We piled into his small van with a couple from New Zealand. The architecture here was noticeably different —less pretty than Ios, with more brown stone buildings and semi-circular roofs. We climbed the steep road to the village of Emporio, near our destination, and then headed down toward Perissa beach, famous for its black sand. Tim’s dorm was a large, simply decorated room with twenty-five bunk beds, while our room was five minutes away in a white-and-blue building. After lunch, we left Tim to rest and went in search of a pottery shop we’d seen earlier. Jax marched ahead, while Jo and I trudged slowly behind—it was far too hot, and we were far too unhealthy to walk fast. We walked for several kilometres in the blistering sun, weaving through narrow, narrow streets with concrete ruins and litter. Eventually, Jo and I collapsed against a lamppost, only to see Jax bouncing along ahead of us. This set us off into a bundle of hysterical laughter—which only increased when my bottom lip got caught on my dry teeth, resulting in a macabre rictus. Later, we hired quad bikes and headed to Akrotiri to see the ancient Bronze Age city, buried by a massive volcanic eruption around 1600 BCE. It was remarkably preserved, offering a window into sophisticated urban life with multi-story buildings, advanced plumbing, and exquisite frescoes—I found it fascinating that women wore transparent tops and nail polish even back then! After visiting the Red Beach, we returned to our hostel, where I finally managed a few strokes in the pool with my ten toes elevated—a momentous occasion that required an immediate email home. 

The Petrol Incident 

As we set off for the black sand beach on our ATV, Tim noted the tank was empty; someone had clearly siphoned it overnight. I set off on foot to the petrol station, but as I walked back with a bottle of fuel, a car came careening down the narrow road straight at me. I lost my balance and tumbled, strewing my purse, hat, and helmet into the verge. I ran the remaining 300 metres back to Tim, nursing a gash on my knee. Disaster struck again when I realised my purse—containing my euros, credit card, and a memory card with all our Ios photos—was gone. I felt incredibly low. We eventually reached Fira, where I found I could still withdraw money from my savings account. I even treated myself to a ‘fish spa’, where ‘big fish’ nibbled the dead skin off my toes—a ticklish but not unpleasant experience. I gave my paper ‘fish-spa’ shoes to Tim, who had no footwear, making him look quite the fashion statement with his crutches and bandana! We spent the afternoon walking through Fira and visiting the archaeological museum, where I saw beautiful murals and a gold figurine of a bull. Later, we drove to Oia, stopping at Santo Wines to enjoy a six-glass tasting special overlooking the caldera. We explored the Lignos Folklore Museum and learned about the famous Santorini ‘cave houses’—carved into the pumice-rich rock to keep cool in summer, warm in winter, and highly resistant to earthquakes. As our trip concluded, we sat at the ‘Pleasure’ restaurant on the beach for one last coffee. Our taxi driver was somewhat nervous, crossing himself several times before launching the car down the steep hill to our awaiting ferry, bringing our Greek island adventure to a close. The Adamantios Korais is a large orange ferry boat. We’re a little early, so we sit at a nearby restaurant for breakfast. Tim tells us he has only slept for four hours as he’d gone to a Full Moon party on the beach. I’m glad he did. It can’t have been much fun trailing around after his mum and two aunties on crutches this trip. I spend most of the time onboard leaning out of the porthole window, taking photographs of each island as we approach. I wake Tim up and help him from the floor to the couch. At Folegandros, the water is a beautiful deep blue colour. Next is Kimolos. Unfortunately, the stop at Sifnos island brings a million people on board, and a brassy blonde woman with three kids tries to get Tim to give up his couch until I intervene, saying he is injured and has taken ‘many drugs’. She sighs and moves on. Jax, Jo and I remain seated in our chairs- we’ve already experienced a lack of seating despite paying when we arrived in Ios. An old man is sitting opposite me since we boarded at Sifnos. Unfortunately, he smells strongly of goat. I can’t rest because foul-smelling fumes keep wafting over. I’m convinced he bathed in goats milk this morning and probably has his pockets full of goats feta. I love old people though and smile at him. He throws a brown toothed smile back and I quickly close my eyes. It’s dark and very humid on arrival at the port of Athens late. In the evening with hundreds of people milling about. We find a taxi that is large enough to take us and all our luggage to Centrotel. A hotel, as the name implies, is a centrally located hotel, which should be very useful. The following day dawns bright and hot, so we take a walk to Central Square after breakfast. The Acropolis and Parthenon are really magnificent in their ancient splendour, and one can only imagine the life that was led when everything was new and intact. We wander on narrow paths and gaze at history until thirst claims our attention, and buy bottles of water for a tidy sum. The parthenon was dedicated to the goddess Athena and is famous for having no straight lines. Its architects, used subtle "optical refinements" to make the massive structure appear perfectly symmetrical to the human eye. The ruins are fascinating and we really enjoy the walk around but soon it is time to leave and head down through the little village in search of coffee. When we find a place, we sit there for nearly two hours, sipping our beverages and chatting about our Greek experience. Our taverna table is outside near a beautiful red brick church so we order lamb and various other meat dishes and watch the world go by. The villagers are all out in their family groups with children running up and down screaming excitedly. After a carafe of red wine with our meal we unwisely decide to finish off with a glass of Ouzo each. They come in small round glasses as a double. I take a photograph as each of us tries a sip. The expressions are classic grimaces. Tim throws his away, but the rest of us finish ours. We end up walking around the streets in almost complete darkness and completely lost. We’re giggling like hyenas as we stumble along the road. Eventually I flag down a taxi to take us back to the hotel. The following morning most of us are sporting headaches and dark glasses. We’re tired and hungover but what a great holiday. Despite the obvious difficulties, I think it helped heal and strengthen Tim’s leg.
