# Chapter 32: Greece 2013: The Journey Begins

Sod's law dictates that the day before leaving for our long-awaited trip to Greece should be as chaotic as possible because, well, that's Sod's law!

Tim broke his left leg in a car accident just over six weeks ago. I almost cancelled the holiday that included my sisters, Jax and Jo, as well as Tim and me. A visit to his surgeon last week established that as far as Dr X was concerned, he was okay to go gallivanting in the Greek isles, as long as he kept his leg elevated, used his crutches and took the strong painkillers he had been given – but he cautioned, not too many. So, somewhat hesitantly after hearing this, we decided to go ahead.

Jax, Dan, Tim and I visited Mum and my brother, John, for tea. Joseph, John’s so-called carer, decided this happy family gathering was a good time to announce his resignation as of 1st July. Mum didn’t take the news well, as was to be expected, with all of us going away. I informed Joseph of his contractual obligations and reminded him that he needed to give us at least a month's notice or at least find his replacement. And that was pretty much how we had to leave it. John you’ll remember is wheelchair-bound after a diving accident that left him paralysed with only some use of his left arm and limited use in his right. He has been through so many ‘carers’ he has threatened to write a book on the subject.

Tim seemed to be managing, negotiating his way around on his crutches. We had our suitcases wrapped in cling film to try to prevent theft en route.

I’d paid for Tim to fly Business Class, so he and I were lucky to be able to board earlier than my sisters. Once on board, I settled Tim into his spacious seat then returned to cattle class to join Jax and Jo. We had some turbulence after take-off, but as soon as I could, I unbuckled my seatbelt and lurched up to Business Class to see how Tim was doing. Well, he was doing fine, thank you! He had his first glass of bubbly and was lying right back, watching something on his big screen. I stayed with him until mealtime, when I was politely asked to go back to my seat. I reluctantly returned to my eight-inch seat and waited for our meal, which took ages to arrive. Jo took Tim his painkiller and reported that he was busy scoffing roast duck with sautéed veg and probably caviar! Ours was some ‘chicken-or-beef’ affair and pudding. I scoffed mine then eyed Jax’s cheese hopefully. She obligingly handed over her finger of cheddar. I was worrying about how Mum and John were getting on. It really was terrible timing to have Joseph leave right then. Colin, meanwhile, was about to enter the deep jungle in search of bearded pigs.

I returned to Tim, ostensibly to see how he was but also to see if I could score a craft chocolate or a sip of champagne, but the steward muttered that Tim was old enough to manage by himself. I ignored him and sat with him in the neighbouring empty seat. It was so comfortable that I fell asleep and woke up half an hour later, with blurred eyes and drooling, to find the steward bending over me and asking me to return to my inferior seat.

A wheelchair was quickly arranged from business class to the terminal and then we were whizzed through to x-rays as Tim had the presence of mind to grab some ‘fast track’ forms allowing us all to hurry behind him.

Usual palaver at X-rays. Jo was beeping and had to be body searched, and they were very suspicious of Tim’s Exogen contraption so I had to show them how it was used which involved an obliging knee up from Tim and an unhealthy amount of gel which took me a while to remove (some plane serviettes did the trick).

Col thoughtfully arranged the Exogen which is a device that delivers low-intensity pulsed ultrasound to fracture sites, which helps stimulate bone healing. It’s also portable which is not only useful but necessary. We followed the pleasant Indian fellow through to the business lounge. Unfortunately only Tim and I could use the lounge so Jo and Jax had to trundle off to look for some seating in the airport lounge. Tim’s leg had swollen up quite considerably so I pushed some chairs together so he could lie down, elevate and rest his leg. I gave him a pain pill and finished off with an Exogen treatment.

Afterwards I left the VIP lounge in search of my sisters, simultaneously discovering that my cellphone was missing so I rummaged frantically through all the bags like a crazy woman before running to a counter to report that I’d left it on the plane. They told me they had a white iPhone, but not my black one. I was miserable knowing I’d really need the phone to keep in contact with Mum and John.

We decided to go for a short walk with Tim hobbling along on his crutches behind us. Jax hijacked a small trolley and we piled all our stuff onto it until she looked like a small bag lady. Jo walked alongside clutching her oversized pillow from home. We looked like a bunch of refugees so nothing new. In the meantime Jo had found out that her favourite perfume was being marketed along the entire floor and samples were handed out. She and Jax walked around systematically oohing and aahing and getting free samples until they'd accumulated almost a bottle worth of sachets. Jo came with me to try to snaffle a few more but they recognised her and her attempt at seeming to have ‘just discovered’ the samples was ignored.

We were caught unawares when a porter arrived just after 9 for Tim and we had to scrabble around, packing away stuff. Tim and I joined the porter to the hoist lift – a bit like a small forklift truck that took us to the plane. We were lifted to the top and I settled Tim into business class again.

I fretted about the wisdom of travelling with him so soon after his surgery and gazed down at him and frowned – he looked so pale. The travel must have been taking it out of him. I tucked the blanket around him and returned to my seat.

This plane was a bit roomier than the last one which was useful for all the hand luggage we had.

My eyes were red and gritty, my nose was stuffy and my calves were swelling unbecomingly over my stockings like an undercooked soufflé.

The rest of the flight went without incident but I fell asleep again twenty minutes before landing and woke with a start as we made touchdown. Jax had taken a most unflattering photo of me dribbling onto my arm. The heat hit us all as we disembarked onto the hot tarmac. It must have been at least 34 degrees Celsius. The ultra efficient Indian porter was wheeling Tim at a considerable pace and we struggled to keep up. A pale-faced Tim was clutching the sides of the wheelchair as we arrived at the Diplomatic Desk. The porter garbled something to the official and miraculously the four of us unlikely looking ‘diplomats’ were whisked to x-rays where my nice little bottle of Robert Olivier Rasteau wine was confiscated. We grabbed our bags and DC papers and were whizzed off to the luggage carousel to reunite with our ‘pupaed’ luggage.

I struggled to keep up with the porter, as this superman of efficiency marched off towards the exit and hailed a taxi. Before you can say, ‘Baclava, hold the honey,’ we were sitting in a taxi with our mountain of luggage squeezed like sausage meat into the boot, while our sluggish brains tried to catch up.

Tim sat grim-faced in the front, clutching his crutches, while Spiros set off with a velocity reminiscent of the track at Kyalami. He turned round to ask us where we were from.

‘Ah, South Africa!’ said Jax.

He beamed and turned round again to inform us that he'd worked in Port Elizabeth in 1973. We assured him there'd be a few changes if he ever went back and Jo mumbled that perhaps he'd better keep his eyes on the road.

I read him the written directions to the ‘Plaka student and Travellers Inn’ where we were to spend the night.

Oleander bushes whizzed by and quite a few semi-derelict houses. Spiros deposited us down a short side street with all our luggage before setting off to collect some more victims.

The street was rather quaint, with pebble-cobbled roads, vendors selling their wares and little shops tucked away off street. First observation as we arrived, they had stairs! Lots of stairs. And a lift that didn't work.

Tim’s room was down five steps, through a computer room, across the courtyard and up five more steps into a small, compact and extremely untidy room with four bunk beds, which were all in use bar one which was luckily a bottom bunk. He lay down immediately and lifted his swollen limb up on some pillows. The Inn was situated down a pretty side street but it was busy with both traffic and pedestrians. A lot of interesting looking restaurants we noticed happily, which would come in handy. It was shortly after this that Dimitris accosted us on the street.

He was a short, swarthy Greek who told us apropos of nothing that his restaurant was also called the Plaka. He said he gave free wine and ice cream to anyone staying at the Student and Travellers Inn and we promised him we’d return for supper.

Later we looked for somewhere to eat and decided to go back to the Plaka.

Andreas served us after whisking away the tablecloth and putting down a paper tablecloth for us dribbling plebs. We amused ourselves watching a black cat play under the next table. Andreas returned with a carafe of red wine. ‘On the house’, we heard excitedly. Supper was a mezze of delights; lamb shank, Greek salad, olive oil, glistening green olives, sheep’s milk feta (okay, this one’s not for me) and large bread rolls. We all tucked in with great enthusiasm and drank a toast to the beginning of the holiday. Later Tim and I returned to his room and I injected him with Clephane to help prevent clotting and this was when I was found brandishing a large needle and syringe by one of his roommates who walked in just as I finished.

He nodded at us uncertainly then backed out of the room before I could tell him I wasn’t injecting anything illegal.

Jo, Jax and I were doing some window shopping up a small narrow side street when we were suddenly accosted by Georgios (‘Call me George’). He assured us that he could help us with whatever we needed. He grabbed Jo's hand and gazed deeply into her eyes, which he did with ease, being only around five foot tall. He insisted on taking us into his tourist office and seating us at his large wooden desk while he made us each a cup of coffee. We sipped the small glass cups of strong coffee gingerly and told George where we needed to be.

He stroked Jo's paw some more and gazed soulfully into her eyes while enquiring of our budget. Jax briskly informed him we were taking the ferry tomorrow to the island of Ios. She told him our budget and looked pointedly at his caressing fingers. At hearing how meagre our cash flow was, he quickly withdrew his plump fingers.

He then leaned confidentially towards Jax and whispered that he could take us to the ferry dock early tomorrow for only €25. Jax nodded, this seemed reasonable so we agreed he could collect us. He wrote down the details on a small notepad and promised us, with a last quick gaze into Jo’s eyes, that he’d be at our hostel at 6:15 tomorrow morning.

I was worried this would be too late but he insisted otherwise. We shook on this, each one receiving a kiss on the cheek before wandering back to the hotel chatting excitedly of the adventure awaiting us. Jo gave me one of her sleeping tablets when they returned, crackling with packets. I put some ear plugs in and I think I must have dozed off because….

…..next thing it was 5:20 a.m. and time to get up and dressed.

I walked to Tim and helped him get ready then we dragged all our luggage onto the pavement outside and stood bleary-eyed, waiting for George’s taxi. The guy at reception told us the fare was too much. He added somewhat smugly that it should be between €16-20. I asked him to telephone George and find out where they were. At 6:20 the taxi arrived and we alternately shoved luggage at him, remonstrating his tardiness and told him we heard he had quoted too much, this didn't seem to perturb Georgios ‘Con-the-lot-of-us’ and he merely shrugged, stepped on the accelerator and in less than ten minutes, we arrived at Piraeus harbour office with a squeal of tyres and were disgorged onto the pavement with a mountain of luggage. I ran inside to sort out the ferry tickets. The woman behind the desk was snappy and short-tempered and irritably borrowed my glasses to read the booking number. She looked up and said we had to be at Gate nine and when I ventured to ask where this was, she waved vaguely in the direction behind the office. As I turned to leave, she said,

‘The ferry will leave in ten minutes’.

I turned to rush out then returned to the desk and plucked my glasses off her nose and rushed to inform the forlorn looking group that we had tickets but less than ten minutes to find Gate nine.

I ran on ahead, leaving Jax, Jo and Tim to make their way as fast as they could… which wasn't very fast, seeing as they had seven pieces of luggage and a lad on crutches. Each time I thought I'd arrived at the right gate, it turned out to be the wrong one until, at last, puffing like a steam train, the sign for gate nine appeared, and I was just in time to hear the official at the gangplank blow his whistle. I ran up to the port authority and wheezed at him that we were supposed to be on board.

‘My sisters and son are coming. Please wait!’ I bellowed up at him.

The official continued to untie the rope and muttered that we were too late.

He blew his whistle to emphasise its imminent departure. I pleaded for him to wait ‘just two more minutes’. At this point Jax arrived dragging most of the luggage while Jo, her giant pillow and Tim were still a block away.

I was still begging him to wait a few more minutes, 'Por favor' when Jax spotted a man on a moped who had just dropped someone off. Without hesitating, she hopped on the back of his bike and bellowed at him to go fetch Tim. He was too astonished to put up any argument and they sped off. The official, having disregarded my pleas, (probably because I was speaking Spanish), told me they were leaving and proceeded to raise the gangplank. In desperation I lunged for it and clung onto the rail as it was slowly being winched up. I noticed the stares of hundreds of passengers watching as this tragedy unfolded before them (and everyone loves a Greek tragedy, right). I tried to gain public sympathy and shouted,

‘Help!’

But my fingers were turning red and threatened to loosen their tenuous grip when we all heard a cry and the sound of a tortured moped engine as it roared up to the gangplank. Turning slightly, I saw the hapless moped rider had returned with Tim and his crutches. Jax and Jo brought up the rear, steaming with exertion and dragging three huge cases on wheels, two rucksacks and a giant pillow.

The crowd cheered and the officer, knowing when he was beaten, blew the whistle again. The gangplank was lowered and my fingers prised off the rail as it was gently but noisily lowered to the ground again. We were ushered unceremoniously on board, stumbling over luggage, tyres and probably farm implements while the officer told us what he thought of us in no uncertain terms and left us to shuffle around the lower decks looking for seats. We found some empty seats and settled in for the journey. It wasn't long afterwards that some people came up to us and claimed the seats as theirs. They waved proof under our noses in the shape of ticket stubs, so we apologised and wandered off to find more empty seats, then had to move again. This happened three times. The ferry chugged forward with the odd belch of bunker fuel fumes. People were gathering at the rails and looking out towards the passing islands. The first one was Aegina, where the famous Aphea Temple is found. The second island, Agistri, which is a short ferry ride from Aegina.

Tim’s leg was beginning to swell up again, so I decided to look for seats that had an empty seat opposite so that he could raise his leg.

After wandering around for a while I found two adjacent seats that were empty. A small family of three sat opposite each other. I smiled hopefully at the mother and asked if one of them could move to the seat next to me and keep the vacated seat for Tim. With no comprehension on the woman's blank face, I tried again, speaking very slowly and enunciating each word with care, naturally this didn't make a whit of difference. Jo had arrived by this time and quickly assessed the situation. She looked for the bartender and asked her to speak to the woman in Greek. The woman served a few customers then approached us, wiping her hands on a damp tea cloth. She spoke rapid Greek to the woman and waited for her response. The woman just looked mystified. It turned out she was Bulgarian and didn't understand a word either of us were saying. The bar lady shrugged and retreated to the bar.

In desperation I stood up, and through a series of intricate mimes attempted to explain our predicament.

‘My son,’ I began, raising my hand above my head to indicate that he was of some considerable height,

‘Has broken his leg,’

and here I mimed a broken leg by thumping on my own swollen limb, I made several swinging movements to indicate his subsequent need for crutches, and the couple shrank into their seats.

'He needs to raise his leg,’ I continued.

I tried to raise my own leg and nearly fell over.

‘So, you see, after the plane journey,’ a quick whirling motion of my arms to illustrate a plane,

'He needs to sit on this chair,’

I pointed to the empty seat behind me,

'And put his leg on this seat!’

I pointed to the occupied seat in front.

This story proved a huge challenge for me and my arms and legs flailed around wildly as I pantomimed the events leading up to this desperate request. Their combined look of bewilderment had turned to actual fear as I reached the climax of my explanation. I was lifting my leg up and down next to the young man, who was practically sitting in his mother's lap in an attempt to remove himself from this mad woman.

It was at this point that Jo returned with Tim in tow.

Tim had time to witness his mother swivelling her leg over a terrified family of three who were all cowering into the corner.

He smiled wanly at the terrified audience then pointed to his swollen limb, complete with scars and calmly eased himself into one seat and lifted his leg onto the recently vacated seat and the woman visibly relaxed. She smiled and nodded her sudden comprehension. I smiled in relief, Jo grinned, we were all nodding and smiling and all was good. Actually I was just plain exhausted and dry-mouthed.

Order restored, I left Tim to find some water then joined my sisters who had found some empty seats in a much nicer part of the ferry with a gentle breeze cooling our sweating brows. We enjoyed the comfort of these bow-seats until we docked at the first island and suddenly three people were looming over us and pointing to the seats. We had to look for another three seats. I was annoyed as we had actually booked seats and were not merely a bunch of pirates! I was thirsty and desperate for a wee and went in search of a toilet.

The ferry loo consisted of a small cramped cubicle on the side of the upper deck. I entered and sat down when suddenly the door swung open and a young girl with a green complexion stood swaying with her hand over her mouth. I was in danger of being puked on as well as being exposed to both the elements and casual passers-by. With one fluid motion, I was up, clothing replaced and flung myself swiftly past the girl, narrowly missing the first jet of projectile vomit. Shakily I made my way back to my seat and sat there quietly till we docked at the next island. The morning’s shenanigans had exhausted me.

I got up from my seat and took a look over the side of the ferry as we docked briefly at each island. The sea was a beautiful azure blue such as is only found in the Mediterranean.

More seat-hopping ensued and we decided to just stand near Tim until we finally docked at our destination at the island of Ios which looked beautiful in its bright white and blue colours. As I came off the ferry, I noticed a man holding up a sign with the legend, ‘Francesco’s’.

This was to be our accommodation so we joined him gratefully. He introduced himself and we piled the luggage and ourselves into his 4X4 and set off upwards on a narrow, steep, dusty street towards the top of the island. On arrival we were greeted warmly by Francesco himself and given a glass of cold water which we sipped gratefully while gazing over to the blue sea below. The accommodation had white-washed walls with white-painted stone paths and bright blue shutters. Typically Greek in style. Bursts of bright colour from bougainvillea bushes broke up the stark white.

We were introduced to Captain ‘Haddock’ (never did get his name but he looked the part) and his wife Maria, their only child, and a woman called Beth who was warm and friendly. After unpacking we trooped downstairs to get something cold to drink and to find a cool breeze then sat at a large wooden table overlooking the harbour and a silver sea, drinking ice-cold water. Maria was telling us about her arm which she broke while dancing on the table after a few too many glasses of ouzo. Jax, Jo and I went for a little look around at the nearby village only five minutes away. Many twisting whitewashed stone paths and alleyways snaked around blue and white painted boutique shops.

That afternoon Jax gave us all an impromptu pilates lesson on the verandah while lying on some towels under the hot sun. I kept falling over. I eventually gave up when I smelled bacon whilst trying to maintain a stiff-legged pose. Tim chatted to Larry, the English guy who recently fell on one of the cobbled paths when drunk. He had two purple eyes and a bandaged nose. It was so relaxing sipping our drinks, watching the scenery and new guests arrive. An hour later we came across a small restaurant tucked away from the busy area. It was called L’olio (the Olive place). The décor took direction from the name and was a soft olive colour with a green olive leaf decoration around the wall. It was very clean, bright and cosy. The owner showed us to a window seat and introduced himself as Harries. We ordered a carafe of red wine to share and variously, a salad with cranberries, orange and olive oil and walnuts – it was really delicious. The food was delicious. We all enjoyed ourselves and laughed a lot. We’d been given another free carafe of wine which added to the general festive atmosphere. After this sumptuous repast we all staggered out of there (three of us due to the consumption of wine and Tim because he was on his crutches), and wended our way up the cobbled labyrinth of streets back to Francesco’s.

My sisters joined us for a traditional brekky after which we walked into the town square to hire a car. Jax chose a Nissan Micra as she'd be the driver. We piled in and drove towards the windmills. There are twelve altogether, overlooking the small church of Agios Nikolaos. Two of the windmills have been restored. They were used to produce energy to grind grains thereby contributing to the agricultural economy and food requirements of the island. After gazing at the windmills for a bit we set off for the south side of the island passing the popular Mylopotas beach. Jax drove over most of the island, stopping here and there for some photos. Oleander bushes abounded and there was the deepest blue sea on either side. Jax stopped at Homer’s tomb. The poet Homer apparently died on Ios and is buried on Plakoto, on a hill at the northernmost part of the island. Cairns of stone and a long pathway led us to the tomb. Tim struggled a bit on his crutches but made it to the top. Goat droppings dotted the pathway as we wended our way up. I met a French couple descending and exchanged a brief, ‘Bonjour’. Homer’s marble tomb was desecrated a few years ago which is disappointing. He was considered one of the great poets of ancient Greece (The Iliad and the Odyssey are best known) so it was interesting being there and imagining his early life on what must have been quite a different island.

It was still raining when we left and carried on up the cliffside road where we passed many small church-like buildings with crosses, looking like small islands ‘floating’ in the sky.

I discovered I’d been wandering around with two long streaks of dried toothpaste on my cheek all morning. Naturally no one thought to tell me.

A trip down to the beach was on the cards as it was already quite hot at 9 a.m. The sea was a gorgeous azure blue and looked very inviting from the balcony. At reception we bumped into Larry who was a regular mine of information (most of it unsolicited). Some of his more interesting gems:

He met Cat Stevens once whose father apparently lived on Ios.

A huge ocean liner was turning into the harbour one day but forgot to stop, so it ended up crashing into several boats before finally coming aground on the beach.

Larry rattled off this information almost without drawing breath and whilst some of it was interesting, I felt exhausted trying to absorb it all.

After a quick wash we met in reception to watch the sunset sitting at one of the large wooden tables, joined by Larry and a friend of his who hailed from the UK, called Mark. He worked on the railways he informed us. He also painted in watercolours and we all received a miniature painting which was quite nice of him. Captain Haddock joined us briefly. He had great standing on the island according to Larry. He also mentioned that the Turks banned the Greeks from flying their flags when they’d taken over the island so locals painted the flag colours around their windows – giving them their distinctive appearance all over the island. He said they’d sent old ladies down to greet any new ships in the harbour – if they didn’t return they knew there were pirate ships and so were prepared for them. When he told us the island of Santorini caused a tsunami and the parting of the Red Sea – I knew he was past the point of no return with his storytelling.

I later heard that there was notably a significant tsunami triggered by a large earthquake in 1956 that caused waves up to 25 metres high on nearby islands and felt a little bad. Still not sure that this incident caused the parting of the Red Sea though.

I woke up several times during the night to the sound of great revelry. Hateful, hedonistic youth. A more appealing sound of church bells followed this morning. I got ready so quickly I didn’t even have time to draw on my eyebrows before leaving for the harbour. Jax jogged there, and I walked down with Jo after stopping for coffee at ‘Pleasure’ restaurant. I was watching another young guy standing nearby with crutches, when suddenly, mid-sip, I glanced down and spied a disembodied hand under the table and jerked away in surprise, spilling half my coffee. The very able-bodied guy approached us holding the crutches and explained that the hand belonged to his friend Jack, who ‘isn’t feeling very well’. This turned out to be a euphemism for ‘blind drunk’. His Aussie friend tried unsuccessfully to revive him, to no avail. It took the genius of the restaurant owner, who poured a glass of very cold water over his head, to get him to sit up, groaning. His friend helped him up, and they staggered across the road with his trousers and underpants at half-mast.

The pool water was a lovely blue colour and was warmish to the touch. I cautiously lowered myself into the pool. It wasn’t long before Jo glided over to me and tried to get me to float. I was not having any of it and instead made some vague, half-swim movements, keeping my chin well elevated and one toe firmly on the bottom of the pool. After hoisting myself up the side, I suggested we have lunch at Moonlight restaurant. It was a beautiful day, with a deep blue sea and a matching sky.

The sea was a beautiful navy blue as Tim and I sat at the large wooden table to gaze at the sunset. The Lord Byron was nestled between a few pubs. It was in a small courtyard with a few wooden tables outside. Some large, striped umbrellas attempted to keep pigeon droppings from decorating our food. Our pretty waitress was from Morocco. She had dark, curly hair and was very tanned. She was also very bossy and was barking orders to all and sundry. When Jax tried to help her pin down the table napkins, she somewhat snippily asked her if she wanted a job here and when Tim asked her not to add tomatoes with his meal, she said the chef wouldn’t be happy with that so no. Her tip was diminishing by the minute. The two stooges (Marc and Larry) arrived. They were warmly greeted by Helga the Horrible, who brought up chairs for them. Jo and I simultaneously noticed a lone diner eating a ginormous plate of spaghetti. He had brought his own colourful napkin, which he first tied underneath his chin. He then began fussily winding mounds of spaghetti around his fork, then bent his head and began slurping the lot in one go. His head didn’t move as he carefully shovelled kilometres of spaghetti through his pursed lips. It was fascinating to watch.

Our repast ended with huge bowls of crème brûlée and peaches and small glasses of raki to finish off the meal. Raki is a Turkish alcoholic beverage, also called ‘lion’s milk’ due to its milky-white appearance when diluted with water. I found it quite strong but not terrible.

The decision was made to return to Mylopotas beach today on the quad bike after Jax managed to get it started. It was a lovely, fresh day, so we stopped off at the Petradi Hotel for breakfast and lazed in a desultory fashion for a few hours.

Maria gave Tim and me a jar of their own honey before we left for the Square to sit at Cafe Tropicana, drinking milkshakes and green tea.

I dragged my big case down to reception then took the small bus down with Francisco. He enveloped each of us in a hug before depositing us at the harbour cafe. At 10 a.m. our ferry arrived – a huge green vessel, part of the Hellenic Seaways line.

The ferry was like a floating hotel – so comfortable and spacious. I watched the approach to the island from First Class. White limestone stalactites clung to the volcanic rock above a navy sea. We sailed slowly into the harbour and docked within minutes. Tim descended with his crutches via a lift while the rest of us were nearly trampled by the impatient crowd disembarking in their droves and nearly wilted in the heat while scanning for our driver. I spotted a short, grey-haired man holding up a sign reading ‘Anna Hostel’. We greeted each other and piled into his small van with another couple from New Zealand.

The architecture in Santorini is noticeably different from Ios. It’s less pretty, with more brown stone buildings that have semi-circular roofs. We climbed the very steep and narrow road to the top, then drove along another tarred road past some small villages until we reached the village of Emporio, close to where we were staying. Our driver headed down towards Perissa beach (famous for its black sand) to reach Anna Hostel, where we checked in. Tim’s dorm was situated further down the road from us; a large, simply decorated room with twenty-five bunk beds. It was strewn with luggage and clothing. Tim’s bunk was in the corner and was luckily the lower one. Our room was five minutes away in a white and blue building.

After lunch we left Tim lying with his foot up on my bed while the rest of us went in search of the pottery shop we saw from the van earlier. Jax marched ahead while Jo and I trudged slowly behind, it being too hot and us being too unhealthy to walk any faster. We walked for several kilometres in the hot sun. It wasn’t a pretty area either, with many concrete ruins, cracked pavements, litter, and abandoned rusty implements and the road was very narrow. We were both within a whisper of being run over a few times. Jax had jogged effortlessly ahead and as Jo and I collapsed against a lamppost, we looked up and saw her small figure bouncing up ahead. For some reason, this set us off and we collapsed in a bundle of hysterical laughter – laughter which increased when my bottom lip got caught on my dry teeth, resulting in a macabre rictus. Jo doubled over again. Eventually Jax turned back to find us screaming, red-faced with uncontrolled laughter.

Later, Jo, Jax, Tim and I hopped back onto our hired quad bikes and headed off to Akrotiri. Tim and Jax rode the bikes, Jo and I rode pillion. We wanted to see the ancient Bronze Age city (over 4,000 years old). It was very pleasant riding through the countryside with a small breeze to cool us off. I read that the women wore transparent tops and nail polish even in the Bronze Age, which I found fascinating.

Akrotiri is a remarkably preserved Bronze Age Minoan city buried by a massive volcanic eruption around 1600 BCE, freezing its multi-storey buildings, advanced plumbing, and exquisite frescoes in time. This thriving port town offers a unique window into sophisticated urban life with paved streets, drainage, and vibrant art before the catastrophic burial, and we walked through its ruins under a protective roof.

This used to be a prosperous trading hub with sophisticated urban planning, where multi-storey buildings (up to three storeys) with complex drainage systems.

Afterwards we set off for the Red Beach, a striking volcanic landscape of red cliffs, black sand, and vibrant turquoise waters not far from the Akrotiri archaeological site.

They had a small pool at the front of our room, so we all hopped in for a swim and, – red-letter day! – I actually managed a few strokes with all ten toes elevated. This was a momentous occasion and only occurred after huge encouragement from Jo, Jax and Tim. I was so pleased. An email was sent immediately to inform the family of my progress. Later, we all went down to the caldera in Megalochori to watch a beautiful sunset over the sea and sat on deckchairs, sipping cocktails. A lovely way to end the day.

Jax and I had decided to go to Fira, the other side of the island by bus, leaving Tim and Jo at the beach.

Back at the room, I tried the small pool again and practised some more swimming. (Well, I wouldn’t go as far as calling it swimming, but with a lot of frantic flapping that propelled me forward, it’s getting there). As I towelled off, I suggested we go to the black sand beach. Tim had come on the ATV, so I hopped on the back, and we set off. Tim said that the tank was empty – it seemed someone had siphoned it last night because we hadn’t used up a full tank in two days. He said we had better fill up first so we set off for the petrol station down the road. Only we didn’t quite make it. The bike suddenly ground to a halt midway. Everything went downhill from here. I grabbed my purse and helmet and set off down the road to the petrol station and asked them to fill a bottle with the promise that we would buy more once we got the bike started again. The petrol attendant said she had just heard from another disgruntled customer that their petrol was siphoned off as well. I left the station clutching my helmet, hat, purse and petrol bottle and hurried back to Tim when suddenly a car came careening down the narrow road straight at me. I tried to avoid it by sidestepping further onto the pavement and consequently lost my balance and tumbled, strewing everything into the verge. I was shocked and shaken but gathered all my things and ran the remaining 300 metres back to where Tim was still waiting. I had a big gash on my knee, and my left arm was very painful. Tim commiserated and filled the small tank before riding us down to the beach where he stopped at one of the restaurants to order coffee. They gave me a bag of ice for my knee which was swelling up painfully. As I went to pay, I fumbled around for my purse but couldn’t find it. Panicking, we looked in the bike's small compartment, but it wasn’t there either. I thought my purse must have fallen into the grassy verge when I was sent flying. Jax took me on her ATV to where I thought the spot was. A look along the road revealed nothing, so despondently, we returned to the restaurant.

I discovered that I had not only lost my euros but also my credit card and a memory card with all the photos from Ios on it. I was feeling very low. The card and money didn’t matter as much as all the memories and I continued searching in the dark with a patient Jax driving me up and down. I received frosty indifference from the hostel reception who just suggested I go to the police in Fira. Even I knew nothing would come of that. We met up with Tim and took a bus to Fira. Tim brought his crutches along. The day was pleasantly hot so we stopped for coffee and breakfast in the town centre. A Filipino waiter with a creased brow and a contradictory smile served us. Refreshed, we set off to look for the archaeological museum which houses a lot of the finds from Akrotiri; some very interesting murals with flower designs, animals, and blue-rimmed frescoes, and a beautiful gold figurine of a bull. We found an ATM, and I discovered I could still draw from my savings card even though it wasn’t cleared for international use.

After passing a fish spa, I was curious and turned back to enquire. The next minute I found myself seated in a box-like chair with my feet dangling into a square, glass fish tank. The Greek ‘therapist’ gazed at my feet for a moment and declared the need for the ‘big fish’. Big fish then swam around for a minute or two to acclimatise then started to nibble on my toes and heels. It wasn’t an unpleasant feeling, just a bit ticklish. They nibbled away at the dead skin for twenty minutes then I was given some paper shoes to wear. I paid €10 and joined the others who had been sitting outside. I gave Tim my paper shoes to wear because he was not wearing any and the cobblestones were hot. He looked a bit strange wearing paper shoes with crutches and a bandana around his head, but they served their purpose.

A restaurant was close by overlooking the caldera so we stopped for lunch. It had a beautiful view of the blue bay, the volcano, and the surrounding ‘croissant’ of Santorini. I sipped my coffee, enjoying the feel of the warm sunshine on my skin and the view while chatting in a desultory manner with my sisters before noticing an old photograph on the stone wall and got up to take a closer look. It showed the volcano eruption of 1950. The ‘new' Kameni volcano, a lava dome within the Santorini caldera erupted on January 10, 1950, and lasted until February 2, resulting in a powerful ash column ejecting incandescent bombs that created the Liatsikas dome. I wondered what happened to the old one.

We continued walking through the stony streets of Fira before taking a bus back to Perissa. Later, walking down to the black sand beach and along the promenade to paddle in the warm Aegean where the moon rose majestically from the sea. After collecting Tim at Anna Hostel, we set off for Oia (pronounced ‘ee-ah’). Jax was driving again, as neither Jo nor I felt confident enough. Our first stop was at the popular Boutari Winery, a three-hundred-year-old vineyard but it was closed, so we continued until we got to Santo Wines. Again we had a fantastic view of the caldera from the large, open verandah. The building looked old and had whitewashed stone walls, bright crimson bougainvillea and many small wooden tables and chairs on the wide verandah. There were quite a few tour buses which had disgorged hundreds of thirsty tourists. We chose the six-glass special, which came as three white wines and three red, with cheese and rusks. Oia was a nightmare drive for Jax with all the cars, buses and tourists milling around the roads but she handled it well. A small museum with the legend ‘Folklore’ caught our attention. The Lignos Folklore Museum was left untouched by the earthquakes of 1956, restored in 1973, and houses antique furniture, family heirlooms, old pottery, bedlinen, etc. It’s small and interesting and we spent a pleasant thirty minutes wandering around. A little further on, we came to some of the Santorini caves. The history of the Santorini cave houses came about due to intense poverty. The lowly crew members of ship owners and sea captains were not able to build their houses on top of the hill in Oia and came up with alternative housing. The peculiarity of the volcanic terrain offered a much cheaper alternative; allowing the inhabitants to carve a building into the earth. These cave houses were once the smallest homes on the island, standing in great contrast to the two-storey neoclassical mansions built by the wealthy ship owners. The rock contains pumice, a porous rock and a bad conductor of heat, allowing cave houses to be kept warm in winter and cool in summer. Perhaps most importantly, they showed high resistance to earthquakes. I use pumice stone on my dry heels at home.

Hundreds of years ago, inhabitants built windmills to take advantage of the ever-present wind and use it to grind and produce flour. The famous windmill of Oia is one of the more prominently featured landmarks of Santorini, adding an extra charm to the picturesque landscape and appearing in thousands of pictures. The island is small, and the roads are narrow and crowded, mainly with tourists, so we headed back via Perissa, where a visit to the ATM was made in order to withdraw another bucket of euros. It was a lovely, balmy evening with not much wind that day, so we stopped off at the beach for coffee at ‘Pleasure’ restaurant.

The taxi collected us and drove towards the port. We were somewhat nervous when he crossed himself several times before launching the car down the steep hill to our awaiting ferry.

The Adamantios Korais is a large orange ferry boat. We were a little early, so we sat at a nearby restaurant for breakfast. Tim told us he had only slept for four hours as he’d gone to a Full Moon party on the beach. I was glad he did. It can’t have been much fun trailing around after his mum and two aunties on crutches this trip. I spent most of the time onboard leaning out of the porthole window, taking photographs of each island as we approached. I woke Tim up and helped him from the floor to the couch. At Folegandros, the water was a beautiful deep blue colour. Next was Kimolos. Unfortunately, the stop at Sifnos island brought a million people on board, and a brassy blonde woman with three kids tried to get Tim to give up his couch until I intervened, saying he was injured and had taken ‘many drugs’. She sighed and moved on. Jax, Jo and I remained seated in our chairs – we’d already experienced a lack of seating despite paying when we arrived in Ios. An old man was sitting opposite me since we boarded at Sifnos. Unfortunately, he smelled strongly of goat. I couldn’t rest because foul-smelling fumes kept wafting over. I was convinced he bathed in goat’s milk this morning and probably had his pockets full of goat’s feta. I love old people though and smiled at him. He threw a brown-toothed smile back and I quickly closed my eyes. It was dark and very humid on arrival at the port of Athens late in the evening with hundreds of people milling about. We found a taxi that was large enough to take us and all our luggage to Centrotel. A hotel, as the name implies, is a centrally located hotel, which should be very useful.

The following day dawned bright and hot, so we took a walk to Central Square after breakfast. The Acropolis and Parthenon are really magnificent in their ancient splendour, and one can only imagine the life that was led when everything was new and intact.

We wandered on narrow paths and gazed at history until thirst claimed our attention, and bought bottles of water for a tidy sum. The Parthenon was dedicated to the goddess Athena and is famous for having no straight lines. Its architects used subtle "optical refinements" to make the massive structure appear perfectly symmetrical to the human eye.

The ruins were fascinating and we really enjoyed the walk around but soon it was time to leave and head down through the little village in search of coffee. When we found a place, we sat there for nearly two hours, sipping our beverages and chatting about our Greek experience.

Our taverna table was outside near a beautiful red brick church so we ordered lamb and various other meat dishes and watched the world go by. The villagers were all out in their family groups with children running up and down screaming excitedly. After a carafe of red wine with our meal we unwisely decided to finish off with a glass of ouzo each. They came in small round glasses as a double. I took a photograph as each of us tried a sip. The expressions were classic grimaces. Tim threw his away, but the rest of us finished ours. We ended up walking around the streets in almost complete darkness and completely lost. We were giggling like hyenas as we stumbled along the road. Eventually I flagged down a taxi to take us back to the hotel.

The following morning most of us were sporting headaches and dark glasses. We were tired and hungover but what a great holiday. Despite the obvious difficulties, I think it helped heal and strengthen Tim’s leg.
