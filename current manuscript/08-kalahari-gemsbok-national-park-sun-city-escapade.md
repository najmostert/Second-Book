# Chapter 7: Kalahari Gemsbok National Park / Sun City Escapade

The Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park is an amalgamation of the former Kalahari Gemsbok National Park. In 1999, a historic agreement between Botswana and South Africa was signed to co-manage the ecosystem as a single protected area. The Kalahari is a magnificent region where the iconic red sand dunes and low scrub fade into infinity and massive herds of gemsbok, springbok, eland and blue wildebeest instinctively follow the seasonal rains; it’s a place where imposing camel thorn trees provide vital shade for huge, black-maned Kalahari lions and the occasional secretive leopard can be seen prowling through the dry riverbeds. We were excited to explore it and set off early along the West Coast road from Cape Town. The drive became significantly more interesting as we approached the famous Namaqualand wildflower country; the town of Springbok in particular was beautiful, vibrant if stark against the arid landscape. It was still freezing cold, but we were in excellent spirits, imagining our coming days in the bush. At the gates of Twee Rivieren after a long, gruelling dirt-road drive through absolute nothingness, the camp looked stark and bleak but I knew from experience that the desert landscape is actually full of fascinating life. After a hurried cup of tea we set off for our first morning game drive and spot a pair of tiny pygmy falcons perched in a tree, springbok, magnificent gemsbok and then—the ultimate prize—a mother lion with two small cubs resting close to the road track. I took over the driving so Col could focus on taking photographs. Unfortunately, several other cars and safari vans arrived soon afterwards with many of the drivers wielding massive telescopic lenses that practically touched noses with the lions—an unfair advantage, especially when one of the large tour buses deliberately drove right off the track to park on top of the pride. We embarked on our second game drive of the day after lunch when the predators were most likely to have finished their midday naps. It proved highly disappointing, and we saw very little wildlife. One English chap roared to a screeching halt at the rest site where we were quietly munching on some crisps and demanded to know if we had seen anything at all, complaining loudly that he had driven sixty kilometres from ‘Twee’ and had seen ‘bugger all’. 
The park warden, on discovering that Col was a medical doctor, immediately approached our tent and asked if he would look at an old lady suffering from severe back pain. We set up camp first with Tim ‘helping’ Daddy put up the brand-new tent. It proved significantly less straightforward than the smooth demonstration given in the outdoor shop and Col was irritated. With his amazing ability to view the glass as half-empty, he grumbled that the structure wouldn't last the week. The desert wind ensured that absolutely everything was covered in a fine film of red Kalahari dust. Later on, Col went off to attend to the back pain victim, who turned out to be an American hypochondriac who had thrown her back out attempting to lift her incredibly heavy suitcase. She promptly inundated Col with a exhaustive list of physical complaints. He prodded around her spine a bit, offered some reassuring advice and left her with a packet of ibuprofen. Meanwhile, I washed my dust-encrusted hair and rinsed a couple of Tim’s cloth nappies, sealing them in a plastic bag before we set off on another game drive. There was absolutely nothing drinking at the solar-pumped waterholes so we drove through the red dunes until sunset. Despite a rough, windy night in the tent, we were determined to enjoy one final game drive before leaving the Kalahari Gemsbok Park and covered nearly eighty kilometres right up to the remote Union’s End section of the reserve where we were treated to the dramatic antics of two massive gemsbok at one of the salt pans. They were having a proper go at each other, aggressively locking their long, rapier-like horns and stirring up a magnificent cloud of white dust. When we returned to camp the heat was already stifling and desert sand was blowing everywhere. The couple camped directly across the track from us, having observed our earlier chaotic packing efforts, watched us like vultures—impatiently waiting for us to vacate our coveted spot under the shade of a large tree. I watched in a state of lethargic stupor as a newly arrived vehicle drove up and promptly got bogged down in the deep, loose sand right in front of our tent. They were eventually pushed free by a few helpful campers. It was therefore with a distinct sense of trepidation that I watched Col attempt to steer our own vehicle down the exact same track a few minutes later. Just as I was about to shout an urgent warning, the car ground to a sudden halt, tyres skidding helplessly in the boiling sand. The same group of unfortunate campers stepped forward once more to push him out. Speaking of pushing—the car’s starter motor had failed the previous morning and I had been forced to physically push-start the vehicle myself. To avoid a repeat performance Col had taken great pains the night before to reverse the car up a steep incline near the park ranger's residence, which sat on a bit of a rise but was located far away from our actual campsite. It was therefore a classic manifestation of Sod’s Law when in the grey light of dawn the female warden observed Col striding purposefully towards the vehicle— dressed in a heavy woollen jersey that was both inside-out and back-to-front—to watch him to climb inside and start the engine on the very first turn with no problem at all. By this point she decided that he was quite eccentric—an impression no doubt solidified by the sight of him frantically filling a brand-new plastic wash bag with handfuls of red Kalahari sand the day before ( works amazingly in preventing camera shake). In the early evening we set off out of the desert towards Johannesburg, the plan being that we would drive until about eleven or twelve o'clock at night, doss down in a roadside motel and then carry on to the city the following morning. Col drove steadily until about 1 a.m when we decided we needed a place to sleep. We arrived in the small mining town of Hotazel (pronounced ‘hot-as-hell)— only it wasn't hot at all; it was actually freezing cold. We drove around the dark streets failing to find a single establishment open and promptly renamed the place ‘Shitazel’, so we had no choice but to drive on to Kuruman arriving at about 2 a.m. Col asked a petrol attendant where the nearest hotel was and he directed us with a pointing finger to two of their ‘best’. One was completely locked up for the night and on no account were they going to let us in which left us with the other. We immediately realised that it doubled as the local brothel. Scantily dressed women paraded the street and a loud, thumping noise emanated from the disco inside. Under the circumstances I was only mildly upset and quite a bit relieved when Col announced that the chap at the desk was far too intoxicated to give us a room. Later we spotted a sign, ‘Leisure Place Lodges only 15 km out of Kuruman’, so we drove like the clappers only to discover it was closed—lock, stock and smoking barrel. It was now nearly 3 a.m. Another hotel advertised as being ‘just outside of town’. Col greased the night watchman’s palm and we all climbed gratefully into one bed and fell fast asleep. 
Next morning, Col and I had to be ready for the AGFA Awards function—the primary reason for us being in Johannesburg and there had to be one. We have our best, creased clobber ironed. Col found a babysitter for the evening called Ivy; an amiable lass if a bit short on neural activity. I had time to shower but not to wash my hair as it would have taken too long to dry. So with Tim happily under the watchful eye of Ivy we set off. Inside the venue, we’re offered champagne and orange juice and given name tags— just as well as I was so tired at that point that I might well have forgotten at least one of our names. Needless to say we didn’t know a soul so wandered about with vague smiles on our faces, trying to read the name tags of familiar-looking VIPs. The Mayor of Johannesburg was there as were Getaway magazine representatives and various press photographers. We nibbled on crisps, sipped wine and tried our best to look alert. I still had quite a bit of red Kalahari dust trapped in my hair and had to remind myself not to shake my head too vigorously lest it release little puffs of dust into the air. We trooped in to watch a slide presentation by Jill Sneesby. It was excellent and we recognised that much of the wildlife footage had been taken back at the Kalahari Gemsbok Park. Then came the big moment. All the winners of the AGFA competition were announced, among them Col for his underwater category. He received a sizeable cheque and a beautifully framed certificate. Afterwards we ambled through to see the display of winning photographs and partook of the delicious finger food. The SABC TV cameras were present and Col gave them a detailed description of his winning photograph. We struck up a conversation with two fellow photographers; Roger and his wife Pat, both of whom were on their way to Kenya. Pat regaled us with absolute horror stories about Sodwana Bay where they had recently spent some time. One story involved a classic Afrikaner family—a huge tannie (auntie), a beer-bellied Oom (uncle), Frikkie, Sussie, a pet budgie and Ouma (granny). As they arrived at the park, Ouma chose that highly inopportune moment to leave this mortal coil. Undeterred and fiercely determined not to let this minor event spoil the rest of their annual holiday, the family promptly had her shoved into the Sodwana commercial freezer where she was to remain until it was time for them to head home. The local staff were apprehensive about the frozen granny and flatly refused to enter the ice room until the ‘matter’ was cleared up. We laughed appreciatively.
After checking out we set off for Hartbeespoort. The error of forgetting to arrange our accommodation before the weekend was soon felt; in fact very little was available. Eventually we managed to find a spare room in a house in the Hartbeespoort village. We were led around a dog poo-covered square of ground to the back entrance of an untidy-looking house, parking amid bits of rusting metal and general bric-a-brac before being guided to the door by a skulking woman with shifty, dark eyes. She spoke English with the hesitant style adopted by those speaking a language not their own and apologised for leading us through the ‘backside’. Our room was a bare, wooden-floored affair entirely devoid of furniture save for a double bed. We had to fight our way through a curtain-festooned bathroom just to get to the bedroom. Neither of us was overly impressed but we had no choice and at least it was clean. She informed us that we were her very first guests— information that did little to reassure. The entire house (we were taken on a brief tour), smacked of traditional ‘Afrikaner country’, complete with mismatched tartan and flower-patterned armchairs and an assortment of hand-knitted doilies. The room came complete with a resident parrot called Cappie and two sniffling bulldogs. We decided to visit the local snake and animal park whilst working out how to avoid staying there for the two nights originally agreed upon. The snake and animal park featured an impressive selection of animals. I fed a public phone box a small fortune in coins trying to locate alternative accommodation for the following night but the public holiday stood against us. Back at the house of curtains we were feeling rather despondent at our failure to find a vacancy. The gauzy net curtains that we constantly had to fight through just to get from room to room were also getting on our collective wick. What was bizarre though was no curtain covered the massive window that looked directly out over the main lounge across the way. I pirouette across the bedroom in nothing but a small towel to display my sheer annoyance and provide the entertainment they were obviously hoping for then spent the next hour regretting it. When perched on the loo you had to lean slightly to the left to avoid fully displaying yourself to anyone in the lounge as the gauzy film of netting did not meet at the strategic place in the middle. So we bathed furtively behind the net curtain and decided we would brave it and venture into the main lounge, mutter politely for a minute or two and then go out somewhere for supper. However, we had not counted on Frikkie. He was the absolute epitome of an egte Boer— large, rotund with a magnificent beer paunch that threatened to burst through his tightly buttoned shirt. He was wearing the classic farmer’s uniform of a two-toned, short-sleeved shirt and baggy khaki shorts. He was an affable chap though and talked non-stop. He offered us a cold beer which we sat perched on the slippery sofa, sipping politely. Of his wife, ‘Lurch’, we saw very little after our initial introduction to the room save for the occasional shadow as she shuffled past the doorway. She had not spoken a single word to us since our arrival and Col and I were trying to work out which of us had offended her—Col with his blunt comment that the place was full of dog shit (which he had uttered right outside an open window earlier), or my semi-naked prancing. Their young daughter saved the day by taking Tim to see their parrot and we dutifully followed. Suddenly Lurch shuffled up beside us declaring that supper was ready which completely put paid to our plans of sneaking off to a restaurant. It seems Frikkie was the resident chef and his sweating bulk appeared and reappeared throughout the meal, carrying plates laden with wors and vleis (sausages and meat) in all its glorious forms. He was assisted by two domestic maids whom he was desperately trying to train in the complex ways of catering for paying guests. We overheard a lot of loud whispering, frantic head-shaking and intense hisses between clenched teeth whilst the two unfortunates tried to work out what they were meant to be doing next. We were joined at the table by their resident guest; a retired dental technician who resided permanently in the caravan parked outside our bedroom window. Supper was pleasant but typically high in cholesterol; every potato, grain of rice and piece of spinach was liberally doused in butter and oil. This high-calorie meal was followed by a mountain of ice cream. Frikkie regaled us with tales of the Kruger National Park where they had lived for three years while he worked as the reserve’s refrigeration technician. By now, our unspoken agreement was that we would remain for the next day. I tried my best not to show my amusement as the dirty dishes piled up while Frikkie kept leaning back in his chair, clapping his hands loudly in an attempt to get the attention of the maids to clear the table. He leaned over and told me confidentially that he had tried ringing a brass bell but they completely ignored that too. Finally we all waddled off to bed. I awakened to the promise of another massive Transvaal breakfast and wasn’t disappointed. As we descended on the dining table en masse, Frikkie was sweating over the frying pans. Heaped plates of meat towered next to buttered toast and savoury mince competed with buttered fried mushrooms and a large jar of Frikkie’s homemade fig jam. We ate incredibly well before setting off for the Pretoria Zoo, an hour's drive away. The day was beautifully warm and the zoo was an enormous place with every conceivable animal represented. Besides those we had seen in the wild, we saw vicuña, camels (both Bactrian and dromedary) and various South American, Indian, and Australian wolves, agoutis and North African antelope. We had to walk for miles to get around the various enclosures. Lastly, we hiked up a fair-sized hill and boarded some colourful cable cars which whizzed gently down over the animal cages below. It was a thoroughly enjoyable day and Tim fell fast asleep in my arms for the final portion—he had walked incredibly far on his little legs. Back in Hartbeespoort, I had a bath with the usual contortions that entailed in order not to be in full naked view of the household—I felt a bizarre urge to wave at one stage. Then we joined Frikkie and ‘Shuffle’ ( never did get her name) for a braai outside in the cement-covered courtyard. Frikkie proudly showed us his prized possession of iron potjie pots and a piece of puce-coloured oliehout that he had brought back with him from his time in the Kruger. The braai comprised steak, sausage (wors), potatoes prepared in various guises and thick slices of bread and rolls. My heart sank at the thought of consuming more heavy carbohydrates and cholesterol but rose again slightly when I heard there was a bowl of slaai (salad) only to fall again when I discovered that this potential bit of green was nothing more than boiled rice and mayonnaise mixed with a few particles of shredded parsley. The carrots were heavily honeyed and sautéed in a rich butter sauce and the ubiquitous stywe pap (stiff maize porridge) sat in a thick, glutinous white mound on a central platter. This repast ended with a huge helping of full-cream ice cream and a rendition of loud whistles and choice swearing from Cappie the parrot before we finally repaired to bed. The next day dawned bright and hot as we made our way with trepidation towards the breakfast table, our livers groaning in anticipation. There were no surprises. Breakfast was a sumptuous, heavy choice of eggs, bacon, a mixture of chopped meats quivering in a rich butter sauce, wors, toast, and a huge assortment of jams and preserves. I tried to sample a polite little finger of each but under the constant, watchful gaze of Frikkie, this proved exceptionally difficult; his paunch rose in visible consternation when he noticed such small helpings on my plate. He leaned over, placed a thick, quivering sausage on my plate and beamed at me. Breakfast successfully dispensed with, Col sent Tim and me off for a long walk whilst he attempted to assemble his presentation talk for the Pathology congress on the morrow. We checked out around 10 a.m. with many cries of ‘Totsiens’ and ‘Lekker bly’ and a loud parting cuss from Cappie before setting off for Bakubung, the Pilanesberg and ultimately Sol Kerzner’s visionary Sun City hotel. 
The drive was uneventful and we arrived just after midday only to find that this renowned establishment would enforce a strict check-in time of 4 p.m. We were more than a little put out as this was no cheap hovel we were paying to stay in. After locking our valuables in the hotel safe, we walked down to the pool and lake area where Sol Kerzner’s resort and the Mostert family legacy wrestled momentarily for supremacy. Sol won the moment; Tim spotted the fleet of miniature motorised cars and trucks available for his pleasure—at a hefty price. He happily careered around the miniature track—and quite often straight off it—making haphazard progress while a sweating attendant trotted frantically next to his vehicle in an attempt to keep him on the path. Col and I purchased two juices at the surf shop for the equivalent price of a small plot in Sandton then feeling very light of pocket, headed back to the hotel where we were finally allowed entry to our cabana. Thus began what was to be a series of continuous minor irritations whilst staying at this plastic place in the sun. Tim and I walked down to the Cascades after breakfast the following morning. The interior design of the resort was fascinating in places. A colossal number of casino slot machines lined the main rooms, incessantly beeping, bopping and blaring staccato messages designed to induce the rapid parting of cash. The Palace of the Lost City was a splendid affair even if one got the distinct impression that every ornate carving carried a ‘Made in China’ label tucked away somewhere underneath. Tim and I walked down a long path towards the ‘ruins’ and eventually reached the Valley of Waves. This was a massive, man-made beach where even the shoreline consisted of sand-covered plastic onto which huge hydraulic waves crashed systematically. Tim slipped a few times on the plastic beach so I decided it was time to move on. There was a tremendous amount to see, do, and spend, so we only managed to get back to our room at about 5 p.m. A formal banquet was to be held that evening to mark the conclusion of the medical congress. I organised a resident babysitter, got dressed then sat waiting for her to show up. Eventually, an emaciated girl arrived at our door, coughing her lungs out. Col and I were more than a little alarmed, wondering privately if she was suffering from active tuberculosis but with insufficient medical evidence we had to leave Tim in her care and pray that he’d avoid any potential tubercle. We arrived at the banquet late as the official speeches were in full swing. We’re hastily shepherded to our designated table where I proceeded to enthusiastically remove the decorative fabric chair cover and tuck it firmly into my waistband under the impression that it was a fancy linen serviette. A discreet waiter stepped forward to make the appropriate adjustments. The meal commenced with smoked salmon and fresh rolls before moving on to lamb parcels in a rich herb crust with suitably disguised vegetables. Our guest speaker for the evening was Adrian Steed who made the night memorable with his quick jokes. He delivered them in the formal manner of a television newscast reader: 
"The President and Chief Mangosuthu Buthelezi met for high-level talks yesterday. Matters became heated, however and ended with Mr Mandela throwing an entire jug of orange juice over Buthelezi. Mangosuthu then retaliated by forcefully shoving two bananas into the President’s nostrils. The evening was later officially described by spokesmen as being ‘tense but fruitful’." 
"It was announced that Saddam Hussein will be commencing a grand tour of his country’s remaining allies today. He is expected to return tomorrow." "Mr Naidoo has finally taken over the former Minister of Finance’s budget portfolio. When questioned by the press, the former Minister admitted he was deeply annoyed by the transition as he hadn't finished colouring it in yet!" 
"In order to uplift the country’s flagging economic opportunities, it has been decided by the government to temporarily change the name of South Africa to Japan. And why not? They already have a Tokyo in government!" (This was a nod to Tokyo Sexwale, the prominent South African politician and former political prisoner). 
"A spokesperson for the Bella Valley nudist resort was said to be seriously contemplating retirement from the colony last week but upon further questioning today he announced that he was prepared to turn over a new leaf and stick it out for another year.”
 "The Minister of Finance, Mr Trevor Manuel, was encouraging the people of South Africa to invest their money in an attempt to overcome public spending. Who knows—in a couple of years, perhaps the Rand will actually be worth something." (Spoiler alert—it wasn't). 
After Adrian finished, there was a long, ponderous speech from a visiting medical professor from England. A live band arrived the moment he left the stage and couples duly got up to shuffle around the tiny dance floor. The band’s arrival coincided perfectly with the serving of dessert—an elaborate ice cream affair done up like a French tart. At about 11:30 p.m I decided it was time to rescue my Tim from the babysitter and whatever nameless respiratory disease she was suffering from, leaving Col at the function as I walked the fair distance back to our room. Tim was fast asleep in our bed so I spent a very uncomfortable night on the sofa—a discomfort that was significantly worsened by worrying when Col still had not returned. By 3 a.m. I was alternating between intense worry that he had been mugged in the resort grounds and anger that he hadn’t. He finally stumbled back a little later and judging by his apparent inability to conduct the simple procedure of inserting the key into the door, I gathered he was well into his cups. 
The next morning Tim and I visited the plastic beach one last time to have a final look around before boarding one of the resort shuttle buses back to the cabanas—only the driver failed to stop there, meaning we were forced to ride around the entire complex twice before being let off. Back at the room Col was frantically piling heavy suitcases onto a luggage trolley before the checkout deadline. I sat down with quiet determination and made myself another cup of tea in the room as there was still time. Col returned to the congress centre briefly only to discover that during his absence, his name had been drawn to win the grand prize of a weekend luxury safari in a Botswana game reserve. Because he’d decided to pack instead of leaving it to me, this near-miss was deemed entirely my fault and we drove the long trip back to Cape Town in stony silence. We stopped at Witput outside Kimberley to book ourselves in for the night with Col still visibly steaming about the missed safari. I realised disappointment meant he needed someone to blame and that it might as well be me. Later that evening after tension thawed we decided that we were going to look through the international medical journals for an overseas posting in the Middle East. Saudi Arabia to be exact.
