# Chapter 12: India: January 2000

Hewa, our Sri Lankan house-help who looked after our massive apartment, arrived to tidy up the rooms and finish off the ironing. He nodded his head sideways in that classic subcontinent gesture, said goodbye to us and ‘baby’, his name for Tim and promised he would lock up the flat securely behind us. The local taxi took us straight to the Indian Airlines office located in the city centre. We were still living in Saudi Arabia at this stage, and had specifically booked our tickets with Indian Airlines, as opposed to the premier Air India fleet, because it was significantly cheaper—though this financial choice was a pecuniary shortcut we would later come to regret. While waiting in the small, stuffy booking office to confirm our flight details, Tim began to display the first telltale signs of an impending illness. It was true to form, of course, and always occurred on day one of any holiday we had attempted to take for the past six years. No matter how meticulously a trip was planned, the severity of his illness was always directly dependent on the degree of geographic isolation from proper medical care. As we sat waiting in the cramped office, the room began to fill up rapidly with people and heavy baggage in equal quantities. There were a great number of securely boxed appliances stacked on the floor—televisions, hi-fis, and microwave ovens—on their way to India, all purchased with the relatively good salaries in Saudi for their families back home. We had a lot of luggage between us, including three cameras. These last items proved to be a matter of some concern to the Saudi bus driver when he finally arrived. He did his best to persuade Col to part with them for storage in the back of the bus with the rest of the luggage—and failed, not knowing, as I did, Colin's stubbornness when it comes to the safety of his cameras. The thobe-clad official persisted in his request. ‘Better to keep my cameras concealed,’ Col stated firmly. The bus driver eventually gave up trying to prise the cameras from Colin, shrugged to show he had done his best, adjusted his ghutrah firmly on his head and returned to the driver's seat. The bus crossed the long bridge that divides Saudi and all that is abnormal and unnatural from Bahrain: the King Fahd Causeway, which is 15 miles (24 km) long and links Bahrain to Saudi Arabia. The water was a lovely pale blue colour and very calm, as is the norm in the Gulf. I hoped customs was not going to be a big ‘mushkila’—we had taken to using several words in Arabic during our time in Saudi; this one means ‘trouble’. Customs itself was fine but the long wait was interminable. We were left on the bus for ages while the drawn-out process of paperwork took place. Prayer time arrived somewhere in the middle, which accounted for another half-an-hour's wait while the obligatory call to prayer was answered. Carpets were unfurled and placed in the general direction of Mecca. Col immediately took advantage of this temporary distraction and spent the next ten minutes trying to disguise his cameras. In case of a thorough search, I was asked to wrap one of them in my jersey and place it under my seat. This mistrust of cameras was proving to be a pain. Photography in general, is not encouraged in the Middle East. It was a mite embarrassing when the officials returned, fresh from their union with Allah, and all the Pakistani and Indian passengers were asked to vacate the bus to have their possessions and persons thoroughly searched. We, however, were told to remain on board and forego the search. It was a dubious advantage of our nationality—thanks to Hansie Cronje's recent fall from grace, we were posing as British tourists. (The South African cricket captain was supposed to have accepted a rather large bribe before an important match against India a few days ago). With customs finally dispensed with and all aboard once more, we drove on into central Bahrain towards the airport. The journey was significant in its well-maintained roads, orderly traffic and clear road signs. It amazed me that only 24 km away from the utter disorder of Saudi traffic lay a world of difference with the relative order in Bahrain. We were shunted off to yet another waiting room at 9 p.m. for some reason known only to the airport staff. As we sat waiting, I gazed around the airport waiting area, amazed at the number of red-faced, sunburnt British tourists on their way home from summer holidays with their husbands in the Middle East. They were all dressed in clothing that was spectacular in the minimal amount of cloth used in its manufacture. The women were braless under their tight T-shirts and cut-away shorts that left absolutely nothing to the imagination—and as imagination is usually all the locals have to go on, this was a blatant show of disrespect for Arabic custom. As most of the women thus dressed were on the dangerous side of middle age, it added a sadly comic tone. Once on board Indian Airlines, we buckled ourselves into our seats and nervously noted the telltale signs of age and neglect: crookedly hanging curtains, peeling paint and faded, mismatching upholstery that was sometimes absent altogether. It was not very reassuring, therefore, when the plane shuddered, juddered, whined, and bounced into a wobbly take-off like some elderly woman trying to run uphill. Col was convinced by the unevenness of the take-off that one of the tyres was flat, a feeling that was intensified by the steward’s rather lopsided walk up the aisle. Tim settled into his seat, gazing out of the porthole window, whilst the rest of us sat bolt upright, straining to hear every word of the safety talk as though our lives depended on it—which they probably do. It was when I thought I heard the word ‘elastic’ that I really got worried and clutched tightly at Colin’s arm. My attention was momentarily diverted by the sight of a wild-eyed Pakistani gentleman who was fumbling around on the floor behind his seat for what turned out to be his bottle of duty-free. It had rolled its way down the aisle on take-off and had come to rest at Colin’s feet. Col was startled when the man's grasping fingers closed firmly around his ankle in his attempt to retrieve the bottle. Whisky and owner were finally united, and both made their way somewhat unsteadily to the seat in front. The flight attendant, noting Tim’s red face and coughing spasm, kindly allowed him to lie across three empty seats at the back of the plane shortly after take-off. She brought him a blanket and a pillow, and our faith in Indian Airlines was restored, albeit temporarily. The shaky take-off was replaced by an equally shaky and shuddering landing not ten minutes later in Doha. It landed with an intensity that could dislodge false teeth and having cast a quick look around our fellow passengers, it appeared it had. The plane must be at least thirty-five years old, I thought anxiously— absolutely no mod cons, just the bare necessities that allow it to be called an instrument of flight.

We waited fifty long minutes in Doha, presumably while they rewound the elastic band prior to take-off. Just minutes before we left the runway, ten people boarded the plane, so we had to vacate our seats and move over to Tim, lying him across our laps so he could continue sleeping. The flight was extremely bumpy and just as I found a position in my seat that was a modicum less cramped than before and closed my eyes, they suddenly snapped on the overhead lights. Everyone sat up, startled, to the offer of hot Cokes, Fanta, or whisky and soda from the briskly voiced cabin crew who were trundling the drink trolleys down the aisles. We decided the occasion warranted the whisky (‘hold the ice!’), and thus we sat, gritty-eyed with tiredness at 3:20 a.m., sipping whisky and sodas and munching a packet of stale peanuts. Col and I argued mildly over who was to have Tim's packet when the decision was made for us; the open packet was jerked from my hands by sudden turbulence, causing its contents to spill all over the floor. The sudden smell of curried something sent my bowels into spasm and I was wondering about the wisdom of having hot curry and whisky at 3:30 a.m. on a plane that was sure to have to make an emergency landing at any time, thereby requiring our full and coherent attention. The kindly Indian attendant with the dimpled smile had just given me some ice and a cloth for Tim’s fever. Despite her good intentions, I was being a touch paranoid about the possibility of any water trickling into his mouth and with it a million virulent germs, so I was using the moistened cloth very carefully.

Tim felt a little cooler and I repositioned his feet carefully over my full tummy. I couldn’t sleep; I never can on night flights, but particularly not with a stiff whisky and a plateful of hot curry inside me. We arrived at the international airport in Kerala at 7 a.m. Indian time and were the last off the plane due to the chaotic assortment of packages and bags that we had. For some reason, a separate bus was provided for us while the rest of the passengers were shunted onto another. This ‘special’ (and completely unwarranted) attention afforded us was very embarrassing. It was already warm and humid but bearable at this early hour. As we blearily made our way through to customs, I took a deep breath of India—and coughed! 'It is true then,' I thought, 'the smell of humanity, spices, heat, and open sewerage is a very unique and all-pervading thing in India.' 
Our ‘special’ status continued in customs when, once again, we were ushered through the queue ahead of the others who were waiting patiently in line. But as it was unbearably humid in the airport building and Tim was still feverish, I wasn’t complaining. A porter came forward to assist us in finding our suitcases ahead of everyone else. One of the officials discovered we were from South Africa and the Hansie story came up immediately. We denied any allegiance, but surprisingly, they all appeared to like him and thought his fall from grace a great pity. They said his mistake was simply in getting caught. Customs pleasantly dispensed with, we stepped out from the depths of dark humidity of the airport terminal and into the bright, early morning sun, scanning the area anxiously for any sign of ‘Reagan Travels’. They had promised us a vehicle on arrival. After looking over the heads of the milling crowd for a few moments, a small sign bobbed into view with the words ‘Coolin Mestart’ in red letters, alerting us to their presence. A few seconds later, a grinning Indian man wearing a white shirt, brown trousers and the ubiquitous rubber flip-flops came forward, gesturing towards his upheld sign and enquired with a sideways nod whether we were the said ‘Mestarts’. We claimed the title and shook hands, simultaneously nodding our heads in typical Indian fashion, then quickly followed behind this representative of Reagan Travels. He grabbed our luggage piece by piece, bundled the lot onto a waiting trolley, and marched purposefully forward through the milling crowds. This was easier said than done, as Tim was still listless and it was at once apparent that there were at least five thousand of the one billion inhabitants of India moving tightly around us. The spicy, damp, warm smell of India pervaded the atmosphere and felt like breathing through a curried, wet blanket. We were led by the Reagan representative to a side road full of old Ambassador cars. These vehicles are the staple taxi in India and are everywhere, together with the three-wheeled auto-rickshaw. These rickshaws are invariably decorated with gold and red or yellow paints and most have a collapsible hood. They feature all kinds of decorative bells, trumpets and dangly, tinkly, shiny attachments. Our driver, having deposited our ten pieces of luggage from the trolley along the pavement, quickly disappeared into the thick crowd to fetch the car. Thus, we were left standing in the beeping, hooting melee that is India, while we held onto our precious cameras and bags and gazed, bleary-eyed and jet-lagged, in the direction in which our guide had gone. ‘There’s bound to be trouble,’ I thought anxiously. Three minutes later, a black Ambassador taxi cab came to a sudden stop in front of us and before we had time to think, we were bundled into it together with our luggage by a chap wearing a red bandana. This observation of headgear is vital to the next series of events. It was not until we were fifteen minutes away from the airport that both Colin and I felt an urgent need to re-establish the identity of our driver. Neither of us could remember if the gentleman who originally met us with the signboard outside the airport had a) worn a blue shirt or b) sported a red bandana. The longer we speculated, the less sure we were. The driver of this specific vehicle, however, sported both a blue shirt and a red bandana. Desperately, Col enquired as to his name. A sideways nod of the head indicated that he had heard the query, but his rapid spurt of Hindi also indicated that he was ‘mafeesh Inglesi’ (no English). We cast around for the signboard that had our name so beautifully misspelt on it, but couldn't see it anywhere. We were now convinced that we had climbed into the wrong taxi and were being led further and further away from any chance of reuniting with our actual chap from Reagan Travels. This led to several frantic, one-sided conversations in pidgin English while we desperately tried to ascertain the identity of our driver. ‘Reagan Travels?' I asked. ‘Yes,’ he nodded happily, but then he nodded at absolutely everything else we asked. Colin shouted at him to stop the car, and when this didn’t work, he leaned right over the seat and grabbed his lapel. The petrified driver swerved to the side of the road (not an easy thing to do at any time in India). He repeated the word ‘hoteli’ several times, to which Col replied, ‘No, no, not hotel! We want Reagan Travels!’ enunciating each syllable with exaggerated care. Col then said he wanted to use a phone. ‘Stop! Want phone. Call man in Reagan, yes?’ The driver appeared to understand the word ‘phone’ and pulled up just outside a ramshackle duka with a sign that stated it had STD availability inside. These letters did not stand for any questionable medical condition, but rather Subscriber Trunk Dialling for the local telephone company. Col asked him first to open the boot, where he scrabbled hastily through the ten pieces of luggage in a last-ditch attempt to look for the offending sign but couldn’t find it. He then accompanied the quivering chap into the phone booth. I was drinking in (albeit tiredly), the chaotic sights and sounds of Calicut, Southern India, whilst waiting for the pair of them to return. Most of the men seemed to be wearing nappy-like skirts tucked into their waists. They’re called lungis and come in a variety of colours and patterns, although white is prevalent. The women were dressed in either brightly coloured saris or dark abayas and burqas. I turned my attention to the surrounding countryside, a fascinating mix of Africa and India with its dusty roads, verdant trees and bushes and the rich sound of birdsong. Everywhere a human tide ebbed and flowed over the dusty roads and streets and vehicles of every description hooting and honking horns incessantly. Dogs, sheep, pigs, cows, and even monkeys criss-crossed the road haphazardly with the rest of the melee. Colin suddenly reappeared with the Indian driver looking slightly less harassed, and said that he thought our driver was the right guy after all. He had been told to take us to the Reagan Travels office right away. As Colin climbed back into the car, a small white signboard with the words ‘Reagan Travels’ and “Coolin Mestart” tumbled as if by magic from the back window ledge onto our laps and we sighed in relief at this sudden evidence that we were indeed with the right man. The Reagan Travels office was located somewhere on the outskirts of Calicut, down a winding back road. We were led up a flight of concrete stairs to the top floor, where Reagan conducted its business in a narrow room with a short counter along its length. Two black plastic chairs sat in front. It was stiflingly hot despite a ceiling fan, which revolved in a desultory manner and merely shifted the hot air ineffectually around the room. Tim and I sat down and dripped perspiration over the plastic chairs, which stuck uncomfortably to the back of our legs. Colin discovered that our earlier confusion stemmed from the fact that there was indeed another chap (wearing a white shirt and not wearing a red bandana) who had first met us—he had simply joined his partner later with the car. The mystery solved, we were driven to the Asma Towers Hotel. The driver was instructed to drive us to see Narkan, the wife of Col’s medical colleague in Saudi, Nasser. Their house was set amid the usual hustle and bustle of side streets. I alighted from the depths of the Ambassador and looked up at the building. The front of the house featured a set of wide marble steps that led up to a red-tiled verandah. Narkan met us at the door. She was a pretty woman wearing a long, pale, flowing trouser suit in an interesting shade of lime with a long, matching floaty scarf. ‘Hello, pleased to meet you. Please excuse Nasser, he is busy at the hospital. Please, come in.’ She welcomed us inside and we followed her into the relative coolness of the house, hastily removing our shoes inside a huge wooden door after noticing that she was barefoot. Large, glossy white tiles were laid everywhere, giving a spacious, cool feel to the house. Narkan handed out glasses of cold lemonade which we sipped while getting acquainted with our hostess. After a half-hour or so of conversation, Col explained that we needed a few items from the local shops, medicine for Tim, who was still feeling sick. ‘Of course,’ she murmured and we were immediately whisked off to do some shopping. Narkan drove well, expertly nipping in and out of the disorganised traffic while chatting to Col in the front. We met Doctor Nasser, a pleasant man in his early forties, who joined us at the hotel a short while later. He was anxious, having recommended it, that we were comfortable with the hotel. We left with him, Narkan, and their pretty ten-year-old daughter for the other side of town where their friends' house was located. Nasser’s friends had invited their neighbour and his wife to join us as well. The house was another large, odd-shaped place with a wide balcony. Heavy, ornate furniture lined the rooms inside. A huge television held pride of place in the corner of the lounge with brass lights like standard lamps placed around it. ‘You light these lamps when there’s a religious holiday,’ explained the hostess with a warm smile. When lit, it cast a beautiful glow over a shrine to one of the many-armed brass goddesses that was situated close by. Fake plastic greenery was immensely popular and abounded in an inner sanctum, which featured a strangely positioned sunken square floor alongside the dining table. A long spiral staircase traversed the sanctum and led to various bedrooms, one of which doubled as the home bar room. ‘Please join us upstairs,’ said the large host after introducing us to his three polite children. It was to an upstairs corner room that we were led and introduced to one of the local gynaecologists and a Coke merchant—we were too polite to ask if they meant the beverage. ‘What will you have?’ asked Sarjan after seating us in a circle of chairs which faced a single bed pushed against a wall. We decided whisky and water seemed the safest option under the circumstances and infinitely preferable to the sickly sweet warm Coke and Fanta that is usually on offer. Conversation revolved around expatriate Saudi life versus India, and India versus South Africa—with a healthy dollop of the Hansie Cronje saga thrown in for good measure. A plate of fresh orange, apple, and cucumber segments was brought around by one of the young daughters, who proffered the plate shyly to each guest. Two or three stiff whiskies later we headed down the ornate white staircase for an early supper (or perhaps 'blupper', as it was now 3:30 p.m.). Anyhow, it was the first thing we’d eaten since our plane journey the night before. The conversation continued where it had left off in the atrium below, where several curry dishes and a plate of roasted quail were laid out on a large, dark wooden and lace-covered table. For dessert, we were treated to a creamy-sauced vermicelli with a subtle rose fragrance; it was delicious and I surreptitiously heaped a few extra tablespoons into my bowl, only to feel a sudden pang of guilt when I noticed there wasn't enough left for the others. After the meal, Tim was heating up again, so we thanked our hosts profusely and took our leave. Nasser kindly drove us back to the hotel, having given us some medication for Tim straight from his surgery. We promised to contact them again on our return from Nagarhole.
A constant, sporadic burst of fireworks scattered throughout the night—seeing in the public holiday, I suppose—but none of it conducive to a good night's sleep. Room service brought up two cups of strong tea made with boiled milk. Col took his anti-malarial pills and promptly vomited them back up again, while Tim flatly refused to touch his medicine. I was beginning to wish we could start this holiday all over again. 
We had a new driver this morning, courtesy of Reagan Travels. His name was Krish—a good name after a couple of stiff whiskies. He seemed a pleasant man and spoke good English, which was an improvement on our previous guide. Our first port of call was to retrieve our gas cylinder which had been inadvertently left behind in yesterday's taxi. It had gone home with the driver. Krish wove expertly in and out of the three-abreast lanes of cars, auto-rickshaws, motorbikes, and buses, all of which were travelling way past any safe speed limit—assuming there were any. We finally arrived at a small tea estate; a very lush-looking area covered with hundreds of olive-coloured bushes. ‘This is Mr Japhta's house,’ Krish informed us with a sideways nod as we pulled up outside a modest mud-and-wattle dwelling. Krish jumped out to investigate, only to discover that Mr Japhta was out. He had in fact strapped the gas cylinder onto his piki-piki —a little Vespa-type scooter—and driven it all the way to our hotel. With no way to contact him we had no choice but to await his return. In the meantime, Krish managed to get us half a coconut from one of the village women who, along with several other locals, had been watching us shyly. A short while later they brought out two sticky, greasy buns for us to eat. They tasted delicious, similar to a rich Madeira cake but packed with cumin seeds. Col, being the fiercely careful lad that he is, immediately handed his bun over to me. ‘No sense in us both dying of food poisoning!’ I muttered wryly. We stood around a bit awkwardly, beaming at the women and eating slivers of slippery white coconut meat. My bladder soon became pressing, as it inevitably does on such occasions, so Col asked the ladies via Krish if I might use their ablutions. This prompted a quick flurry of activity as they set about preparing their modest toilet for their honoured guest. I felt acutely embarrassed that I had caused them any anxiety. I quickly smiled my appreciation at the small group before disappearing behind the mud-and-wattle door. It was the usual hole in the ground flanked by porcelain footrests, and spotlessly clean. I conducted a quick recce for any signs of unwanted wildlife before squatting gratefully. My toilette complete, I emerged to beam at the assembled crowd, who appeared to be anxiously holding their collective breath in case I had found their facilities wanting. ‘Thank you, very nice and clean,’ I murmured, somewhat inadequately. Honour was successfully maintained and I looked up to see the errant driver finally put-putting up the road in a spray of red murram dust with our gas cylinder strapped firmly to the back of his motorbike. The cylinder was returned with much side-nodding —that beautifully and uniquely Indian custom—and we took our leave with smiles and waves, heading onwards towards Karnataka. Bangalore was a mere 60 kilometres away but given the current road conditions and the continuously heavy traffic, the journey took us all of five hours. We drove slowly past vast forest trees and climbed the steep Ghats mountain pass. It was a one-way road only for which I was profoundly grateful given the undisciplined driving I had witnessed so far. Beautiful Charaxes butterflies fluttered around the edge of the tarmac and we spotted either a golden or an immature black eagle. We pulled over at a place called the Vythiri Resort, a lovely jungle lodge situated about two hours away from Calicut airport. Vythiri sits at about 2,600 feet above sea level completely surrounded by dense, pristine rainforest. As we were sitting under the thatch-roofed verandah looking out into the canopy, I noticed a sudden flash of reddish-yellow in the trees. ‘What was that?’ I asked, scanning the foliage quickly with my binoculars. It dashed past again, and Col identified it as a Malabar giant squirrel. I was amazed that he could pick it out so accurately without the benefit of binoculars. We paid our bill and left the comfort of the cool forest lodge, climbing back into the sweltering interior of the car. Krish became quite chatty, telling us all about the local area and its people. He switched on the car cassette player and the high-pitched singing so typical of Indian music filled the cabin, creating just the right atmosphere as we navigated the winding roads towards Nagarhole National Park. 
Our wooden cabin looked out over a small, scenic lake. ‘Can we go swimming?’ asked Tim eagerly the moment we entered the cabin. Col said there were most probably crocodiles lurking in the water. Tim thought that over for two seconds. ‘OK,’ he said pragmatically and quickly located a small hammock tied between two small trees outside. He clambered in with his Game Boy and settled down for some serious gaming fun. The dining area was a beautiful, circular stone-floored verandah sheltered beneath a wide thatched roof. It featured a long row of trestle tables upon which bubbled a number of pots containing three different types of curry, with a mountain of poppadoms on the side. Even the basic vegetables were heavily curried. Outside the office, we were introduced to the rotund figure of the park manager and one of the game wardens, also named Sarjan. Three tourists were already sitting in the open safari jeep when we climbed aboard for our game drive. One of the young men, sporting a baseball cap, sat in the front seat, smoking endlessly and showering poor Sarjan with both ash and questions; he spent far more time talking than actually looking for animals. I had noticed visiting for a workshop on wildlife photography. They marched around the camp importantly, clutching colossal cameras and heavy lenses. 
The air was hot but not uncomfortable. ‘We’re just acclimatised, living in Saudi,’ Col remarked with a shrug. The first wild animal we spotted on Indian soil was a small barking deer. With its rich reddish-brown colour, it resembled our South African duiker, only significantly larger. Then we came across a breeding herd of spotted deer, or chital. We were destined to see hundreds of them during the remainder of the holiday but the first sighting of this common but pretty deer was thrilling. Our guides told us that on this specific route, we would likely encounter wild boar and the massive Indian bison known as the gaur. The bull gaur were dark brown in colour and were huge-shouldered beasts; the females were lighter in colour but no less impressive. A troop of langur monkeys were playing happily in the branches of a massive banyan tree, messing around with the cotton-like seedpods that abound at this time of the year. It was amusing watching their antics with the balls of fluff. Other wildlife sightings included an Indian cobra, a golden-backed woodpecker, a greater racket-tailed drongo, the spectacular Indian roller, that great avian mimic—the Indian myna bird, a banded snake eagle, and the common grey jungle fowl, from which all our domestic chickens originally derive. It is amazing what fun it is to see even a common bird in its natuaral setting. Before heading back to camp we pulled up at a beautiful clearing next to a lake, featuring a neatly cropped lawn and a peaceful line of pale, lime-barked fever trees. It was here that we enjoyed our first sighting of a small group of wild Indian elephants. They are noticeably smaller than African elephants, possessing a distinct convex curved back, much smaller ears and comical, tufted hairy heads. I was videoing some wild peacocks on our way back to the camp when a voice suddenly crackled over the jeep's two-way radio; it was the chief game warden and he was demanding to know why we were wasting valuable time looking at common peacocks! Col and I replied with a laugh that we enjoy all aspects of wildlife and were there to appreciate everything the park had to offer. I think the staff were pleased with that response—and it was certainly true. Personally, I have little time for tourists back home who only want to see ‘the Big Five’. 
As night fell, the air reverberated with the deafening sound of cicadas.
As we ventured down to the dining hall for breakfast, I couldn't believe my eyes or my nose. It was curry in all its shapes, forms and imaginable guises—curried eggs, curried tomato and onion, curried omelettes and some incredibly greasy curried balls of rice served in a spicy soup. Look, don’t get me wrong, I like the occasional curry as much as the next person, but usually only once a week at most. I asked the kitchen staff to boil us some eggs. As I munched on my dry toast, I contemplated our geographical situation and felt bad about my aversion to so much spice; after all, a visiting Indian tourist to South Africa would likely find our total lack of seasoning an equally frustrating matter. I smeared a tiny bit of curried egg onto my toast as mild compensation and felt a little better. The cicadas were still clicking away noisily as I brewed a pot of tea on our gas stove. Out on the water, some local men were rowing a series of quaint, circular boats across the river. These were coracles—small, round, bowl-shaped vessels traditionally constructed from woven reeds or bamboo and covered in waterproof hides. They are still widely used for local fishing and transportation across the deep river channels.
Later we departed with another guide named Krish (a common name around these parts) and a fellow tourist named Vishnu and his wife, embarking on a boat trip along the Kabini River, a major tributary of the Cauvery River. It was beautifully cool out on the water and we immediately spotted gaur, followed shortly by wild elephants, spot-billed ducks, lapwings and several massive mugger crocodiles basking on the mudbanks. Later that evening at the camp bar, we ran into Krish and a chap named Ghosh (pronounced ‘Goosh’—I decided not to consume more than one Kingfisher beer with all these sibilant names floating around). Ghosh turned out to be a space technician working on communications satellites up in Mumbai. I returned to the cabin to check on Tim; he was still feeling hot. I sponged his skin down with cool water once more and returned to the dining area to see if a culinary miracle had occurred and the kitchen had laid out a suckling pig and roast potatoes for supper. Alas, it was curried something-or-other yet again, so I simply nibbled on a handful of plain boiled rice. Tim’s temperature was thankfully down the next morning so I made a last-minute decision to join Col on the early game drive. We joined Krish on the open truck. The morning air felt crisp and cool after the recent rain. This time out, we spotted elephants, sambar deer, chital, and more beautiful Indian rollers. Suddenly, ‘Look over there!’ Col shouted excitedly, from his perch high on the front seat of the truck. ‘It’s a pack of Indian wild dogs!’ he exclaimed. ‘This is exceptionally rare.’ He immediately began snapping away furiously with his camera. There were about six or seven dholes in the pack and we watched them trotting purposefully along the dirt road for several minutes. I  hoped I managed to capture some excellent footage on my video camera—which was now held together by elastic bands, as the plastic cassette release door stubbornly refused to stay closed. Next, we made our way down to the river bank for what was, unbeknownst to me, a planned turn in one of the coracles. I couldn't believe they expected me to step inside one of these giant, floating woks. Ghosh assured me they were constructed from strong bamboo and thick cowhide and were watertight. I climbed gingerly into the circular piece of matting alongside five other passengers and prayed fervently that we would remain afloat. The moment everyone stepped on board, the coracle listed sharply to my side, forcing the edge of the ‘wok’ deep into the water. I clutched the woven sides for dear life and closed my eyes tightly. The local pilot was adept, however, utilising his single flat paddle to turn the vessel smoothly or pick up speed and it turned out to be rather pleasant as we drifted gently down the river current and back again for about twenty-five minutes. As we neared the shoreline to disembark, the pilot spun the coracle around and around in a traditional finale until I felt quite dizzy. 

We were up at 5:30 a.m. the following morning and I brewed a fresh pot of tea on our gas cooker. I let Tim sleep until the very last minute while Col and I drank coffee on the steps, overlooking the quietly flowing river. A fellow passenger who’s name I heard as ‘Shadup’ and his companion ‘Rubella’ (I might not have heard correctly over the engine) joined us on the morning drive, alongside Sumer, who was wearing his signature wide-brimmed bush hat. We saw a lot of the same as the previous day.
At the supper table that evening, the staff informed us that the meat had been left un-curried tonight, implemented I suspected due to public demand. I was therefore more than a little irritated when Tim took a large portion of chicken and cried out that his mouth was on fire. The kitchen staff simply couldn't resist shaking a few teaspoons of curry powder over the meat. A Malaysian woman at the next table made her feelings on the matter clear, demanding plain chicken and chips for her children in an impeccable, ringing British accent. ‘NO curry whatsoever!’ she warned the waiter menacingly. I amused myself the next morning in wondering what specific concoction of curried jungle fowl awaited us at the buffet. It was common to see these wild ancestors of the domestic chicken scratching through the dry deciduous forests, their colourful plumage standing out vividly against the dusty undergrowth. There were quite a number of spectacular birds in this habitat; our favourite was the brain fever bird—a type of cuckoo whose repeated, rising cry of ‘one more bottle!’ rang out all day long; famously known to drive many a man half-mad with its persistent call. I carefully picked out the cherries and bits of pine nuts that decorated the yellow rice platter, wondering idly if there would ever be life after curry. The cicadas were incredibly noisy again; the sound would rise in a massive crescendo and then fall away again, continuing for hours at a time. Tim and I spent some time watching one of the camp cooks prepare fresh chapatis. He rolled the dough into a long sausage shape, chopped off a neat section, rolled it into a ball, then expertly flattened the dough with his bare hands over a smooth circular stone. The large, flat pancake of dough was then slapped directly into a fiery, cylindrical tandoor oven, where it stuck to the inside clay wall. The chapati bubbled and browned and the finished product was skilfully hooked out using a long metal stick. 

The Splendour of Mysore

Later that afternoon we prepared for our final departure, joined in the car by a British couple in their forties, both sporting long hair, strands of beads, and tie-dyed clothes reminiscent of the 1960's. Their names were Sarah and Ashby. Fortunately, both were knowledgeable when it came to regional bird identification, which sort of made up for their retro attire as far as Col was concerned. 
We reunited with our driver, Krish and he packed the car to capacity with our luggage. He had been back to the regional office in Mysore, where we were now headed after saying our final goodbyes to the staff at Nagarhole. The scenery en route was as verdantly beautiful as ever, with the ever-present fronds of banana trees fanning the horizon. Krish located the Green Hotel, known historically as the Chittaranjan Palace and film studio, after navigating past the usual chaotic mass of emaciated, long-horned cattle, auto-rickshaws, overcrowded buses laden with people, luggage, and locals balancing on piki-pikis and bicycles. We passed women dressed in multi-coloured saris en route, many featuring pretty, fragrant garlands of jasmine flowers woven into their hair. 
Our room at the Green Hotel was plain but functional, equipped with a heavy ceiling fan that whirled in a desultory manner, making not the slightest bit of difference to the oppressive heat. The hotel had historically served as a palace for one of the lesser-ranked Maharajas and in 1954, it had been converted into a commercial film studio for the region's growing movie industry. It was a fascinating property inside, with a great deal of old British colonial influence it was decorated with wickerwork furniture, green ferns, huge gold-framed oil paintings and lavish Victorian ornaments. After lunch, we set off to visit the famous Mysore Palace in the centre of town. It practically glowed in the distance as we approached—a spectacular structure topped with shimmering gold and ivory domes. It was breathtakingly beautiful. We hired a local Muslim guide, feeling the property deserved a professional tour in order to extract as much history as possible from the visit, after handing over our grubby, unworthy shoes at the entrance kiosk for the princely sum of 50 rupees. It was sumptuous, rich and featured an unimaginable amount of gold leaf burnished onto every surface. The interior boasted fourteen magnificently carved solid teak doors, silver wrought chairs, teal-blue and green marble inlaid intricately onto the floors, precious stones and magnificent stained-glass windows featuring peacock themes that had been imported all the way from Glasgow, of all places. There were richly painted oil murals decorating the high ceilings. Tim especially liked a portrait where the eyes of the Maharaja appeared to follow the observer around the room, an optical illusion achieved by the artists using specialised ochre paints. It was interesting to note the distinct vestiges of British imperial influence scattered throughout the palace—in their symbolic use of the grandiose lion rather than the native, majestic tiger, for instance and members of the British Royal Family featured prominently in many of the paintings. Once we had completed our tour of the grand palace, marvelled at the ornate ceilings, and admired the giant murals depicting the Maharaja alongside his cavalry horses, we returned to retrieve our footwear from the low-walled stone collection point. Given the chaotic mountain of shoes sitting there without a name tag or reference number, it was an absolute miracle the attendants could match them up to the tourists at all. It reminded me of the massive piles of sandals that would sit outside the mosques back in Saudi Arabia—all looking virtually identical and strewn haphazardly around. The wisdom of that procedure is often tested and found wanting, especially when one notices the number of local people limping along the street wearing mismatched sandals in completely different sizes. But I digress. We fought our way back to the waiting car, pushing past street vendors selling large bags of rice, bars of yellow laundry soap, loose tea and coffee and tins of preserved fruit. Suspicious-looking green and blue iced cakes vied for supremacy with bright yellow, sticky buns and multi-coloured sodas leaned precariously against bolts of brightly dyed cloth. After this violent assault of colour on our senses we desperately needed to recoup, so returned to a patiently waiting Krish, who drove us back to the hotel. After a tepid shower, Col, Tim, and I lay flat on our beds and allowed the ceiling fan to sweep the warm air over us in shuddering waves. The beds, we later discovered, were rock-hard and unforgiving, and the matching pillows could have propped the heavy front doors open. Three of us lay in that semi-stupor that constitutes sleep on such tropical nights, while the fan simply redistributed the hot, muggy air over our sweaty bodies. Colin  accused me of wriggling around somewhere near midnight. An unfair accusation as it was, in fact, the rhythmic shuddering of the ancient fan unit that was causing continuous agitation of our bedsheets. I had to admit, it did feel like someone fidgeting under the covers. 

Wetlands and Wildlife: Ranganathittu to Mudumalai

After checking out the next morning, we departed for the Ranganathittu Bird Sanctuary, located about an hour's drive away. The sanctuary surrounds a number of tiny, rocky islands nestled within the Cauvery River, specialising in nesting waterbirds, including spoonbills, ibises, cormorants, and herons. We hired a local wooden boat whose captain rowed us for an hour around the islands, charging us a fee that was four times the cost of the standard park entrance ticket. It was very pleasant however, rowing along the cool green waters surrounded by overhanging trees amid hundreds of breeding birds. The painted storks were beautiful and did indeed look as though they had been carefully streaked with the delicate brush of a watercolour artist. We watched them nesting and working their large, rather ungainly bracken-like nests with great care. Then we watched mud sparrows constructing their intricate pocket nests out of wet river mud, lining the interiors with soft white egret feathers. A massive mugger crocodile swam lazily up the river current right between the busy nesting islands. Col was in his element; he whipped out various cameras and telephoto lenses and began clicking away furiously from the bow. Back at the entrance gates, we found a small timber kiosk that advertised both Coca-Cola and Fanta, but discovered that the only beverage actually available was a local soda called ‘Thums Up’. I scanned the tiny list of ingredients on the back of the can, where a statement indicated that the contents contained absolutely NO fruit. Well, whatever it didn't contain, the label forgot to mention that there was also absolutely NO taste; it was in fact, nothing more than guano-flavoured sugar water. A few packets of vile crisps also boldly declared on the packaging that they were ‘ready to eat’—a description that gave one the distinct impression there was very little else to commend them. We returned briefly to the Green Hotel to deposit both Tim and myself in the shade, while Col and Krish disappeared down a track towards a nearby stagnant pond to look for spot-billed pelicans. Only Col could find striding around a muddy peat bog in 40°C heat an interesting afternoon pastime. On the way up to the palace grounds earlier, I had noticed that the local farmers placed huge piles of un-threshed wheat directly onto the public tarmac for the cars, trucks and other passing vehicles to automatically thresh as they rode or drove over the piles; it was rather an ingenious method. A local chap stood near the palace entrance playing a beautiful tune on a traditional wooden flute, which lent a lovely, mystical touch to the atmosphere. While navigating the bird park, I experienced an urgent need for the ladies. A quick look inside the concrete structure that declared itself to be a public toilet, revealed a row of flat porcelain tiles laid directly over a single, long, narrow concrete trough. This setup was apparently designed so that women might squat companionably, four in a row, and relieve themselves whilst simultaneously bonding with total strangers. Thankfully this intimate level of social bladder relief was not required as I was the only person, save for a thin woman with a thick plait of grey hair dressed in a tattered white sari, who was sweeping the floor with a rustic reed brush. She hurriedly scooped a bucket of fresh water out of a plastic barrel and sloshed it down into the concrete trough the moment she saw me enter. She was clearly a member of the Dalit, or ‘untouchable’ caste and sadly that’s exactly what she must have been by the end of the day. I made sure to slip her some coins as I left. I loved the vibrantly coloured flamboyant trees that lined the roadsides. Many beautiful holy shrines were dotted around the countryside near these colourful trees, all intricately designed and depicting various many-limbed Hindu goddesses. Most of them were painted in classic ice-cream colours—pale pistachio green and soft fuchsia pink—with a splash of bright gold paint thrown in for dramatic effect. 
The Mudumalai National Park was about a two-and-a-half-hour drive away, offering interesting scenery along the way, dominated by the ubiquitous roadside shrines and herds of emaciated, long-horned cattle. We eventually crossed the provincial border from Karnataka into the state of Tamil Nadu. Krish pulled over at a small roadside temple, featuring a little stone alcove with a serene Buddha statue inside. Baskets of burning incense, yellowy-ochre and pinkish-red ceremonial powder and garlands of fresh and wilting flower heads were available for worship inside. A sadhu stepped out of the temple shadows and Krish respectfully passed him a few coins for which he was permitted to have some of the bright red powder pressed directly onto his forehead. Tim watched this ritual from the back seat and asked why the ‘funny man’ was busy plastering the driver’s face with curry powder. Krish informed us that sadhus are Hindu or Jain holy ascetics who have renounced worldly life, family, and possessions in order to pursue spiritual enlightenment. I noticed that the local authorities painted the lower portions of the roadside tree trunks with two thick stripes of white paint separated by a thinner middle stripe of brown. Krish told me this specific coating keeps the mosquitoes away. The Monarch Hotel in Mudumalai finally came into view. The low-slung building clung precariously to the steep edge of the mountain like a giant white clam. We enjoyed a quick cup of chai before departing immediately for the nearby game reserve office. There, it started to pour with rain whilst traditional Indian bureaucracy, learned from the British colonial administration, it must be said—reared its ugly head. They operated some peculiar booking system that involved waiting in a long, queue even if you had already purchased and paid for your tickets online, while a sullen official slowly shuffled through massive piles of ink-stained paper in search of bureaucratic proof, completely ignoring any physical evidence you had to offer, such as a printed copy of your official receipt. Furthermore, they charged an extra fee for cameras. Just as I was about to poke his eyes out, the official decided our receipt of payment would suffice and we finally boarded a large, battered Tata bus alongside twenty other local tourists, taking off for an expensive ride around the Mudumalai park. During the trip, our only wildlife sightings consisted of a couple of common peacocks and a few chital. Col declared the entire excursion an absolute waste of money and I agreed. Back at the camp, I had the staff lay a mattress down for Tim on the floor of our wooden-walled room, before heading over to the dining hall to endure yet another heavy curry-laced supper 

On the Road to the Coast

En route to Cochin the next morning, we endured a few terrifying near-misses as Krish apparently enjoyed taking blind mountain corners exceptionally wide, or aggressively overtaking huge commercial buses when oncoming visibility was virtually zero. We did not share his driving enjoyment and clung to our passenger seats for dear life. As we rumbled along the dusty roads, I noticed many Christian churches, Islamic mosques and Hindu temples standing cheek-to-cheek, often positioned right next to a massive political poster depicting some bearded, long-haired communist leader. I marvelled at this apparent landscape of religious and political tolerance. It was surprising to think that Muslims, Christians, Hindus and Communists could live peaceably together, but Krish assured me they do, on the whole, tolerate each other remarkably well in this specific province. I enjoyed the magnificent, sweeping views of the Western Ghats mountain range before we finally arrived in Calicut, hot, dusty just after midday. 
Kozhikode, as the coastal city is also known, is a significant spice trade centre in the southern state of Kerala, located close to Kappad Beach—the site where Portuguese explorer Vasco da Gama first landed in 1498.
For some bizarre reason we were forced to change both our car and our driver at the depot. A man named Babu was assigned to drive us around for the next leg. Babu was incredibly thin and quiet and his English definitely not great, but we were gradually learning to simply shrug off these continuous little travel annoyances. After all, I reasoned, neither of us spoke Hindi or Malayalam. After enjoying a light repast, we finally arrived in the city of Thrissur at around 5 p.m. following a hair-raising journey. Babu’s love for blasting the horn at ten-second intervals was matched only by the number of times this sound was repeated by every other manner of transport on the road, coming at us from all sides in a horrendous, deafening ‘sensurround’. Earlier in the day, I had remarked to Col that back in Saudi Arabia where the driving is notoriously bad, my worry is always that the chaotic traffic rushes up blindly from behind you; whereas in India, the reverse is true. It is frightening to witness how many lanes of oncoming traffic can be aggressively squeezed into a single, narrow strip of tarmac. Add to this the meandering, sacred cows, stray pigs, goats, and packs of dogs and it makes for a thoroughly nail-biting experience—well, when you dare to open your eyes. Personally, I preferred to keep mine firmly closed for most of the drive. Babu eventually led us to the Casino Hotel, a fairly upmarket, family-type establishment. We were allocated a comfortable room—large, airy, and equipped with a huge television set, which Tim fell upon at once like a child deprived. We lay on the bed and watched a fascinating documentary about tigers, which only served to increase our hopes that we might actually get to see one of the big cats in the wild during this holiday. After a quick wash, Col and I walked downstairs to secure a much-needed drink from the quiet sanctuary of the Casino bar. To say it was dimly lit would be putting it mildly. In any event, I had to follow the disembodied sound of Col’s voice just to find out where he was sitting in the gloom. I quickly discovered that the only way to locate my chair was by shuffling slowly backwards until I made contact with the back of my knees. The murky twilight revealed a sulky-looking bartender who laconically poured a large Kingfisher beer. Without use of his vocal cords, he managed to convey the outrageous price of 65 rupees simply by shoving the receipt across the counter. Col bravely asked if they had any nibbles to accompany the drinks, prompting a small packet of mildewed cashew nuts to slide across the bar top. As our eyes gradually adjusted to the dim lighting, a long wall of built-in fish tanks was revealed. Inside, a depressing collection of goldfish and koi swam around in murky green water, gulping frantically at the surface for air. Deciding not to outstay our welcome, we quickly slurped down our beer and left the cave to find out what was on offer for supper. The next morning, breakfast was laid out buffet-style in the dining hall. We sat down and ordered an ‘American’ breakfast, waiting with bated breath to see what interpretation this particular establishment might have. It materialised as a bowl of cornflakes served alongside two peeled, hard-boiled eggs that were as hard as tennis balls, accompanied by a plate of cold toast. They had obviously gone to the same school of hostelry as the other establishments we had visited this week. The butter tasted suspiciously like heavy cooking ghee and the milk was off, so I wisely opted for a strong cup of Taj Mahal—the local tea—whitened with my own little container of long-life milk that I had carried all the way from Tamimi supermarket back in Dhahran. After this select repast, we set off for Munnar and the usual nerve-shredding drive ensued. At one point, three separate overtaking vehicles in the oncoming lane missed our front bumper by a mere hair's breadth. We arrived at the Royal Retreat Hotel in Munnar. Munnar in the picturesque town of Kerala. It was nestled in the Western Ghats mountain range; an historic hill station and former summer resort for the British Raj elite, surrounded by rolling, verdant hills dotted with sprawling tea plantations that were first established in the late 19th century. Although our travel agent, Roshni, had booked us in for a five-day stay, we discovered on arrival that today was not actually one of them. Colin was forced to frantically negotiate and change dates, before we were eventually allocated a small, damp, mushroom-smelling room featuring a continually leaking shower and no view. However as we would only be sleeping in it for a single night we figured it would be acceptable. Col struck up a conversation with a fellow tourist at the hotel who had spent the entire day searching the ridges for wild goats, but he dejectedly reported that he had seen absolutely nothing. We remained hopeful that we’d have more luck. Mind you, Colin—ever the pessimist—announced that we wouldn't see a thing and that he would be forced to leave Southern India without having laid eyes on these rare mountain goats. Although I’m as fond of mountain goats as anyone, I was still rather hoping we might spot a tiger at some point during this trip. We returned to the damp, cold, fungus-smelling room, where I discovered I had to tread through several inches of water just to navigate the bathroom floor. When perched precariously on the cracked toilet seat, you were faced with two choices: either lift your legs into the air for the duration of your ablutions, or let your feet rest in tepid, greasy floodwater. Neither prospect was particularly pleasing. 

The Heights of Eravikulam

It felt like waking up inside a cave the next morning. Tim’s mattress and our bedsheets felt damp and clammy from the mountain mist and I was feeling irritable as a result. We dried ourselves down as best we could, drank our version of ‘safe’ tea—utilising boiled water and powdered milk mixed by my own hand—and drove down to the Eravikulam National Park to see if we could locate the rare Nilgiri tahr (the aforementioned goat). We arrived at the slopes of Anamudi, Southern India’s highest peak at 2,695 metres, at eight in the morning, finding the mountain air cool and pleasant. Babu opted to stay behind in the warmth of the car while the three of us began the long climb up the road towards the top of the ridge. Col’s sharp eyes soon spotted our very first tahr, silhouetted high above us on the rocky crest of the mountain. It was rather exciting to see it standing so perfectly still, gazing majestically over the vast valley below. Imagine our surprise when, just a little further along the walking path, we came across a small family of tahrs scattered casually along the corner of the road. We crept along slowly and quietly so as not to startle them and subsequently spent a very satisfying twenty minutes photographing them. It was a wonderful, opportunity to observe them up close in the quiet of the early morning. That calm wilderness experience came to an abrupt, screeching halt half an hour later with the sudden, noisy arrival of the local masses. Their blaring hooters, shouting voices and sputtering vehicles instantly rent the still morning air and successfully scared the shy tahrs into the thick undergrowth. One real eejit of a tour driver beeped his horn merrily just as Col and I were trying to photograph a female and her young calf. The passengers laughed raucously when we showed our displeasure. The more we shouted at them to please be quiet, the more they laughed, beeping their horns louder and hanging halfway out of the windows and waving their arms in a deranged fashion. We saw a couple of park officials sitting in a patrol truck a bit further up the track but they merely shrugged their shoulders indifferently when we explained what had happened. After sliding back into the sweltering leather seats of the Ambassador, Babu drove us slowly back towards the Copper Hills Hotel, passing many beautiful tea plantations en route. According to Col, these commercial tea estates were ‘actively ruining the natural indigenous forest’, a statement he delivered with a sharp, accusatory glare at me. Hell, I only drink the bloody stuff; I can’t help where they choose to grow it. 
Babu, at his own insistence, had been sleeping in the car overnight. He was looking visibly sick—sweating profusely and coughing continuously. Col gave him some medicine. We’re suspecting he might be suffering from either tuberculosis or malaria, given the propensity for our drivers to carry one or the other. Later he drove us over a muddy river bridge to the local country club, where we were hoping to enjoy a few cold beers. However, a grey-haired man wearing a neat white shirt and pressed trousers gave our casual attire a disparaging stare, coldly informing us that as non-members, we were not welcome. No, he didn't happen to know if there were any other ‘drinking establishments’ operating nearby. We thanked him dryly and asked Babu to keep driving so we could search for an alternative pub. Col was by now desperate for a cold Kingfisher but the closest we came to a drink was breathing in the thick alcoholic fumes of a local pedestrian, who gave us a series of garbled, booze-laden directions to a spot where he claimed we could acquire some alcoholic brew. The establishment he was describing must have been a figment of his inebriated imagination, because we found absolutely nothing down those tracks except dead-end dirt roads and a tremendous amount of mud. Eventually we turned around and returned somewhat dejectedly to the hotel, which we knew was run by a Muslim family, meaning there was nary a drop of alcohol to be found on the premises either. ‘It’s not the end of the world,’ I told a miserable Col. ‘There’s always tea.’ He glared in reply. Col had handed a butterfly carefully wrapped in paper to our waiter the previous night, asking him to store it in the kitchen fridge overni ght to preserve the specimen. That waiter has been giving our table wide berth ever since. Our next breakfast took on a distinct Fawlty Towers quality, featuring a rapid succession of linguistic misinterpretations that led to bowls of cornflakes and porridge continuously arriving, disappearing, reappearing separately, or failing to manifest at all. We asked for simple boiled eggs and a plate of eggs on toast arrived instead. I wanted black tea with no milk, but received a cup of pre-made, heavily milky tea. 
The interior of the car, with Babu sleeping, coughing, spluttering, and spitting inside it all night long, had taken on the distinct pong of the unwashed, paired with the occasional tubercle bacillus. It was a nightmare to be seated for any length of time within its foetid confines. I tried holding my breath as we drove, but was forced to give up the attempt when Tim persisted in asking me a string of questions that required the act of exhalation in order to answer him. While gazing out of the back window at the passing villages, I noticed that the sheer number of women making up the hard manual labour force in this country was mind-boggling. When local houses were being built and bricks cemented into place, it was the women who carried the large, flat baskets of wet, heavy cement balanced on their heads. When the roads were being tarred in the blistering heat, it was the women who actively shovelled and carried the hot tar along to the men. Meanwhile, massive, lurid movie posters featuring the latest Bollywood romantic actors were plastered across the crumbling concrete walls and every other available space. They invariably depicted the same smouldering, moustachioed actor holding a variety of multi-sari-clad women in positions of intense, mock passion. I noticed sadly that many local hotels featured prominent signs stating they did not support child labour, which naturally led one to the conclusion that many businesses did. 

The Thattekkad Sanctuary

For lunch, the menu presented us with a culinary choice of ‘paultry’, ‘bailed’ or ‘pouched eggs’, so we dutifully clucked our choices to the waiter and quietly dreamed of juicy double cheeseburgers. Babu waited for us in the car while we, in turn, waited on the riverbank for the car ferry to transport us across to the other side of the Thattekkad Bird Sanctuary—home to the magnificent Malabar grey hornbill, woodpeckers, rollers, and vibrant parakeets. It was about 38°C in the shade and we had each sweated away our daily fluid intake by midday. The local ferry carried ourselves, a commercial bus, a 4x4, and about forty-five other vehicles across the wide Periyar River where we disembarked and walked to the sanctuary gates. There, we met the absolute pinnacle of Indian bureaucracy in the form of a rotund, heavily sweating official with atrocious halitosis. He ignored us until every single local tourist had been served before granting us a modicum of eye contact, despite the fact that we had been first in the queue. He abruptly demanded to see our passports. In a bird park? Anyway we reluctantly complied, waiting a further ten minutes while he slowly studied our passport photographs and visas, had a spot of lunch, took a brief nap, and finally slid the documents back across the wooden ledge whilst simultaneously quoting an outrageous international entry fee. We parted with a few centimetres of rupee notes and walked away from the heat, humidity and halitosis into the relative coolth of the forest canopy. Col immediately commenced the weird, balletic movements that chasing butterflies in extreme temperatures entails, while Tim and I took a distinctly more clumsy approach, trying to catch a few specimens using our sun hats. Later, following two hours of baking in the sun led to a desperate, dehydrated search for a cold drink. None of the roadside stalls offered anything safe, featuring only old Coke bottles filled with fluids of a highly dubious, neon colour. A trek past the dusty roadside shacks revealed little more than empty glass jars of sun-bleached boiled sweets and yellow, greasy buns. Eventually, a small stall appeared housing a decrepit—but miraculously humming—fridge stocked with club soda. We spent an age debating the safety of the contents before unadulterated thirst finally dictated the purchase of three bottles. Standing in the intense heat, with nothing left to do but drink, praying the contents weren't simply bottled river water. Throughout this process of indecision, an old geezer dressed in a dusty, torn lungi hobbled up to spectate, spitting copious amounts of bright red betel nut juice directly onto the murram earth. He presented a magnificent vision of crimson-toned horror, watching us open-mouthed until our transaction was finally completed. So, quenched with questionable fluids we returned to ‘happy chappie’ at the gate. He tried, but failed to smile a welcome and then grudgingly—as though we were annoying irritations rather than paying guests—took us for a quick tour of the grounds. We saw Asian palm civets, though they were currently occupying neither a tree nor a palm, looked at some partially hidden snakes in a concrete enclosure and peered at a few fish swimming in a small, murky aquarium. It was a bit of a moth-eaten group of paraphernalia but at least the staff had made an effort, which is what counts. It was still intensely hot and the warm club soda we had guzzled earlier had hardly touched sides, so we decided to embark on a boat trip down the river channel. We were led towards a distinctly rickety-looking wooden boat after parting with yet another small fortune in banknote notes. The vessel proved to be surprisingly solid once on board, with not a single leak in sight and we spent a lovely hour sailing down the river, admiring the lush surrounding foliage and vibrant red Thechi flowers (Jungle Geraniums). It was much cooler out on the water which helped immensely. The rower dropped us off on the far side of the river, where a very relieved-looking Babu was waiting to meet us, anxious that we had disappeared into the jungle. 
Under the distinct impression that we were going to be served a Western breakfast the following morning, given the excellent supper we had enjoyed the night before, I was surprised by the usual array of heavily curried substances that greeted our wary gaze at the buffet. I ordered a simple cheese omelette for Tim, enunciating the words with exaggerated care so there would be absolutely no room for error. The waiter held my gaze and informed me that they did’nt serve Western breakfasts. Undeterred, I marched to our room and brought down the printed room service menu, which clearly advertised that the kitchen did, in fact, serve Western fare—or at least a reasonable semblance of it. Col tried his best to pretend he didn't know the woman holding the menu, a strategy that failed when Tim said, ‘Mummy, I don’t want hot stuff!’  We ended up with a plate of ‘bawled’ eggs and a few strips of local bacon that had been fried to an absolute crisp. I had a nagging suspicion afterwards that the kitchen staff may be Muslim, which would explain their reluctance to handle or serve bacon and the realisation made me feel guilty for having made such a fuss. I realise I am on occasion, that awful Western tourist that everyone detests. 
Another hair-raising drive ensued and I took my mind off any imminent death by watching the local men's many and varied uses for their lungis. When worn loose, the garments hang down to the knees like a skirt, but as the day heats up, the fabric ends are caught up and tied with a few deft movements around the waist, giving a distinct, nappy-like appearance. Some men simply hang onto the two loose corners of their lungi as they walk, clutching them in both hands; others use the cloth to wipe their sweaty faces. I witnessed one man blow his nose copiously into one corner of the fabric. All lungis were paired with a standard shirt, most of which were worn unbuttoned in the heat. By contrast, the women’s dress in these parts consisted of a long, nightie-like gown featuring an elaborate, high frill at the neck—somewhat Victorian for modern India. 
Tim’s laboratory blood results came back via Nasser, testing negative for malaria and positive for typhoid. His bowels were working overtime for most of the day resulting in a continuous stream of watery stools. Luckily Col had given him an anti-typhoid injection before we left Cape Town so it manifested as a mild case. 

Return to the Kingdom

The flight back to Saudi Arabia passed relatively uneventfully and we landed in Khobar midday. Col secured a local taxi to take us to the military hospital accommodation. Then the final security hurdle of navigating our way through Gate One and Gate Two before the cab finally deposited us outside the door of our compound apartment, G1-45. As we walked into the apartment, I noticed that the central air conditioning was off. The gruff, sleepy voice at the other end of the maintenance phone uttered the dreaded words: ‘Mafeesh mushkila. We fix tomorrow, Inshallah!’ We knew by now from our years in the Kingdom that ‘don’t worry’ usually translates as, ‘start worrying immediately’, and ‘Inshallah’—while meaning ‘if God wills it’—usually meant when hell freezes over. Come back India, all is forgiven.
