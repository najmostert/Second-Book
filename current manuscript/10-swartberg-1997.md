# Chapter 8: Swartberg 1997

The Swartberg is a dramatic mountain range known for its diverse geology and unique Cape ecosystems, including the famous Swartberg Pass where we were spending a few days exploring the region. Col had again won an award—the annual AGFA photography competition. His photograph depicted a vibrant clownfish and he had secured first place in the underwater section. We were due at the official ceremony later this evening. I would have grown a beard waiting for Col because at 4:30 he phoned to say I had to come and pick him up in town. Paul, Col’s boss, was busy holding court when I arrived at the laboratory and I had to restrain from poking him in the eye with my car keys; he was not known for showing any consideration when it came to his colleague taking time off. Col managed to back away from the meeting and we were soon hurtling towards the Kirstenbosch National Botanical Gardens where the exhibition was being held. We needn't have rushed because the organisers were still setting up the displays when we arrived. We helped ourselves to our name cards and stood around trying to look inconspicuous as people rushed past carrying large, framed photographs. I tried to wolf down a large, gourmet butterball encrusted with sesame seeds, which turned out to be pure blue cheese. I absolutely detest blue cheese and promptly coughed the offending piece into a linen serviette. A husband-and-wife team had first prize overall with their photograph. The husband was rather dishy so I quickly wiped the greasy sesame seeds off my face and smiled warmly at them. Col chatted to Patrick Wagner the editor of Getaway magazine. Our photograph was taken standing proudly next to Col’s winning entry of a tropical parrotfish captured in motion. 
The following day, I sorted out Nanny and Tim comfortably in the back seat and we set off. It became hotter still by the time we reached Laingsburg, where we stopped to replenish Nanny’s tannin levels with tea. After ordering I nipped off to the loo and was quite chuffed when I heard someone whistling at me on the way back. I was just congratulating myself on the fact that I ‘still had it’ when I realised the sound was emanating from a stuffed parrot on the wall. Talk about a reality check. We headed back out onto the road towards Seweweekspoort. A small, structural crack in the back-door seal of the car allowed thick white dust to enter the cabin and envelop everyone. It was intensely hot by now. Tim’s preferred way of coping with the journey was to moan incessantly about the heat before falling fast asleep and emitting poisonous farts until we reached Oudtshoorn. I had accidentally smeared his face with quick-tan lotion this morning under the impression that it was suncream and he was now sporting bright ginger eyebrows and a matching orange face. He also featured a few dark brown patches on the back of his legs where I had failed to rub it in properly. I am clearly never going to win Mother of the Year at this rate. The light was incredibly bright and the sun’s glare bore down mercilessly on the windscreen as we drove. I handed Col my sunglasses—the ones with the large, round, shiny mirrored circles and put on a cheap, cyclamen-pink pair I had bought, which made me look like a laboratory experiment gone wrong. Col meanwhile, tried his best to look effortlessly cool, missing the mark by a mere millimetre. As the temperature rose higher and higher I became anxious about Nanny sitting in the back who was not tolerating the Karoo heat. She was turning visibly pink and limp. Col tried his best to reassure her that we were almost there but the road seemed endless. We were just about to give up and pull over when we reached ‘Ou Tol’, the historic spot where we had rented a cottage for the long weekend. Our designated rondavels were zebra-themed and featured a comfortable bedroom each complete with an en-suite shower. A small kitchenette with a fridge lay just off the large central lounge. Once Nanny declared herself much better after a cold drink, we left her to rest in the shade, and the three of us took a walk up the lower slopes of the Swartberg Pass. 
The next morning we trooped silently down to the dining room for breakfast. Tim didn't want to eat anything and instead focused his energy on running wildly around the room, bumping into expensive antiques and rare artefacts. Nanny ordered her morning tea but it arrived at the table as Rooibos, the local red-leaf herbal tea. She declared firmly that she didn't like ‘rooey-booi’ and ordered another pot of proper Ceylon tea. Then she complained that the ‘margareen’ wasn't real butter which she preferred. A slight linguistic misunderstanding ensued regarding the toast that Col had ordered and my own tea arrived far too weak. I distinctly heard the staff sigh with relief as we departed and piled into the car to visit the Cango Caves, with me remarking brightly that, 
‘It’ll clear up soon,’ to which Col replied flatly, ‘No, it won’t! It’s going to rain all weekend!’ 
The caves were spectacular and despite my inherent claustrophobia I enjoyed viewing the strange, towering clusters of stalactites and stalagmites. It was slightly musty and highly humid inside the caverns but I walked as far into the depths as the ‘chimney’. Taking a quick look at the length of the narrow, dark passageway ahead, I decided I had seen quite enough of the underworld and turned back to rejoin the others in the main chambers. 
This morning, we walked over to the small dining room for breakfast, bringing with us our standard request for items they didn't have, or being handed food we hadn't asked for. Because we had been mistakenly served Rooibos tea yesterday, the moment a large ceramic teapot arrived at the table Nanny immediately assumed it was the herbal variety. She once again declared that she didn't like ‘rooey-booi’ and asked for black coffee instead. Col chimed in to say he didn't mind Rooibos but would also prefer a cup of coffee. I looked at the steaming pot and said pragmatically,
‘Well, I don’t mind Rooibos so I’ll drink it myself.’ The waitress looked perplexed as she watched me pour myself a perfectly acceptable, steaming cup of standard Ceylon tea. The moment the rest of the family saw it was normal tea, everyone at the table decided they wanted a cup after all and ordered another pot. By this stage the wretched waitress was actively backing away from our table. Then came the usual palaver about the toast being white versus brown bread, followed by a complaint that the breakfast sausages were ‘too spicy’. Finally, Nanny beckoned the waitress over to the table and said, ‘Ay don’t like “margareen”, please bring me buttah. Also you could wriggle bulldogs through the holes in this tablecloth!’ We could only check in at our next accommodation at 3 p.m. so Col pulled over at a roadside picnic site and set off with his butterfly net in one hand, wearing a look of fierce determination. The rest of us drove over the pass to Prince Albert to enjoy a picnic lunch. At 68.5 kilometres, Prince Albert  is the longest accessible mountain pass in South Africa. I picked Col up later that afternoon and drove slowly along the unpaved road to the Cango Nature Reserve where we would be staying the night. It was Christmas Eve so we headed back to Cape Town the following day.
