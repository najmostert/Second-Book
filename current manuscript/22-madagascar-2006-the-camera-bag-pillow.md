# Chapter 19: Madagascar 2006: The Camera Bag Pillow

As our first trip had proved so incredibly interesting, we decided to visit Madagascar for a secondary expedition. We had a massive mountain of luggage with us, but what else is new for our family? Whereas I like to think I pack logically for what is actually required for the holiday, Col completely foregoes unimportant items like, let’s say, clothing, in favour of packing extra heavy telephoto lenses for his cameras and twenty more packets of spare film. He shoved Tim's hand luggage into a large canvas carry-all that was already bursting with camera equipment, and told Tim he was to casually sling the heavy bag over his shoulder as though it contained no more than a light travel pillow. Col opened the garage door to load the car, which instantly set our house alarm off and delayed our departure further. We need not have rushed down the highway, however, as our plane flight faced a delay. Our boarding time was suddenly announced over the loudspeaker at the exact moment Tim had bought us a cold drink each. We had to empty the contents as quickly as we could down our throats, causing a lot of excess gas and loud burping from the three of us on the tarmac. For some bizarre reason, the airline system had seated our family in completely separate seats on board. I had a very large lady sitting next to me in the window seat. Her ample thighs heavily encroached into my seat space, forcing my body into a coy position with my shoulders narrowed and my legs pressed primly together. It was not a comfortable trip at all, but luckily it was a relatively short flight, and soon we were landing in Johannesburg. Tim manfully held onto his oversized bag while we grabbed our many pieces of ‘hand luggage’ and disembarked into the terminal with a few hours to spare. We saunter sauntering slowly over to Gate 49 about half an hour before boarding, only to discover to our horror that our plane had been shifted to Gate 2—about five kilometres away! We ran as fast as three people carrying double their body weight in hand luggage humanly can (i.e., not very fast at all) and made the threshold with mere seconds to spare. It seems the pilots had parked the aircraft at the wrong gate entirely. I hope the airport traffic wardens gave them a ticket. At the gate counter, we were greeted by two airline employees whom I privately dubbed Freda Flintstone and Happy Harry. I could have cheerfully strangled both of them on the spot. There was absolutely no apology offered for having us rush to the gate from the next village; they just stood there behind the desk while ‘Harry’ calmly chomped his way through a large bag of Niknaks, sprinkling a fine dusting of orange cheese powder onto his tie while engaging in a long, involved conversation with ‘Freda’ that was peppered with clicks and deep sighs. They eventually took our proffered tickets (Harry left a large orange thumbprint directly on my pass), and we hoisted ourselves onto a shuttle bus and tiredly swayed our way towards the Boeing aircraft that stood waiting for its passengers. The cabin seats were so small that our movement was entirely restricted to shallow breathing. Col's knees were pushed up to his chest level, and he did not look happy at all. It was incredibly muggy and claustrophobic on board, and I spent the first ten minutes in the air fiddling frantically with the plastic overhead toggle thingy, trying to direct a jet of cool air onto my melting visage. I succeeded only in ejecting a weak blast of warm humidity for my pains. I sat back irritably in my seat after trying unsuccessfully to move my seat back recliner, waiting for the safety seatbelt sign to go off so I could toddle over to the loo. I suddenly spotted three vacant seats further down the aisle and on my return, I told Col I was moving there. He joined me and we left Tim sprawled across the three newly vacated seats. 

Arrival in Tana and Hotel Argeme 

Due to our late take-off, we only arrived at Tana after 11 p.m. with a very bumpy landing. There was a horrifically large queue in the airport as a massive Air France Boeing had just landed ahead of us. We eventually made it to the other side of passport control, collected our mountain of luggage, and found a rather wilted Hari waiting for us with a handheld sign declaring the words, 'Mestorts fambly'. We had met her on our last trip to Madagascar and had enlisted her services again. Col immediately left me to chat to Hari while he went off to exchange Rand notes into a wheelbarrow of Ariary. I was tired and irritable, and my brain cells rummaged into their depths from forty-odd years ago to come up with a string of French words that would pass for intelligible, and failed by a verb or two. Hari looked puzzled and said no, she didn't want any wine. Strong, purposeful hands suddenly lunged for the pile of luggage at my feet. I yelled a few, ‘Non, non! Merci!’ to prevent the removal of our baggage by two eager porters to the awaiting voiture. They had been hovering over us since our pale—read ‘wealthy’—faces first entered the waiting hall. Col appeared looking strangely misshapen of torso. His birder's jacket, that of the many pockets, had bulges everywhere. He muttered that several million Ariary notes had been acquired and were now safely stuffed in his pockets. We followed Hari into the car park where the company 4x4 awaited us. It was a newish model and boded well for our future travel plans. The Hotel Argeme appeared after a circuitous route through Tana. The narrow streets and haphazard houses were incredibly reminiscent of our trip to Nepal a few years before. The hotel was nestled down a narrow cobbled street and tucked away in the corner—a beautiful remnant of its colonial past. There were many interesting antiques, bric-a-brac, and items of interest. A large wooden rickshaw model stood on the counter where we checked in. A beautiful silver and bronze peacock stood outside the door to our chambre, which was very clean and airy inside. A bar fridge huddled under a small counter and a huge mosquito-netted bed came complete with the ubiquitous French bedroll, which Col instantly flung onto the floor. What is it with cushions and men, I wonder. Even at home, there’s a continuous removal and replacement of cushions in the lounge. In the lounge, we encountered a group of très raucous Frenchmen in very high spirits (mentally and physically, it would seem). Apparently, France had just won some major sporting event. We repaired to our chambre for a shower. I was horrified to discover it was 12:45 a.m. local time, as we were to be up by 6:30 a.m. for petit déjeuner. I was really tired but tossed and turned like a basting turkey until the early hours. 

Exploring Andohahela Parc 

I couldn't believe the sheer cheek of our alarm clock when its shrill tones startled me out of my dreams and alerted me to the fact that 6:30 a.m. was almost upon us. A splash of cold water about the visage helped. I had to restrain Col from practising his French for, while his recall is passable, his pronunciation is absolutely not. Petit déjeuner consisted of yoghurt, bananas, rolls, and croissants washed down with some strong coffee. Hari suddenly appeared with Dina in tow. She wished us a smiling, ‘Bonjour.’ We had met Dina before—a small, dynamic woman who runs the Setam show with great efficiency. She presented us with our itinerary and entry tickets for the duration of our trip. She also handed us two data cards for Orange, the local network provider. We left with Hari for the airport. Tim and I sat in the back of the Land Cruiser watching the scenery move swiftly by: many buildings in various states of repair, vast rice paddies, and wooden stalls stacked high with oranges and other seasonal fruit. Women in colourful cloth and pork-pie hats wandered along the paths, clutching either baskets of rice or young children. Some of the baskets were heavily laden with washing or wood for fires. The colourful wooden pousse-pousses ambled by in huge numbers. At passport control, there was some delay because our hand luggage was deemed overweight. Col kept repeating, ‘Camera!’ while I said, ‘Computer!’ Eventually, the officials gave up and waved us through. At around ten to eleven, we boarded the small plane to Fort Dauphin, on the south-east coast of Madagascar. The crew had just enough time to fling a poisson-flavoured roll and a bottle of water at us before we were touching down. We were met with thick, oppressive humidity and struggled with our heavy hand luggage to the waiting bus. A few other couples joined the coach and we set off through the seaside town of Tôlanaro to our hotel. The sea looked incredibly rough and the waves were high. The roads were not at all good and the ride was very bumpy, but the hotel was set in pleasant surroundings. A soft rain mixed with the heat to create a steamy jungle vibe. A tall, thin man wearing a grey cap joined our group and introduced himself as 'Gooti', declaring his services as our local guide. He apologised for interrupting our lunch but said that due to the recent heavy rainfall, the roads were now completely impassable to the forest camp. He suggested we visit the transition forest instead, which included a spiny forest. Col was a little disappointed as he had some exotic butterflies in mind to add to his ever-expanding collection, but we agreed it was better to change our plans, especially as climbing with heavy suitcases to the other camp would have been extremely unpleasant with the muddy paths. Col and I headed out for a short walk down to the harbour after lunch. Small, alcoved lean-tos housed locals huddled around coal braziers, surrounding them in thick plumes of steam. A huge oil tanker bobbed gently in the grey sea while the skeletal remains of ill-fated vessels lay abandoned on the beach, bearing silent testament to the rough seas and inclement weather this side of Madagascar. We walked up to a small stone church where the beautiful sounds of a choir emanated from its holy depths and added to the general atmosphere. 

Camping in the Transition Forest 

On Monday, 3rd July, Gooti, a driver, and a cook climbed into our hired vehicle, so the seating was a bit squashed. I had my laptop balanced on my knee, Tim was lolling over my shoulder, and Gooti was wedged next to him. The cook was practically buried under a mountain of luggage in the back. The drive was fascinating, and we watched the landscape change from lush rainforest to dry transition forest. Gooti spoke very good English; he said he was entirely self-taught, and we enjoyed some interesting conversations. After several stops along the way at local villages for extra water and more supplies, one stop revealed the presence of rare pitcher plants (Nepenthes madagascariensis). Col and I jumped out immediately to photograph these fascinating insectivorous plants. The road soon deteriorated into mostly giant potholes filled with pure mud and dirt. The bumpy track did nothing to help Tim, who sat grim-faced throughout the journey while his guts churned uncomfortably. We passed the protected area where the famous three-cornered palm (Dypsis decaryi) is found; it is the only place in the world where this unique species exists natively. We arrived at last at the entrance to Andohahela National Park, where the official 'office' was no more than a basic grass-roofed lean-to where two rangers sat on plastic chairs next to a small wooden desk. Tim and I lay down on a mattress on the riverbank while the crew set up camp. Col had already disappeared into the nearby forest, as is his wont. It was lovely and beautifully cool by the river. We were feeling quite peckish and I was wondering what on earth would be offered for lunch, as I hadn't seen much more than various fruits stored in the back of the Land Cruiser. I sniffed suspiciously as a plume of woodsmoke trailed towards us. We had brought some long-legged chickens along, but they looked incredibly scrawny to my eye. I heard from Gooti that the porters were going to buy some wild hog meat from a hunter halfway up the mountain. Half an hour later, we were called for lunch to find a makeshift wooden table and some rickety benches had been set up under the trees. The meal was surprisingly delicious, given the circumstances: a starter of potato, cucumber, and boiled egg, followed by a superb prawn and ginger dish served with rice and some fresh rolls. Gooti brought us a piece of the wild hog to taste; I politely deferred, and Tim didn't eat much as his stomach was still incredibly delicate. After lunch, I brewed some tea in a small bowl, and then Tim and I returned to our mattress next to the water, spending an hour relaxing in the cool breeze. I was busy transferring digital photos from my camera to the laptop when a shadow suddenly fell over me. I looked up, startled, to find a small lad smiling down at me. He was wearing tatty shorts and a torn T-shirt, but his smile was wide, bright, and wholesome. He said his name was Constantine, he was eleven years old, and he wasn't any taller than my elbow. His English was minimal and halting, so I tried out some French. The result was an interesting conversation conducted entirely in Franglais. He told me he lived nearby and that his father worked for ANGAP. He said he usually went to school by bus, but he eventually tired of our conversation and slipped quietly away into the trees. Later on, Col, Tim, and I went for a long, circuitous walk around the riverbank and into the transition forest. There was a fair bit of rock-hopping involved, but we successfully located baobabs and unique succulents en route, along with a beautiful waterfall and a few cool streams. After two hours of exploring, we headed back to camp. The wooden table had been moved closer to the vehicle, and the crew had stuck candles inside sand-filled Coke bottles that had been cut in half, which brought sufficient light for us to see exactly what we were eating. 

Spiny Forest and Night-Lens Lemurs 

Around 3 a.m., I needed an urgent wee, so I crept outside barefoot and squatted the prerequisite distance from the tent canvas. Bladder successfully emptied, I walked gingerly back to the tent over the gravelly river sand, bunching my toes tightly as I walked to avoid the sharp stones. As I reached the tent flap, my toes closed around a loose pile of sandy gravel. As I swung my leg into the zippered opening, my toes unexpectedly unfurled and released their entire contents—roughly half a cup of coarse gravel—straight over Col's sleeping face and hair! To say he was thoroughly displeased is to make light of the moment; he was instantly startled into furious wakefulness, frantically scrabbling as he attempted to empty his mouth and hair of coarse grit. My frantic attempts at an apology were somewhat hampered by my own uncontrollable laughter. It had become a lot cooler by dawn, and there was even some thick dew settling around the edges of the tent canvas. The provided cups were so small as to be entirely useless in my opinion, so I resorted to drinking my hot tea straight out of a tin bowl. Following this repast, we commenced a long walk through the spiny forest to reach our next camp; the car was to meet our group there after packing away all the gear. It was a long, hot walk but not too physically strenuous. I thoroughly enjoyed looking for giraffe weevils, chameleons, and different unique foliage types, such as the famous octopus tree (Didierea madagascariensis), fan palms, and many other plants unique to the country. We arrived at the entrance to the spiny forest reserve after an eight-kilometre trek. There wasn't much infrastructure here besides a small thatch-roofed office, a zebu pen, and a demarcated dirt car park. Running water wasn’t available either, but we had wisely brought some containers from our previous camp. After resting for a while on some plastic chairs, Col promptly disappeared into the brush with his net. Later on, the staff set up a large wooden table for lunch: rice and vegetables served alongside a spicy chicken and potato stew, followed by delicious baked bananas in a rich chocolate sauce. It is truly amazing what those cooks can accomplish using only a couple of basic charcoal burners! Several mangy local hounds waited expectantly at our knees for a dropped morsel or two. That evening, the cook made our family a huge plate of fresh pancakes. So much food! We embarked on a guided night walk after supper to successfully walk off some of the heavy calories. We headed directly into the pitch darkness, head torches ablaze, and soon came right across a white-fronted mouse lemur nestled tightly in a spiny tree, followed shortly by a beautiful reddish mouse lemur (Microcebus rufus). I discovered that the absolute trick to tracking nocturnal primates is to shine the torch beam steadily into the branches and watch for the distinct, reflective glint of an eye; once you focus your beam on that glint, a tiny lemur is usually revealed. I spotted five individual lemurs this way. There were also a few sleeping sifakas huddled high up in a tree trunk—unfortunately far too high for our beams to see clearly. Back in camp, we announced to the kitchen that we weren't at all hungry, just incredibly thirsty, but when our supper platters arrived, the food was so delicious we devoured it all anyway: tender and juicy zebu kebabs grilled with tomato, onion, and aubergine, served with rice. We used a plastic bucket and a cup to wash our hands, and I sent out a hearty, ‘Bonsoir!’ into the night before retiring to our tent. 

Berenty Bound: The Hidden Flush 

After entering the secondary spiny forest path the next morning, we finally came to the established camp where we were originally supposed to have stayed. A small group of sifakas were perching high in a tree canopy. Gooti expertly pointed out a white-fronted mouse lemur curled up asleep inside its tree hole quite close to the walking path, so we were able to take some excellent close-up photos. The campsite was significantly nicer than the one we had occupied, but it was totally inaccessible by car due to the mud. In the continuing, historic saga that involves me and my public ablutions, I suddenly needed the loo, and our guide pointed vaguely in the general direction of the bush. I couldn't find a path, so I simply squatted down behind some crumbling stone fireplaces. I was just dusting myself off, so to speak, when the guide suddenly materialized from the leaves and announced, ‘Les toilettes sont ici!’ He pointed directly behind my shoulder, and there, half-hidden by some thick Thilachium latifolium bushes, was a proper, modern flush toilet housed in a clean cement enclosure! I blushed a bright crimson, muttered awkwardly that I hadn't seen the structure, and did a swift side-shuffle straight back to the car. Benoir, our new professional guide for the next three days, met our family at the crossroads and introduced himself. He spoke excellent English but with a remarkably strong American accent which I tried my absolute best not to feel internally irritated by. How it is that most non-English speaking people the world over choose to learn American English with its accompanying twang— which bears very little structural resemblance to the original British English—is entirely beyond my comprehension! Tim whispered that I desperately needed to ‘chill’, and he was probably entirely right. The bus rumbled heavily over the uneven dirt roads, and we gazed through the dusty windows at the passing rows of sisal fields before finally arriving at the famous Berenty Reserve, eager to see what the next few days of wildlife viewing would bring. The reserve appeared completely empty of other international visitors besides our family. Our private cabin featured two bedrooms and a sizeable shower recess. We enjoyed some cold drinks at the restaurant deck, looking out over the red dust and the sparse thorn trees, before setting out to look for primates. Within minutes, we found our very first beautiful ring-tailed lemurs (Lemur catta) resting in a tree. 

The Sunning Ringtails and the Ghost of the Forest 

The next morning, Col and I joined Benoir for an extensive walk around the Berenty estate; it is a huge property. We soon saw our very first sunning ringtails sitting in a neat row on a log, completely frozen in classic yoga poses with their arms outstretched and their white bellies exposed directly to the hot morning sun. Further down the path, we located a troop of Verreaux's sifakas with their dark brown faces and thick white furry bodies, sitting quietly in a large Didierea tree. Col generously lent Tim his digital camera for the loop, so our son was happily snapping away in pure delight at some sifakas nearby. Around six o'clock, just as it was getting dark in the forest, we walked back to the lodge for some hot chocolate, then joined Benoir in the combi van for our official night walk. We enjoyed some exceptional sightings of mouse lemurs, brown lemurs, sportive lemurs (Lepilemur), white-footed sportive lemurs, and a fat-tailed dwarf lemur tonight. It was all incredibly exciting, and we chattered animatedly en route to the dining room, where delicious crab crêpes, chicken, and bananas awaited our table. There was some traditional Malagasy dancing organized tonight around a huge bonfire in the clearing. About eight dancers dressed in bright red costumes, accompanied by a few local musicians playing an accordion, gave us a wonderfully lively performance. At one stage—probably after my second glass of potent coconut rum—I unexpectedly got up from my seat and joined the troupe on the sand for a roaring 'Malagasy Mamba' dance, which was met with great hilarity and cheering from the staff. I awoke the next morning to an intensely bright light shining down directly onto my face; I had completely forgotten to switch off our bedroom lights last night, and the camp generators usually automatically cut off at midnight. Breakfast was nothing startling to write home about, consisting of cold sweet potato, a hard French loaf cut into jagged pieces, and fruit jams of indiscriminate origin. For some bizarre reason, stale Madeira cake was also part of this unappealing, carb-laden morning fare; luckily, my tea did not fail me. My specialized powdered milk-making process was watched in pure amusement by a British girl who passed by our table, announcing to the room that the lodge did indeed possess fresh milk. I told her firmly that ever since Tim had become dangerously ill in India from consuming unpasteurised milk, I was absolutely not taking any chances with local diaries. She nodded understandingly, mentioning that she currently worked at a zoo in Bristol, UK, focusing mainly on primate husbandry, and had just returned from doing field research in the northern part of Madagascar. Tim, Col, and I joined Benoir for another extensive forest walk. It was beautifully cool under the canopy, and we watched the early morning stirrings of a few ring-tailed lemurs and sifakas. Then, we suddenly came right across a rare silky sifaka (Propithecus candidus) sitting high in the canopy. The silky sifaka is a large, magnificent lemur characterised, as its descriptive name implies, by long, snowy-white, silky fur. It has an incredibly restricted geographic range and is known locally to the Malagasy as the simpona or the ‘ghost of the forest’. It is officially one of the rarest mammals on Earth, making this an incredibly privileged wildlife sighting for our journal. Benoir whispered excitedly that there were only four individual silky sifakas currently living in this entire forest sector! We were absolutely ecstatic at seeing this beautiful creature. We then followed a group of red-fronted brown lemurs across the red earth road, where they immediately commenced their morning yoga sunning ritual. They scampered around happily after warming themselves, and Tim, who was still clutching Col's digital camera, took some lovely, clear photos of them jumping from tree to tree. I sat on a log and chatted with Benoir about his family life; he has four children but mentioned he only gets to see them every couple of weeks during the high tourist season. I said that must be incredibly difficult for his heart, and he just gave a small, resigned shrug. We met up with the British zookeeper, Sally, later that afternoon. She had logged much the same bird species as us, but she was intensely, visibly jealous of our rare silky sifaka sighting. Today's lunch was porc avec les légumes sautés (pork and sautéed vegetables). Over our plates, Benoir explained to me the immense importance of ritual surrounding death in Madagascar. He explained that after a family member passes away in certain tribes, the custom dictates they must burn the traditional house down and sacrifice all the family's zebu cattle. I told him I only knew of the famous Famadihana ritual practiced by the Betsileo and the Merina peoples, who observe the custom of 'turning' the dead. The ancestral bones are carefully exhumed from the crypts, rewrapped in brand-new shrouds of expensive silk, and sometimes sprayed with perfume or wine, after which they actively 'participate' in the communal festivities of music, dancing, and food before they are respectfully replaced in their tombs. This turning ceremony, held every two to seven years, is a vital part of maintaining familial links with their revered ancestors, who still play a very real role in daily life. Unfortunately, we weren't able to witness such a ceremony during our trek. Benoir met our family at 4 p.m. for a walk to the bat forest. The Madagascan flying fox (Pteropus rufus) is the largest of the island’s fruit bat species, consuming wild figs, flowers, leaves, and nectar. We came right across a colossal tree that was completely covered in thousands of them. They were a rich, reddish-brown colour. Benoir cautioned us to keep perfectly still so as not to disturb the roost, then he suddenly made a few sudden, exaggerated movements with his arms which sent the entire colony exploding into the air! This action confused us at first, but we managed to capture some spectacular photos of the bats in flight against the sky. 

The Three Tree Frogs 

This morning, we stopped our vehicle about fifteen kilometres outside a rural village to search for unique tree frogs, and we were incredibly lucky enough to locate three of them hiding in the roadside bushes: a bright yellow frog, a brown frog, and a beautiful, large lime-green specimen. Our next stop was down a dusty, sheer mountain track to look for wild pitcher plants. We successfully located a large clump near the side of the road and looked down into their slippery, acidic trumpets to find captured beetles, butterflies, and grasshoppers which were being slowly broken down by the digestive enzymes lying at the bottom of the cone-shaped plants. As our vehicle arrived at the wilderness lodge situated about five kilometres outside of Fort Dauphin, I noticed several beautiful wooden cabins situated right along the edge of a pristine lagoon— ours was literally on its doorstep. There was a fantastic panoramic view of the water from our table in the dining room. I switched on my mobile phone and saw I had a missed call from Gautier. I phoned him back immediately to let him know we had safely arrived at the coast. He arrived at our table ten minutes later and explained sadly that he was unable to personally take Col to the Sainte Luce coastal forest as originally planned, but reassured us that a professional guide friend of his would be taking his place tomorrow. 

Flea the Airport Escort 

Tim put on Col's neoprene wetsuit and spent the afternoon snorkelling happily in the lagoon. He made fast friends with a local stray dog whom we promptly named Flea; he was a remarkably friendly-looking golden Labrador cross. Tim fed him some stale bread rolls from our bags, which the creature wolfed down gratefully. Flea actually slept the entire night curled up right outside our bedroom door, and dutifully followed our family to the breakfast table the next morning, where he was rewarded with some more stale bread and a bit of my egg omelette. During breakfast, I received a text message from my mother back in South Africa to let me know that my brother John had been featured on television last night, talking about his career as a disabled artist based in Clarens. What a pity I missed seeing that broadcast! I phoned John immediately after breakfast (chocolate croissants and hot tea) and we enjoyed a wonderful chat about his television debut. It was raining softly this morning, shrouding the entire lagoon in a beautiful grey mist. Tim went out for another row in the lodge's yellow canoe while I packed our suitcases. Col disappeared into the coastal forest to try and net a few more butterflies and to check his traps. When our taxi arrived, Flea boldly jumped straight into the back seat alongside our luggage, so we had to turn around and drop him back at the front reception desk. He managed to escape and followed our car down the road, and this time we successfully convinced the taxi driver to let the dog ride with us and promise to take him back to the lodge after dropping our family off at the airport terminal. At the check-in counter, we encountered Benoir, who laughed and explained that Flea always accompanies guests with whom he makes friends all the way to the airport gates! Apparently, his original expat owner had abruptly left Madagascar a year previously, and the poor doggie had been left abandoned behind. On seeing my tears welling up at the sad story, Benoir quickly reassured us that the airport and lodge staff keep him exceptionally well-fed and looked after. 

Meeting Moosa in Tuléar 

The Toliara Airport was a small, bustling, and chaotic little place. Upon entering the arrival hall, we noticed at once a local man holding up a cardboard sign featuring our name magnificently misspelt, and we headed straight towards him; he was to be our driver for the southwest leg. We endured a short, tortured conversation conducted entirely in broken Franglais. Tim purchased some cold Cokes off an Indian woman who was selling drinks out of a tin bucket on the pavement. The driver seemed incredibly anxious to get moving down the road immediately, but Col insisted that we were scheduled to meet a man named Moosa first. The driver shook his head stubbornly and claimed he didn’t understand the request. I intervened, using my best authorative French: ‘Mon mari veut rendez-vous avec Moosa maintenant!’ This linguistic effort seemed to successfully jog his memory. He announced that we would meet the man at his property, then proceeded to drive down the highway like someone possessed by demons. Occasionally, he would point out of the window at a passing blur and announce that it was ‘l’oiseau’ (a bird) or another, but we entirely had to take his word for it given our speed. Eventually, we arrived at Moosa’s homestead, which was more of a basic hut really, constructed of rough wood and dried palm leaves. A thick-set, swarthy man stepped out of the dwelling, hand outstretched, and proudly claimed the title of Moosa. Col had arranged to meet with him earlier via email, as he’d heard from other birders that the man was an exceptional local tracker. They planned a wilderness rendezvous for tomorrow morning at precisely 6:30 a.m. The Hotel Nautilus loomed out of the darkness as we pulled up, and we were warmly greeted by a pleasant woman of French origin named Natalie. Our private bungalow room was uniquely cochlea-shaped, resembling a sea snail shell, and lay right on the sandy beach. The round, inner sanctum of the spiral contained the shower and toilet facilities. The shower stall also unfortunately contained some local wildlife in the shape of a giant Madagascan cockroach, which elicited a short, sharp scream from my throat—an auditory reaction that was largely ignored by both Col and Tim. We unpacked our bags and went down the beach in search of some supper. This required a short walk down a sandy path to a huge wooden verandah equipped with dining tables and an outside bar. A lovely sea-themed mural had been painted against one concrete wall. The waiters were not at all conversant with English but, with a lot of enthusiastic miming, pointing, and smiling, we managed to get by and found ourselves tucking into a delicious plate of écrevisses au gratin (grilled crayfish). We were also handed a complimentary rum punch by the house. Thus replete, we waddled back to our snail-shaped room, where Col prepared his camera gear for his safari tomorrow. He mentioned casually that he was travelling to the site via quad bike, and on hearing this, Tim instantly perked up and announced that he would be joining his father. 

The Room Pantomime and the Razor Incident 

A short night’s sleep later, Col and Tim were fumbling around in the pitch dark of the cabin, as the property's electricity generator only comes on at 7 a.m. I got dressed and decided to head down to the deck for breakfast. I noticed that our wooden door lock was incredibly flimsy, so I carefully hid our laptops and some expensive cameras deep under the bedframe. Even if the local staff are thoroughly trustworthy—which I’m sure they are—I reasoned that it’s never fair to tempt anyone by leaving items of high value lying around in plain sight. I then placed a large pile of pillows underneath the bedsheets, shaping them to look exactly like a human body sleeping there. Sod’s Law dictates that this was the exact moment the chambermaid walked past the window frame just as I was about to close the door, forcing me to pantomime an elaborate, loud waving goodbye to a silent lump of pillows! I discovered, unfortunately a few sips too late, that the lait in my morning coffee was of the local zebu variety, and I instantly felt a bit ill. I am sure there is absolutely nothing biologically wrong with zebu milk, but I am notoriously fussy when travelling, as you might have noticed by now. However, my subconscious spoke directly to my stomach and whispered that the milk may not be— shudder—pasteurised, and consequently, I found I had developed a sudden, severe running tummy. I grabbed my digital camera and took the wooden steps leading down to the beach, where I was instantly inundated by crowds of local children wanting to sell me boat rides, lengths of colourful cloth, hair-plaiting services, or just demanding cash without all the unnecessary giving of something in return. I hastily retreated back to the sanctuary of the room to tidy up our things and to search for a spare padlock which we usually keep somewhere in our suitcases. I couldn't find the lock, but when plunging my bare hand blindly into a deep wash bag, my fingers accidentally encountered the exposed, sharp blade of Col’s razor. This sliced off a good portion of my digit, and bright red blood spurted forth; simultaneously, my bowels clenched violently, indicating the imminent, catastrophic release of their contents. I rushed frantically to the loo stall, grabbed the toilet paper, and wrapped half a roll around my bleeding finger, while desperately lowering my drawers with the other hand. It was with absolute horror that I suddenly heard the chambermaid push open the front door and call out to ask if I had any dirty laundry! Her footsteps were actively approaching our inner bathroom sanctum, so I grabbed my trousers with one bloody hand, trying my absolute best not to bleed all over the fabric, and bellowed at the wall, ‘Un moment, por favor!’ Which, besides being half-Portuguese, was spoken far too late. She stepped around the curve, and I was caught entirely with my pants at half-mast, my incomplete finger still dripping blood onto the tiles; the expression of pure surprise on the maid’s face was both comical and deeply embarrassing. ‘Le plaster! I look for le plaster!’ I babbled frantically, casting my eyes around the room for our small box of Disney plasters. She retreated out of the room with a garbled, ‘Désolé!’ I eventually located a box of Elastoplast instead of the Mickey Mouse plasters and dressed my sliced finger as best as I humanly could. Col and Tim returned from the bush half an hour later. Col announced happily that he had successfully spotted the rare sub-desert mesite (Monias benschi) and the long-tailed ground roller (Uratelornis chimaera), and Tim had ridden the quad bike across the dunes, so both boys were thoroughly happy and not the least bit interested in hearing about my loose bowels or my bleeding finger. 

Captive Guests and the Basket Toss 

I had left the window curtains open last night, so the view of the sparkling azure lagoon was absolutely lovely to wake up to on this sunny day. Tim and Col left the beach for a boat dive, Tim dressed in a hired neoprene wetsuit. I went for a long walk along the water’s edge—actually, I intentionally waded in the shallow water, as that physical activity takes significantly more effort, which is exactly what I was aiming for having consumed far too many croissants au beurre at breakfast. I passed several local villagers searching diligently under rocks and in the damp sand for molluscs and small shells. There were children carrying plastic buckets, and fishermen gathering in their daily catch from rickety wooden boats. I watched a lone fisherman stand and cast his net wide from his small wooden dhow, which made for a fantastic photograph. At the restaurant, Monsieur et Madame ‘Le Suave’ greeted us regally and escorted our family to our table for lunch. We ordered zebu steaks fois trois—two prepared with a rich mustard sauce, which was absolutely delicious. An indecently short time later that evening, it was time to reassemble my dry, stiff locks into something vaguely resembling human hair. Tim was sporting a massive bouffant look, and Col possessed a straight, sleek cap of hair from the sea. This interesting trio of gens trooped off to the bar for a pre-dinner drink. We discovered that the staff had pushed all the timber chairs and sun loungers into a tight semi-circle face the deck, in clear preparation for our evening’s mandatory entertainment. Col tried his best to avoid both the cultural show and the accompanying cash payment by sitting far away on one of the dining tables, but he was soon ushered briskly back to the chairs by Madame ‘Nautilus’ herself. So, we were going to watch the entertainment whether we wanted to or not. An eager waiter pranced over to our seats and handed us a ‘free’ rum punch. Our glasses, and those of the other captive international guests, were refilled almost before we had even finished half the contents. This clever commercial ploy worked exceptionally well, as by the time a troupe of half-naked, bum-wiggling, bosom-thrusting dancers came out onto the sand with much cheering, shouts of rather premature ‘encore!’ and general loud encouragement from the already heavily inebriated audience. We had endured only half an hour of shuffling around and singing when a small woven basket came around the rows for a cash collection. We slid a few banknotes of the absolute lowest denomination into the wicker, feeling this adequately paid for the ‘free’ drinks and the miniature garlic clams we had consumed. Col’s smile slipped somewhat when Monsieur ‘Le Suave’— completely overcome with rum punch and the sight of his beloved Madame shuffling around with the dancers—enthusiastically grabbed the Ariary-laden basket, emptied the cash straight into his own pockets, and energetically tossed the empty basket back into the audience with a loud, ‘Voilà!’ He had also flung several ‘mille d'argent’ into its depths and encouraged the guests to place ‘plus d'argent encore’ within. The second round of collection was fraught with our internal miserliness fighting aggressively with social guilt. Guilt won the toss, and banknotes piled up inside the wicker again as men fumbled in their wallets and were forced to extract the folded 20,000 Ariary notes they had desperately tried to hide during the first round. Col took so long with the basket as he fumbled with a large fan of high-denomination notes, searching frantically for a lower amount, that I was forced to physically lean right over his knee to hide his miserly attempt from the dancers. Now thoroughly disgruntled and hungry in equal measure, we sipped on the last of our ‘free’ rum and waited for the entertainment to cease. Tim and Col secured the last two available zebu steaks from the kitchen, so I ordered, ‘Une crayfish avec fromage?’ The plate arrived entirely sans fromage, but tasted absolutely delicious anyway. I watched with immense interest a French couple sitting at the next table, their lips pursed in a permanent moue as if constantly prepared for an elegant speech. None of us slept well that night. Col had developed a heavy head cold, which he loudly claimed he ‘knew’ he would contract after being sneezed on by a passenger on our domestic flight. I had luckily missed being sprayed with those virulent bugs because my head happened to be buried deep inside an Air Madagascar sick bag at that exact moment; I hadn't been at all nauseous, I was simply trying the bag out for size. I had also stomped outside our room wearing nothing but a bath towel around 2 a.m., in a furious search for the source of loud music I could hear vibrating through the walls. The moon was full and beautifully bright over the sand but there wasn't a single sign of any musicians. It later turned out that Tim had fallen asleep with my iPod headphones on, and the device had continued to play loudly next to our headboard until the battery ran completely out. 

Trading Pens for Leopard Conches 

The next morning, I left Tim sitting on the verandah swinging lazily in a hammock and went for another long wade to the far side of the beach. Along the shore, I successfully exchanged a free plastic pen—which I had received from a well-known pharmaceutical antibiotic company—for a beautiful leopard-shell conch featuring a small hole in its spiral. An old village woman had been ferreting in the damp sand at low tide looking for clams when her fingers found the shell. Despite being easily ninety years old, severely myopic, and probably entirely illiterate, she seemed well pleased with the corporate pen. I figured the exchange was perfectly fair, as she can sell the pen to the highest bidder in the village (pens being a highly popular commodity in rural parts), and I secured my conch shell without causing any damage to the living coral reef. A perfect win-win. I waded back to our cabin just in time to see Col alighting from a small wooden dhow that he had certainly not originally sailed off in. It seems he had been transferred sur la mer from a diving boat onto the canoe. Although he reported it had been a spectacular dive, his head was still completely full of cold mucus, which makes deep diving exceptionally difficult, so we enjoyed an early tea and then Tim and I went to inspect some local tombs nearby. I was only hoping it wasn't considered fady by the elders to have us walking all over the sacred burial grounds. Famadihana, or the 'dancing with the dead', occurs when the Malagasy families open the stone crypts of their ancestors every few years. They carefully rewrap the bodies in fresh silk burial cloth, and then members of the family carry the deceased for a ceremonial dance around the tomb with great music and celebration before returning them to the earth. The bodies are sometimes sprayed with expensive perfume or French wine before they are securely wrapped in their new shrouds. This turning of the bones ceremony, held every two to seven years, is a vital part of helping to maintain active spiritual links with their revered ancestors, who still play a very real role in daily life. Unfortunately, we weren't able to witness an active ceremony during our stay. Our lunch was, be still my beating heart, zebu steaks à trois sans moutarde—which was a great pity, as I rather like a bit of mustard on my zebu. I figured we had, between the three of us, successfully consumed an entire zebu cow over the course of this single trip. Col subsequently walked out to the forest to collect more butterflies with his canvas net and to check his traps, which were absolutely redolent with the odour of ‘eau de rotten banane’. 

The Window of Isalo 

The car was packed the next morning, and we headed off in the direction of Isalo National Park. It seems Saturday was the main market day for most of the rural villages that we passed along the highway. They had their agricultural wares on colourful display as our 4x4 rumbled past the stalls. In the main town, Hari helped me choose some travel provisions: I purchased bananas, long French loaves, cream cheese, bottled water, Coke, and chocolate, then walked into some small stalls that smelled strongly of industrial soap and cleaning fluid to buy some cans of soda juice. As we continued driving towards Isalo, the surrounding seaside scrub and dry bush gradually became grassy, before transforming into rugged, jagged mountains. Outside a small village, we pulled over for a picnic lunch. Realising too late that we didn't possess a knife to smear the cream cheese onto the bread, I improvised using small twigs and the metal back of a keyring. The French loaves were incredibly long, so I ended up handing the remainder of my bread over to a passing local villager. I was forced to carry Tim on my back to the car door, as the ground was covered in sharp thorns and he was entirely barefoot. He was nearly as tall as my own height by this stage, and we were both in absolute stitches of helpless laughter as I staggered as quickly as I could to the waiting vehicle under his weight. It was intensely hot and very dry in the interior, so we swigged at our water bottles and Hari turned the vehicle's air conditioning on full blast. We stopped to inspect a colossal baobab tree growing right next to the asphalt, its root-like branches stretching out almost as if welcoming our car to the park. It wasn't long before our windows were completely surrounded by local youngsters aggressively brandishing startled-looking chameleons on the end of large wooden sticks. Col shook his head firmly through the glass, indicating that live chameleons were definitely not on our list of souvenirs to take home to Cape Town. The children stood and pressed their noses flat against the car windows until we finally drove away. There was a neat row of small, round guest huts fitted with thatch roofs, flanking a much larger communal building in the centre, when we finally arrived at our hotel late that afternoon and announced ourselves at the reception desk. Hari brought the car around shortly afterwards, and we set off to view ‘La Fenêtre’ (The Window)—a famous, massive sandstone rock formation that features a natural window-like aperture through which you can photograph the setting sun. Numerous tour buses and 4x4s were parked at the site, with crowds of international passengers pouring out of the doors and walking briskly up the rocky incline towards the ‘window’ before the sun dropped. We climbed to the top of the ridge and waited our turn in the crowd, noting that it was indeed a spectacular, breathtaking view from the stone portal as we stepped up for our turn at photographing the red orb. 

Fady Pointers and the Snow White Witch 

Neither of us enjoyed a great night’s sleep; we collectively blamed the sour, vinegary Clos Malaza wine we had consumed at dinner last night. After packing all one hundred individual pieces of our luggage back into Hari’s 4x4 the next morning, we set off to pick up our park guide and followed him into the deep forest tracks. He immediately cautioned our family not to point our fingers directly at any geographical object or landmark, as this physical gesture is considered a severe, disrespectful fady taboo within the local Bara tribe. I suddenly felt an intense, overwhelming psychological need to point my finger at a absolute number of things the exact second I heard I shouldn't! Although it wasn't an exceptionally difficult hike for my legs, my terrible sense of physical balance made the track challenging at times when the path became particularly rocky. We waded across a small, cool stream and climbed over many boulders until we came to a lovely, hidden waterfall surrounding a clear natural pool around three metres deep. In winter, when the heavy rains fill the gorges, this entire sector is completely impassable to tourists. Tim and I followed the guide to the next piscine pool, then we turned back to the car and waited half an hour for the others to join our position. The guide brought our family a picnic lunch after escorting us to another scenic site nearby, producing fresh cheese and omelette rolls, bananas, juice, and bottled water from the back of the vehicle. Tim and I were subsequently dropped off at our next hotel, which was uniquely set directly into the rock face and was mostly constructed of heavy stone blocks quarried from the surrounding mountain. It looked exactly like a medieval castle. The property featured beautifully manicured grounds with small lily ponds dotted around the lawns. After enjoying a hot shower, we headed straight for the bar to receive some welcome drinks of tart cherry juice (called carcadie). The next morning, we commenced the long, arduous drive to Ranomafana, located some 350 kilometres away. The terrain became increasingly mountainous, lush, and verdant the closer our vehicle approached the national park boundaries. Col popped into a local bank branch along the route with Hari, leaving Tim and me to wait inside the car. The cabin soon felt intensely hot and stuffy, so I asked Tim to please open his door to let a breeze in. He pushed the door open, and in instantly fell a cackling, wizened old local woman who looked exactly like the evil witch straight out of Snow White! Tim and I cowered back into the far corner of the seats as she spouted forth a torrent of rapid words, waving her thin, skeletal hands with terrifyingly long fingernails directly at our faces. I wondered frantically what to do to defuse the situation, then decided to extract a 1,000 Ariary note (which is practically worthless in real value) from my purse and thrust it at her hand. That financial strategy seemed to do the trick perfectly; off she went down the pavement, clutching the note tightly to her skinny chest and cackling wildly to herself. 

The Hairy Fossa and the Ochre Huts 

The outskirts of Ranomafana, which had been originally created as a protected national park back in 1991, finally came into view through the trees. The lodge where we were scheduled to stay for a few nights was perched spectacularly against the steep mountainside, offering a panoramic view of the surrounding rainforest canopy. Hari joined our family at the bar after dinner, then walked over and started playing the electric piano, which was a huge, wonderful surprise to us all. An impromptu lodge band immediately followed her lead, with one of the porters rhythmically banging on a wooden salad bowl, and the front receptionist made an appearance to sing center stage. We figured she must double as evening entertainment to supplement her monthly income. Not long after setting off into the dense rainforest path with our guide the next morning, we came right across a rare—and recently discovered—golden bamboo lemur (Hapalemur aureus). This was an incredibly exciting highlight for Col's journal, and we stood watching the primate for some minutes before continuing down the muddy track. Later that evening, Tim joined us for our official night hike, and the five of us set off into the pitch-dark forest. Our tracker, Emile, showed us a colossal leaf-tailed gecko resting on a trunk, its head pointing directly downwards towards the roots. High Humidity Torrential Rainfall Environment Gecko Rests Facing Head-Downwards Gravity Naturally Drains Rainwater Away from Nostrils Airways Remain Clear Prevents Drowning Emile explained that because these reptiles spend their entire lives in incredibly humid, high-rainfall environments, water can easily pool inside their nostrils if they are facing upwards during a torrential downpour; therefore, resting head-downwards allows gravity to naturally drain any rainwater away from their respiratory openings, keeping their airways perfectly clear. Further up the path, our lights caught brown lemurs with glowing red eyes, scampering high in the upper branches. Then, at an open wooden lookout point, we encountered a small group of international tourists staring intently at three fossas (Cryptoprocta ferox) moving through the brush. The fossa is the absolute largest native predator in Madagascar. Despite possessing a remarkably dog-like face and a long, cat-like muscular body, it is in fact a specialized member of the mongoose family (Herpestidae). They efficiently hunt small rodents, forest fish, lemurs, and even birds. As we watched them from the rail, they silently slinks back into the deep forest and disappeared from view. It was thrilling to see them hunting in the wild. Next, our beams caught some tiny mouse lemurs sleeping curled up in the crook of a large tree trunk. Emile pointed out some more chameleons in the leaves. It was a thoroughly fascinating walk, and we were almost reluctant to return to the lodge for our supper. Tonight in the dining room, the front receptionist broke into song once more; she was wearing a formal grey business suit for the occasion and had put on a very bright red lipstick. At two o'clock the next afternoon, we drove with Hari to a section of the river that was surrounded by deep limestone caves. Equipped with our head torches, we crawled inside to search for the fruit bats that reside there. We also tried our best to locate some local cave frogs, but didn't spot any specimens; Hari reckoned the amphibians were likely buried deep underneath a huge pile of rock rubble left behind by the municipal Roadworks Department. Our next major stop along the route was the town of Antsirabe. I carefully noticed that the local village huts here were a little different from the usual dark brown mud-and-wattle dwellings we had encountered elsewhere, featuring thick grass roofs of a more distinctive, bright ochre colour; some of the homes were even double-storey structures. There were many terraced rice paddies and neat vegetable gardens lining the valley. Local people had set up simple bamboo lean-tos along the asphalt, on which their colourful agricultural wares were displayed: bright orange carrots fought for visual supremacy with vivid green lettuce, crimson-red tomatoes, and bright yellow lemons and oranges, making colourful pyramids of fruit and vegetables along the road. Hari drove on via muddy, waterlogged roads until lunchtime, pulling over at a restaurant close to Antsirabe where we passed a small group of local women manually pounding dried maize inside a large wooden bowl. This flour will eventually be used to bake bread and create a kind of thick gruel or morning porridge. Later that afternoon, we checked into a historic hotel that structurally resembled an old railway station, featuring brick walls painted a dark green, paired with wide wooden stairs. The dark interior and opulent, heavy furnishings were incredibly reminiscent of the 1940s colonial era. 

The Zebu Horn Factory and the Naked Dream 

I spent most of the following day's journey staring out of the car window, watching daily Malagasy life play itself out in standard activities: cutting wood, buying, selling, carrying heavy water pots balanced on the top of heads, stray dogs ambling around looking for food, and young children running around playing in the dusty streets with old tyres and tin cans. We stopped to visit a rural zebu horn factory, where local artisans create beautiful models of birds fashioned out of the bone from zebu horns. We were led to a wooden bench to watch the process, which was absolutely fascinating. First, the raw horn is heated intensely over an open fire, which softens the bone structure and easily dislodges the core inner piece from the outer horn by hitting it gently with a small wooden mallet. The inner bone piece is heated over the flames again to make it completely malleable, and then it is skillfully fashioned into a bird shape using hand tools. A hot iron poker provides the dark eye sockets, and a hot chisel expertly defines the individual feathers. The entire piece is subsequently smoothed over using a combination of fine sandpaper, hot wood ash, and water. The end result is really lovely; some of the finished birds are dyed with bright paint. A slight cough from the craftsman and the proud words, ‘Voilà!’ alerted us to the fact that some of the factory staff had set up a wooden table displaying the finished products for sale. Naturally, we had to purchase a piece for our collection, and they generously gave us a rough, unfinished bird horn to keep as well. Col had arranged to meet up with a renowned local collector named Monsieur Andre Perieras to purchase some exotic butterflies that he’d not managed to personally catch himself in the forest. He was absolutely in his element, spending an age going systematically through all the wooden drawers of beautifully set Papilionidae specimens before choosing a few rare items for a quarter of the standard international going price. Hari subsequently drove the 4x4 down a narrow, cobbled road to the Hotel La Varangue, where we checked in for our final night. My sleep was again punctuated by stressful anxiety dreams about being back at work—naked, in charge of the hospital ward, but knowing absolutely nothing about the patients—which woke me up with a sudden start. I absolutely hate those naked work dreams; the memory stays with you for a few hours into the morning, leaving you feeling vaguely embarrassed on the pavement. After enjoying our very last petit déjeuner in Madagascar, I took a final wander down to the local marketplace, immediately inundated by ankle-biters wanting ‘les cadeaux’ (presents) and vendors aggressively selling anything from postcards to vanilla pods. Hari dropped our family off at the airport terminal gates. We thanked him profusely for all his excellent driving, mechanical assistance, and wilderness guiding. We were deemed overweight on our luggage scales again (in more ways than one, after all those high-carb holiday meals!), so Col was forced to pay extra cash for his primary camera bag. Our Air Madagascar plane stood waiting on the tarmac, and we turned and waved a fond farewell to the island. For sheer diversity in unique animal life, it is truly hard to beat.
