# Chapter 19: Madagascar 2006: The Camera Bag Pillow

As our first trip was so interesting, we decided to visit Madagascar again. We had a lot of luggage, but what else is new. Whereas I liked to think I packed for what is required for the holiday, Col went without unimportant items like, let’s say, clothing, for extra lenses for his cameras and twenty more packets of film. He shoved Tim's hand luggage into a large carry-all that was already bursting with camera equipment and told Tim he was to casually sling this over his shoulder as though it contained no more than a travel pillow.
Col opened the garage door, which set the alarm off and delayed us further. We need not have rushed as the plane was delayed. Boarding time was suddenly announced just at the moment Tim had bought us a drink each. We had to empty the contents as quickly as we could down our throats, causing a lot of excess gas and burping from the three of us. For some reason, we were all seated in separate seats. I had a very large lady sitting next to me in the window seat. Her ample thighs encroached into my seat, forcing me into a coy position with shoulders narrowed and legs pressed primly together. It was not a comfortable trip, but luckily it was a relatively short one, and soon we were landing in Johannesburg. Tim manfully held onto his oversized bag while we grabbed our many pieces of 'hand luggage', and disembarked with a few hours to spare. We sauntered slowly over to gate 49, half an hour before boarding, only to discover that our plane was now at gate 2 – about five kilometres away. We ran as fast as three people carrying double their body weight in hand luggage can, (i.e. not very fast) and made it with seconds to spare. It seemed they had parked at the wrong gate. I hoped they got a ticket.
At the gate, we were greeted by Freda Flintstone and Happy Harry. I could cheerfully strangle both of them. There was no apology for having us rush to the gate from the next village. They just stood there while Happy chomped his way through a large bag of Niknaks, sprinkling a fine dusting of orange onto his tie while engaging in a long, involved conversation with Freda that was peppered with clicks and sighs. They eventually took our proffered tickets (Happy left a large orange thumbprint on mine), and we hoisted ourselves onto a bus and tiredly swayed our way towards the Boeing that stood waiting for its passengers. The seats were so small that movement was restricted to shallow breathing. Col's knees were at chest level and he did not look happy.
It was muggy and claustrophobic on board and I spent the first ten minutes in the air fiddling with the toggle thingy trying to get a jet of air onto my melting visage. I succeeded in only ejecting a weak blast of humidity for my pains. I sat back irritably after trying unsuccessfully to move my seat back, waiting for the seatbelt sign to go off so I could toddle over to the loo. I spotted three vacant seats at the back and on my return and told Col I was moving there. He joined me and we left Tim sprawled across the three newly vacated seats.
Arrival in Tana and Hotel Argeme
Due to the late take-off, we only arrived at Tana after 11 am with a very bumpy landing. There was a horrifically large queue in the airport as a huge Air France Boeing had just landed. We eventually made it to the other side of passport control and collected our mountain of luggage and found a rather wilted Hari, waiting for us with a handheld sign declaring the words, 'Mestorts fambly'. We met her on our last trip to Madagascar and had enlisted her services again. Col immediately left me to chat to Hari while he went off to exchange Rand notes into a wheelbarrow of Ariary. I was tired and irritable and my brain cells rummaged into their depths from 40-odd years ago to come up with a string of French words that would pass for intelligible, and fail by a verb or two. Hari looked puzzled and said no, she didn't want any wine. Strong, purposeful hands suddenly lunged for the pile of luggage at my feet. I yelled a few, 'Non, Non! Merci' to prevent the removal of our baggage by two eager porters to the awaiting voiture. They had been hovering over us since our pale (read 'wealthy') faces first entered the waiting hall.
Col appeared looking strangely misshapen of torso. His birder's jacket, that of the many pockets, had bulges everywhere. He muttered that several million Ariary notes had been acquired and were now safely in his pockets.
We followed Hari into the carpark where the company 4X4 awaited us. It was a newish model and boded well for the future travel plans.
Hotel Argeme appeared after a circuitous route through Tana. The narrow streets and haphazard houses were reminiscent of our trip to Nepal a few years before. The hotel was nestled down a narrow cobbled street and tucked away in the corner - a remnant of its colonial past. There were many interesting antiques, bric-a-brac and items of interest. A large wooden rickshaw model stood on the counter where we checked in. A beautiful silver and bronze peacock stood outside the door to our chambre, which was very clean and airy inside. A bar fridge huddled under a small counter and a huge mosquito-netted bed complete with the ubiquitous French bedroll which Col instantly flung onto the floor. What is it with cushions and men I wonder. Even at home, there’s a continuous removal and replacement of cushions in the lounge. In the lounge we encountered a group of très raucous Frenchmen in very high spirits (mentally and physically it would seem). Apparently, France had just won some sporting event. We repaired to our chambre for a shower. I was horrified to discover it was 12:45 local time as we were to be up by 6:30 for petit déjeuner. I was really tired but tossed and turned like a basting turkey till the early hours.

Exploring Andohahela Parc
I couldn't believe the cheek of our alarm clock when its shrill tones startled me out of my dreams and alerted me to the fact that 6:30 was almost upon us. A splash of cold water about the visage helped. I had to restrain Col from practising his French for, while his recall is passable, his pronunciation is not. Petit déjeuner consisted of yoghurt, bananas, rolls and croissants washed down with some strong coffee. Hari suddenly appeared with Dina in tow. She wished us a smiling, Bonjour. We had met Dina before, a small, dynamic woman who ran the Setam show with great efficiency. She presented us with the itinerary and entry tickets for the duration of our trip. She also gave us two data cards for l’Orange, the local network provider. We left with Hari for the airport. Tim and I sat in the back of the Land Cruiser watching the scenery move swiftly by. Many buildings in various states of repair, rice paddies, wooden stalls stacked high with oranges and other seasonal fruit. Women in colourful cloth and pork pie hats wandered along the paths, clutching either baskets of rice or children. Some of the baskets were laden with washing or wood for fires. The colourful wooden pousse-pousse's ambled by in huge numbers.  At passport control, there was some delay because our hand luggage was overweight. Col kept repeating, 'Camera!' while I said, 'Computer!' They gave up and waved us through. At around ten to eleven, we boarded the small plane to Fort Dauphine, on the South East coast of Madagascar. They had just enough time to fling a poisson-flavoured roll and a bottle of water at us before we touched down in Fort Dauphin. 
We were met with thick humidity and struggled with our heavy hand luggage to the waiting bus. A few other couples joined the bus and we set off through the seaside town of Jolagnaro to our hotel. The sea looked rough and waves were high. The roads were not at all good and the ride was very bumpy but the hotel was set in pleasant surroundings. A soft rain mixed with the heat to create a steamy jungle vibe. A tall, thin man wearing a grey cap joined us and introduced himself as 'Gooti' and declared his services as our local guide. He apologised for interrupting our lunch but said due to the recent heavy rainfall, the roads were now impassable to the forest camp, he suggested we visit the transition forest which included a spiny forest. Col was a little disappointed as he had some exotic butterflies in mind to add to his ever expanding collection, but we agreed it was better to change the plans, especially as climbing with heavy suitcases to the other camp would have been extremely unpleasant with the muddy paths.
Col and I went for a short walk down to the harbour after lunch. Small alcoved lean-tos housed locals huddled around coal braziers surrounding them in steam. A huge oil tanker bobbed gently in the grey sea while skeletons of ill fated vessels lay on the beach, bearing testament to the rough seas and inclement weather this side of Madagascar. We walked up to a small stone church where the sounds of a choir emanated from its holy depths and added to the general atmosphere. Monday, 3rd July: Camping in the Forest
Gooti, a driver, and a cook climbed into our hired vehicle so the seating was a bit squashed. I had my laptop on my knee, Tim lolling over me and Gooti next to him. The cook was buried under a mound of luggage in the back. The drive was interesting and we watched the change from lush rainforest to dry transition forest. Gooti spoke very good English; said he was self-taught and we had some interesting conversations. After several stops along the way at local villages for extra water, more supplies, one stop revealed the presence of pitcher plants (Nepenthes madagascariensis). Col and I jumped out to photograph these interesting plants. The road had deteriorated to mostly potholes with pure mud and dirt. The bumpy road did nothing to help Tim who sat grim-faced throughout the journey while his guts churned. We passed the area where the three-cornered palm is found (Neodypsis decary). It is the only place in the world where it exists. We arrived at last at the entrance to 'Parc Nationale Andohahela' where the 'office' was no more than a grass-roofed lean-to where two officials sat on plastic chairs next to a small wooden desk. 
Tim and I lay on a mattress on the riverbank while they set up camp. Col had disappeared into the nearby forest as was his wont. It was lovely and cool on the river. We were feeling quite peckish and I was wondering what would be offered for lunch. I hadn't seen much more than various fruits in the back of the Land Cruiser. I sniffed suspiciously as a plume of smoke trailed towards us. We had brought some long-legged chickens but they looked very scrawny to me. I heard from Gooti that they would go to buy some wild hog meat from someone halfway up the mountain. Half an hour later we were called for lunch to find a makeshift wooden table and some rickety benches had been set up. The meal was surprisingly delicious, given the circumstances. A starter of potato, cucumber and boiled egg followed by a superb prawn and ginger dish with rice and some rolls. Gooti brought us a piece of wild hog to taste. I deferred and Tim didn't eat much as his tum was still delicate. After lunch I made some tea in a small bowl then Tim and I returned to our mattress next to the river and spent an hour relaxing with a cool breeze. I was busy transferring digital photos from camera to laptop when a shadow fell over me. I looked up startled to find a small minx smiling down at me. He was wearing tatty shorts and a torn T-shirt, but his smile was wide and wholesome. He said his name was Constantine and he was 11 years old. He wasn't any taller than my elbow. His English was minimal and halting so I tried out some French. The result was an interesting conversation in Franglaise. He told me he lived nearby and that his father worked for ANGAP. He said he usually went to school by bus. He eventually tired of our conversation and slipped away. Later Col, Tim and I went for a long, circuitous walk around the river and into the transition forest. There’s a bit of rock hopping involved. We found baobabs and succulents en route. A beautiful waterfall and a few cool streams. After two hours we headed back to camp. The table had been moved closer to the car and candles stuck in sand-filled Coke bottles that had been cut in half which brought sufficient light to see what we were eating.

Spiny Forest and Lemurs

Around 3 a.m. I needed a wee so I crept outside barefoot and squatted the prerequisite distance from the tent. Bladder emptied, I walked gingerly back to the tent over the gravelly sand, bunching my toes as I walked to avoid sharp stones. As I got to the tent flap, my toes closed around a pile of sandy gravel. As I swung my leg into the opening, my toes unfurled and released their contents (roughly half a cup) of gravel over Col's face and hair. To say he was displeased is to make light of the moment, and he was startled into wakefulness with frantic scrabbling as he attempted to empty his mouth and hair of coarse grit. My attempts at apology were somewhat hampered by laughter.
It had become a lot cooler in the dawn and there was even some dew around the edges of the tent. The cups were so small as to be useless in my opinion so I resorted to drinking my tea out of a tin bowl. Following this repast, we walked through the spiny forest to the next camp. The car was to meet us there after packing away everything. It was a long, hot walk but not too strenuous. I enjoyed looking for giraffe beetles, chameleons and different foliage types such as an octopus tree (Didierea madagascarensis), fan palms and many others unique to the country. We arrived at the entrance to the spiny forest reserve after 8 km. There wasn't much here besides a small thatch-roofed office, a zebu pen and a demarcated car park. Water wasn’t available either but we had brought some from the other camp. After resting a while on some plastic chairs Col disappeared into the forest. Later they set up a large wooden table for lunch. Rice and vegetables with a spicy chicken and potato stew. This was followed by baked banana in a chocolate sauce. Amazing what they can do with a couple of charcoal burners. Several mangy hounds waited at our knees for a dropped morsel or two. The cook made us a huge plate of pancakes that evening. So much food! We went on a night walk after supper to walk off some calories. We headed into the darkness, head torches ablaze, and came across a white-fronted mouse lemur nestled in a spiny tree. Then we saw a red or rufous mouse lemur. I discovered the trick was to shine the torch into trees and pick up a glint of an eye, then focus, and usually another lemur was revealed. I spotted five lemurs this way. There were a few sleeping sifakas high up in a tree – too high to see clearly. Back in camp, we said we weren't hungry, just thirsty, but when supper arrived it was so delicious we devoured it all anyway. Zebu kebabs, tender and juicy with tomato, onion and aubergine with rice. We used a plastic bucket and cup to wash our hands and I sent out a hearty 'bonsoir' into the night and retired to our tent.

Berenty Bound

After entering the spiny forest we came to the camp we were supposed to have stayed in. A small group of sifakas perched high in a tree. Gooti pointed out a white-fronted mouse lemur curled up in its hole quite close to the path so we were able to take some good photos. The campsite was much nicer than the one we were in but totally inaccessible by car. In the continuing saga that involves me and my ablutions: I needed the loo and the guide pointed in the general direction. I couldn't find it so I squatted behind some crumbling fireplaces. I was just dusting myself off, so to speak, when the guide appeared and said,
‘Les toilettes sont ici!’
He pointed behind me, and half hidden by some Thilachium latifolium bushes was a proper flush toilet in a cement enclosure. I blushed and muttered that I didn't see it and awkwardly side-shuffled to the car.
Benoir, our new guide for the next three days, met us and introduced himself. He spoke good English with a strong American accent which I tried not to feel irritated by — how it is that most non-English-speaking people the world over choose to learn American English with its accompanying twang, which bears little resemblance to the original, is beyond me — Tim said I needed to ‘chill’ and he was probably right.
The bus rumbled over the uneven dirt roads and we gazed through the dusty windows at the passing sisal fields before arriving at Berenty Reserve, eager to see what the next few days would bring. The area appeared empty of visitors besides ourselves. Our cabin had two bedrooms and a sizeable shower. We had drinks at the restaurant and looked out over the red dust and sparse thorn trees before looking for lemurs and soon found the lovely ring-tailed lemurs in a tree.

Berenty Wildlife

Col and I joined Benoir for a walk around Berenty estate. It was a huge place. We saw our first sunning ringtails sitting in a row in yoga poses, arms outstretched, bellies exposed to the sun. Then we found a troop of sifakas with their dark brown faces and white furry bodies sitting in a large Didierea tree.
Col lent Tim his digital camera so he was snapping away in delight at some sifakas nearby. Around six, just as it was getting dark, we went for some hot chocolate then joined Benoir in the combi for a night walk. We had some good sightings of mouse lemurs, brown lemurs, lepilemur, white-footed lemur and a fat-tailed dwarf lemur that night. All very exciting and we chattered animatedly en route to the dining room where crab crêpes, chicken and banana awaited us. There was some traditional dancing that night around a huge bonfire. About eight dancers, dressed in red, with a few musicians playing an accordion, gave us a lively performance. At one stage, probably after my second coconut rum, I got up and joined them for a 'Malagasy Mamba' which was met with great hilarity.

Rare Sightings

I awoke to an intensely bright light shining down on my face. I'd forgotten to switch off the lights last night and the generators usually go off at 12. Breakfast was nothing startling as it consisted of cold sweet potato, hard French loaf cut into pieces and jams of indiscriminate origin. For some reason, stale Madeira cake was also part of the unappealing carb-laden fare. Luckily my tea didn't fail me. My milk-making process was watched in amusement by a British girl who passed by and announced that they did have milk here. I told her that since Tim was very ill in India from drinking unpasteurised milk, I was not taking any chances. She said that she worked in a zoo in Bristol, UK, working mainly with lemurs and had just returned from doing some research in the northern part of Madagascar.
Tim, Col and I joined Benoir for a forest walk. It was lovely and cool and we saw the stirrings of a few ring-tailed lemurs and sifakas. Then we came across a rare silky sifaka sitting high in the canopy. The silky sifaka is a large lemur characterised, as its name implies, by long, silky, white fur. It has a very restricted range and is known locally as the simpona or ‘ghost of the forest’. It is one of the rarest mammals on Earth apparently and a privileged sighting. Benoir said there were only four of them in this forest. We were ecstatic at seeing this beautiful creature. We then followed a group of red-tailed lemurs across the red earth road where they began their yoga ritual. They scampered around after sunning themselves and Tim, who was still clutching Col's digital camera, took some lovely photos of them jumping from tree to tree. I chatted to Benoir about his family. He had four children but only saw them every couple of weeks in high season. I said that must be difficult for him and he gave a small shrug.
We met up with the British girl, Sally. She had seen much the same as us but was very jealous of our silky sifaka sighting. Today's lunch was 'porc avec les légumes sauté’ (pork and sautéed vegetables). Benoir told me of the importance of ritual in death on Madagascar. After someone dies, they burn the house and kill all the zebu. I only knew of Famadihana; the Betsileo and the Merina peoples who have the custom of 'turning' the dead. The bones are rewrapped in new shrouds of silk, sometimes sprayed with either perfume or wine, after which they 'participate' in the festivities of music, dancing and food before they are replaced in their tombs.
Benoir met us at 4 p.m. for a walk to the bat forest. The Madagascan flying fox bats are the largest of the island’s species and they eat figs, flowers, leaves and other fruit. We came across a huge tree that was covered in them. They were a reddish-brown colour. Benoir cautioned us to keep still so as not to disturb them then he made a few sudden exaggerated movements which sent them flying, which confused us at first but we got some nice photos of them in flight.
Fort Dauphin

This morning we stopped about 15 km outside a village to look for some tree frogs and were lucky enough to find three of them in the bushes: a yellow frog, a brown frog and a big lime-green one. The next stop was down a dusty, sheer road to look for pitcher plants. We found a clump near the side of the road and looked down their slippery trumpets to find captured beetles, butterflies and grasshoppers which were being slowly digested by enzymes at the bottom of the cone-shaped plants.
As we arrived at the lodge around 5 km outside of Fort Dauphin, I noticed several wooden cabins situated along the edge of a lagoon — ours was literally on its doorstep. There was a good view of the lagoon from our table in the restaurant. I switched on my mobile phone and saw I had missed a call from Gautier. I phoned him back and told him we had arrived. He arrived ten minutes later and said he was unable to take Col to the Ste Marie coastal forest as planned but a friend would be taking his place.

Table Tennis and Snorkelling

Tim put on Col's wetsuit and had a snorkel in the lagoon. He made friends with a local dog whom we called Flea. He was a friendly-looking golden lab. Tim fed him some stale bread which he wolfed down.
Flea slept outside our room last night and followed us to breakfast where he was rewarded with some stale bread and omelette. I got a message from Mum to say that John was on TV last night, talking about being a disabled artist in Clarens. Pity I missed that.

I phoned John after breakfast (chocolate croissants and tea) and we chatted about his television debut. It was raining softly that morning, shrouding everything in a grey mist. Tim went for another row in the yellow canoe while I packed. Col disappeared into the forest to try to get some more butterflies in his net and to check the traps. Flea jumped in the taxi so we turned round and dropped him back at the hotel. He followed us again and this time we convinced the taxi driver to take him back after dropping us off at the airport. We met Benoit who explained that Flea always accompanies guests that he makes friends with to the airport. Apparently his owner left Madagascar a year ago and the poor doggie was left behind. On seeing my tears welling up he reassured us that he was well fed and looked after.
Toliara Airport was a small, bustling little place. We noticed at once a man holding up a sign with our name (mis)spelt and headed towards him. He was to be our driver. We had a short tortured conversation in ‘Franglais’. Tim bought some Cokes off an Indian woman selling drinks from a bucket. The driver seemed anxious to get going but Col said we were to meet a man named Moosa.

The driver shook his head and said he didn’t understand. I told him,

‘Mon mari veut rendez-vous avec Moosa maintenant!’ (My husband wants to meet with Moosa now).

Which seemed to jog his memory. He said we would meet him there then drove like someone possessed. Occasionally he pointed to a passing blur and said it was some l’oiseau (bird) or other but we had to take his word for it. Eventually we arrived at Moosa’s house which was more of a hut really, made of wood and dried leaves. A thick-set, swarthy man arrived, hand outstretched, and claimed the title of Moosa. Col had arranged to meet with him earlier as he’d heard he was a good birder. They planned a rendezvous tomorrow at 6:30 a.m.

Hotel Nautilus loomed in the dark and we were greeted by a pleasant woman of French origin called Natalie. Our room was cochlea-shaped, and lay right on the beach. The round inner sanctum contained the shower and toilet.
The shower also contained some wildlife in the shape of a giant cockroach which elicited a short scream from me (largely ignored by Col and Tim). We unpacked and went in search of supper. This was a short walk away, down a sandy path to a huge verandah with tables and an outside bar. A lovely sea-themed mural had been painted against one wall. The waiters were not totally conversant with English but with a lot of miming, pointing and smiling, we got by and found ourselves tucking into an ‘écrevisse au grillé’. We were also given a complimentary rum punch. Thus replete, we waddled back to our room where Col prepared for his sortie tomorrow. He mentioned that he was going by quad bike. On hearing this Tim perked up and said he would join him.

A short night later, Col and Tim were fumbling around in the dark as the generator only came on at 7 a.m. I got dressed and decided to go down for breakfast. I noticed that the door lock was very flimsy, so I hid the laptops and some cameras under the bed. Even if the staff are trustworthy, which I’m sure they are, I reasoned that it’s never fair to tempt anyone by leaving items of value lying around. I then placed a pile of pillows under the sheets to look like someone was sleeping there. Sod dictated that this was a great moment for the chambermaid to walk by as I was about to close the door, so I pantomimed an elaborate waving goodbye to a lump of pillows.

I discovered, too late, that the ‘lait’ in my coffee was of the zebu variety and felt a bit ill. I’m sure there’s nothing wrong with zebu milk but I’m fussy (you might have noticed). However my subconscious spoke to my stomach and whispered that the milk may not be — shudder — pasteurised and consequently I found I had a runny tummy. I grabbed my camera and took the steps leading to the beach where I was instantly inundated by kids wanting to sell me boat rides, lengths of colourful cloth, hair-plaiting or just cash without all the unnecessary giving of something in return. I hastily retreated to the room to tidy up and to search for a spare lock which we had somewhere in our luggage. Couldn't find it but when plunging my hand into a wash bag, my fingers encountered the sharp blade of Col’s razor. This sliced off a good portion of digit and blood spurted forth; simultaneously my bowels clenched, indicating the imminent release of their contents. I rushed to the loo, grabbed some toilet paper and wrapped half a roll around my finger, while lowering my drawers with the other hand. It was with absolute horror that I heard the chambermaid enter the door and ask if I had any dirty laundry. Her voice was approaching the inner sanctum, so I grabbed my trousers with one hand, trying not to bleed all over them, and bellowed,

‘Un moment, por favor!’

Which besides being half Portuguese, was spoken too late. So I was caught with my pants half mast, my incomplete finger still dripping blood, and the surprise on the maid’s face was both comical and embarrassing.

‘Le plaster! I look for le plaster!’

I babbled, casting round for the small box of Disney plasters. She retreated with a garbled, ‘Désolé!’

I found a box of Elastoplast instead of the Mickey Mouse plasters and dressed my finger as best as I could. Col and Tim returned half an hour later. Col said he saw the rare ground roller and Tim rode the quad bike so both were happy and not the least bit interested in hearing about my bowels or bleeding finger.

I’d left the curtains open last night so the view of the sparkling azure lagoon was lovely to wake up to that sunny day. Tim and Col left for a boat dive; Tim in a hired wetsuit. I walked along the water’s edge — actually I waded in the water as that takes more effort, which is what I was aiming for having consumed too many croissants au beurre. I passed local people searching under rocks and in the damp sand for molluscs and small shells. There were children with buckets, and fishermen gathering in their catch from rickety wooden boats. I watched a lone fisherman stand and cast his net wide from his small dhow which made for a good photo.
Monsieur et Madame ‘Le Suave’ greeted us regally and showed us to our table for lunch. We ordered zebu steaks fois trois. Two with mustard, which was delicious.
After an indecently short time later, it was time to reassemble my dry stiff locks into something resembling hair. Tim was sporting a bouffant and Col a straight, sleek cap of hair. This interesting trio of gens trooped off for a pre-dinner drink. We discovered the chairs and sun loungers had been pushed into a sort of semi-circle in preparation for our evening’s entertainment. Col tried to avoid both the entertainment and accompanying payment by sitting on one of the tables, but was soon ushered briskly to the chairs by Madame ‘Nautilus’.
So we were going to watch the entertainment whether we wanted to or not. An eager waiter pranced over to us and provided a ‘free’ rum punch. Our glasses and those of other captive guests were filled almost before we’d finished half the contents. This ploy worked well as, by the time a troupe of half-naked, bum-wiggling, bosom-thrusting dancers came on, there was much cheering, shouts of rather premature ‘encore!’ and general encouragement from the already inebriated audience. We had endured only half an hour of shuffling around and singing when a small woven basket came around for collection. We slid a few notes of the lowest denomination into the basket, feeling this adequately paid for the ‘free’ drinks and miniature garlic clams that had been consumed. Col’s smile slipped somewhat when Monsieur ‘Le Suave’, overcome with rum punch and the sight of his beloved Madame shuffling around with the dancers, enthusiastically grabbed the Ariary-laden basket, emptied it into his pockets and energetically tossed the empty basket back into the audience with a loud, ‘Voilà!’ He’d also flung several ‘mille d’argent’ into its depths and encouraged us to place ‘plus d’argent encore’ (more money) within.
The second round was fraught with miserliness fighting with guilt. Guilt won the toss and notes piled up again as men fumbled in their wallets and were forced to extract the folded 20,000 Ariary note they’d tried to hide the first time round. Col took so long with the basket as he fumbled with a fan of high-denomination notes, while searching for a lower amount, that I was forced to lean over his knee to hide the miserly attempt. Now thoroughly disgruntled and hungry in equal measure, we sipped on the last of our ‘free’ rum and waited for the entertainment to cease.
Tim and Col had the last available zebu steaks so I ordered,

‘Une crayfish avec fromage?’

It arrived sans fromage but tasted delicious anyway. I was watching with interest a French couple at the next table with lips pursed in a permanent ‘moue’ as if constantly prepared for speech.

None of us slept well. Col had a cold, that he ‘knew’ he’d get after being sneezed on by a passenger on the plane. I’d just missed being sprayed with virulent bugs as my head was buried in an Air Madagascar sick bag at that moment. I hadn’t been nauseous; I was just trying it out. I’d also stomped outside wearing nothing but a hand towel around two a.m. in search of the source of music I could hear. The moon was full and bright but no sign of music. It later turned out that Tim fell asleep with my iPod on and so it continued to play till the battery ran out.

I left Tim on the verandah swinging in a hammock and went for another wade to the other side of the beach where I exchanged a free pen from a well-known antibiotic company for a leopard-shell conch with a small hole in it. The old woman had been ferreting in the damp sand at low tide, looking for clams, when she found it. Despite being ninety years old, myopic and probably illiterate she seemed well pleased with the pen. I figured the exchange was fair as she could sell it to the highest bidder (pens are a popular item) and I got my shell without damaging any reef. Win-win. I waded back in time to see Col alighting from a small wooden dhow that he’d certainly not sailed off in. It seemed he was transferred ‘sur la mer’ from the other boat. Although it was a great dive, his head was still full of cold which made diving a little difficult so we had an early tea then Tim and I went to look at some tombs nearby.
I was hoping it wasn’t considered ‘fady’ (taboo) to have us walking all over the sacred burial ground. Famadihana or ‘dancing with the dead’ occurs when the Malagasy people open the tombs of their dead every few years. They rewrap the bodies in fresh burial cloth then members of the family take their ancestors for a ‘dance’ near the tomb with great ceremony before returning them to the tomb. The bodies are sometimes sprayed with perfume or even wine before they’re wrapped in their new shroud. This turning of the bones ceremony, held every two to seven years, is a vital part of helping to maintain links with their revered ancestors, who still play a very real role in daily life. We weren’t able to be part of any such ceremony unfortunately.

Lunch was (be still my beating heart) … zebu steaks ‘à trois sans moutarde’ which was a pity as I rather like mustard on my zebu. I figured we had again consumed an entire zebu over the course of this trip.
Col walked to the forest to collect butterflies with his net and to check the traps, redolent with ‘eau de rotten banane’.
The car was packed and we headed off in the direction of Isalo National Park. It seemed Saturday was market day for most of the villages that we passed. They had their wares on colourful display as we passed by. In town Hari helped me choose some provisions — I bought bananas, long French loaves, cream cheese, water, Coke and chocolate then walked into some stalls that smelled strongly of soap and cleaning fluid to buy some cans of juice (soda).
As we continued towards Isalo, the surrounding seaside scrub and dry bush became grassy then mountainous. Outside a small village, we stopped for a picnic lunch. Realising we didn’t have a knife to smear on the cream cheese, I improvised with small twigs and the back of a keyring. The French loaves were quite long so I ended up giving the rest of mine to a passer-by.
I carried Tim on my back to the car as the ground was very thorny and he was barefoot. He was nearly as tall as me now, and we were in stitches of laughter as I staggered as quickly as I could to the waiting vehicle. It was hot and very dry so we swigged at our water and Hari turned on the air con. We stopped to look at a huge baobab next to the road, its root-like branches almost welcoming us. It wasn’t long before we were surrounded by youngsters brandishing startled-looking chameleons on the end of large sticks. Col shook his head indicating that chameleons were not on our list of purchases to take home. They stood and pressed their noses against the car windows till we left. There was a row of small round huts with thatch roofs with a much larger one in the centre when we arrived at the hotel late afternoon and announced ourselves at reception.
Hari brought the car around and we set off to see ‘la Fenêtre’ (the window), which is a large sandstone formation that has a window-like section through which you can photograph the setting sun. Numerous tour buses and 4x4s had people pouring out and walking briskly up the incline towards the ‘window’ before sunset. We climbed to the top and waited our turn, noting that it was indeed a spectacular view from the portal as we stepped up for our turn at photographing.

Neither of us had a great night — we were blaming the vinegary Clos Malaza we had consumed last night. After packing all 100 pieces of luggage into Hari’s 4x4 we set off to pick up the guide and follow him into the forest. He cautioned us not to point our fingers at anything, as this is considered ‘fady’ with the local Bara tribe. I suddenly felt an urgent need to point at a number of things as soon as I heard I shouldn’t. Although it wasn’t a difficult hike, my terrible sense of balance made it challenging at times when it was particularly rocky. We waded across a small stream and climbed many rocks till we came to a lovely waterfall with a clear pool around three metres deep. In winter when the rains fill the area, the entire sector is impassable. Tim and I followed the guide to the next piscine then we turned back to the car and waited half an hour for the others to join us. The guide brought us a picnic lunch after taking us to another site nearby. He produced some cheese and omelette rolls, bananas, jus and water from the back of the vehicle.
Tim and I were dropped off at the next hotel which was set in rock and mostly built of stone from the surrounding mountain. It looked a bit like a medieval castle. Beautifully manicured grounds with small ponds dotted around. After a shower we headed for some ‘welcome’ drinks of cherry juice (called carcadie).

Today we began the long drive to Ranomafana, which is 350 km away. It became very mountainous and verdant the closer we were to the park. Col popped into a bank with Hari leaving Tim and me to wait in the car which soon felt very hot and stuffy so I asked Tim to open the door. He did so and in fell a cackling, wizened old woman who resembled the witch in Snow White. Tim and I cowered in the corner as she spouted forth waving thin, long-nailed hands. I was wondering what to do, then decided to extract a 1,000 Ariary note (practically useless) and thrust it at her. That seemed to do the trick and off she went clutching the note to her skinny chest and cackling to herself.

The outskirts of Ranomafana, which was created in 1997, came into view. The lodge where we would stay for a few nights was set against the mountainside and had a spectacular view of the surrounding rainforest. Hari joined us at the bar then started playing the electric piano which was a huge surprise. An impromptu band followed, with someone banging on a wooden bowl and the receptionist made an appearance to sing. We figured she doubled as evening entertainment to supplement her income.
Not long after setting off in the forest, we came across the rare (and recently discovered) golden bamboo lemur. This was very exciting and we watched it for some minutes before continuing down the path.
Later Tim joined us for a night hike and the five of us set off into the forest. Emile showed us a huge leaf-tailed gecko, its head pointing down so as not to drown when it rains — because they spend their lives in humid, high-rainfall environments, water can easily pool in their nostrils if they are facing upwards during a torrential downpour, so gravity naturally drains any water away from their respiratory openings, keeping their airways clear.

Brown lemurs with red eyes scampered up high in the branches. Then at a lookout point, there was a small group of tourists staring at three fossas (pronounced ‘foosh’), which are the largest predator in Madagascar. Despite having a dog-like face and cat-like body, it is in fact a member of the mongoose family. They eat small rodents, fish and even birds. As we watched, they silently slunk back into the forest and disappeared from view. It was exciting to see them in the wild. Next we saw some mouse lemurs sleeping in the crook of a large tree. Emile pointed out some more chameleons. It was a very interesting walk and we were almost reluctant to return to the lodge for supper. That night in the dining room, the receptionist broke into song again. She was wearing a grey suit for the occasion and had put on a very bright red lipstick.

At two p.m., we left with Hari to a part of the river that was surrounded by caves. With the use of some torches we looked for the bats that reside there. We also tried to find some local frogs but didn’t see any and Hari reckoned they were buried under the huge pile of rubble left by the roadworks department.

Our next stop was Antsirabe. I noticed the local huts were a little different from the usual dark brown mud-and-wattle huts, having grass roofs of a more distinctive ochre colour; some dwellings were even double storey. There were many rice paddies and vegetable gardens. People had set up simple bamboo lean-tos on which their colourful wares were displayed. Bright orange carrots fought for supremacy with vivid green lettuce, crimson-red tomatoes, bright yellow lemons and oranges making colourful pyramids of fruit and vegetables.
Hari drove on via muddy roads till lunchtime, stopping at a restaurant close to Antsirabe where we passed a small group of women pounding maize in a large wooden bowl. This would be used to make bread and a kind of gruel or porridge. Later we booked into a hotel that resembled a railway station with its brick, green-painted walls and wide stairs. The dark interior and opulent furnishings were reminiscent of the 1940s.

I spent most of the journey staring out of the window watching life play itself out in day-to-day activities: cutting, buying, selling, carrying water in pots on the tops of heads, dogs ambling around looking for stray morsels of food, and children who ran around playing in the dusty street with tyres and tin cans. We stopped at a zebu factory where they make beautiful birds out of bone from zebu horns. We were led to a wooden bench to watch the process which was fascinating. First the horn is heated over an open fire which softens the bone and easily dislodges from the horn by hitting on it gently with a small wooden mallet. The inner piece is heated again to make it more malleable, then it is fashioned into a bird shape. A hot poker provides the eye sockets, and a hot chisel defines the feathers. The whole piece is smoothed over using a combination of sandpaper, hot ash and water. The end result is really lovely. Some of the birds are dyed with paint. A slight cough and the words ‘Voilà!’ alerted us to the fact some of the factory staff had set up a wooden table with the finished products for sale. Naturally we had to buy a piece and we were given the rough, unfinished bird to keep as well.
Col had arranged to meet with Monsieur Andre Perreras to buy some butterflies off him that he’d not managed to catch himself. He was in his element and spent ages going through all the wooden drawers of beautifully set papilionidae before choosing a few for a quarter of the going price. Hari drove down a narrow cobbled road to Hotel La Varangue where we checked in.

Sleep was again punctuated with dreams about being back at work, naked and in charge but knowing nothing, which woke me up with a start. I hate these naked dreams — they stay with you for a few hours while you remain vaguely embarrassed. After our last petit déjeuner in Madagascar I took a last wander down to the local marketplace inundated with ankle-biters wanting ‘les cadeaux’ (presents) and sellers of anything from postcards to vanilla pods. Hari dropped us off at the airport. We thanked him for all his driving, assistance and guiding. We were overweight again (in more ways than one after all the high-carb meals), so Col had to pay extra for the camera bag. Air Madagascar stood in wait on the runway and we turned and waved a fond farewell to the place. For diversity in animal life, it’s hard to beat.
