# Chapter 19: Madagascar 2006: The Camera Bag Pillow

As our first trip was so interesting, we’ve decided to visit Madagascar again. We had a lot of luggage, but what else is new. Whereas I liked to think I packed for what is required for the holiday, Col went without unimportant items like, let’s say, clothing, for extra lenses for his cameras and twenty more packets of film. He shoved Tim's hand luggage into a large carry-all that was already bursting with camera equipment and told Tim he was to casually sling this over his shoulder as though it contained no more than a travel pillow.
Col opened the garage door, which set the alarm off and delayed us further. We need not have rushed as the plane was delayed. Boarding time was suddenly announced just at the moment Tim had bought us a drink each. We had to empty the contents as quickly as we can down our throats, causing a lot of excess gas and burping from the three of us. For some reason, we were all seated in separate seats. I had a very large lady sitting next to me in the window seat. Her ample thighs encroached into my seat, forcing me into a coy position with shoulders narrowed and legs pressed primly together. It was not a comfortable trip, but luckily it was a relatively short one, and soon we were landing in Johannesburg. Tim manfully held onto his oversized bag while we grabbed our many pieces of 'hand luggage', and disembarked with a few hours to spare. We sauntered slowly over to gate 49, half an hour before boarding, only to discover that our plane was now at gate 2 – about five kilometres away. We ran as fast as three people carrying double their body weight in hand luggage can, (i.e. not very fast) and made it with seconds to spare. It seemed they had parked at the wrong gate. I hoped they got a ticket.
At the gate, we were greeted by Freda Flintstone and Happy Harry. I could cheerfully strangle both of them. There was no apology for having us rush to the gate from the next village. They just stood there while Happy chomped his way through a large bag of Niknaks, sprinkling a fine dusting of orange onto his tie while engaging in a long, involved conversation with Freda that is peppered with clicks and sighs. They eventually took our proffered tickets (Happy left a large orange thumbprint on mine), and we hoisted ourselves onto a bus and tiredly swayed our way towards the Boeing that stood waiting for its passengers. The seats were so small that movement was restricted to shallow breathing. Col's knees were at chest level and he did not look happy.
It was muggy and claustrophobic on board and I spent the first ten minutes in the air fiddling with the toggle thingy trying to get a jet of air onto my melting visage. I succeeded in only ejecting a weak blast of humidity for my pains. I sat back irritably after trying unsuccessfully to move my seat back, waiting for the seatbelt sign to go off so I could toddle over to the loo. I spotted three vacant seats at the back and on my return and told Col I was moving there. He joined me and we left Tim sprawled across the three newly vacated seats.
Arrival in Tana and Hotel Argeme
Due to the late take-off, we only arrived at Tana after 11 am with a very bumpy landing. There was a horrifically large queue in the airport as a huge Air France Boeing has just landed. We eventually made it to the other side of passport control and collect our mountain of luggage and found a rather wilted Hari, waiting for us with a handheld sign declaring the words, 'Mestorts fambly'. We met her on our last trip to Madagascar and had enlisted her services again. Col immediately left me to chat to Hari while he went off to exchange Rand notes into a wheelbarrow of Ariary. I was tired and irritable and my brain cells rummaged into their depths from 40-odd years ago to come up with a string of French words that would pass for intelligible, and fail by a verb or two. Hari looked puzzled and said no, she didn't want any wine. Strong, purposeful hands suddenly lunged for the pile of luggage at my feet. I yelled a few, 'Non, Non! Merci' to prevent the removal of our baggage by two eager porters to the awaiting voiture. They had been hovering over us since our pale (read 'wealthy') faces first entered the waiting hall.
Col appeared looking strangely misshapen of torso. His birder's jacket, that of the many pockets, had bulges everywhere. He muttered that several million Ariary notes had been acquired and were now safely in his pockets.
We followed Hari into the carpark where the company 4X4 awaited us. It was a newish model and boded well for the future travel plans.
Hotel Argeme appeared after a circuitous route through Tana. The narrow streets and haphazard houses were reminiscent of our trip to Nepal a few years before. The hotel was nestled down a narrow cobbled street and tucked away in the corner - a remnant of its colonial past. There were many interesting antiques, bric-a-brac and items of interest. A large wooden rickshaw model stood on the counter where we checked in. A beautiful silver and bronze peacock stood outside the door to our chambre, which was very clean and airy inside. A bar fridge huddled under a small counter and a huge mosquito-netted bed complete with the ubiquitous French bedroll which Col instantly flung onto the floor. What is it with cushions and men I wonder. Even at home, there’s a continuous removal and replacement of cushions in the lounge. In the lounge we encountered a group of très raucous Frenchmen in very high spirits (mentally and physically it would seem). Apparently, France had just won some sporting event. We repaired to our chambre for a shower. I was horrified to discover it was 12:45 local time as we were to be up by 6:30 for petit déjeuner. I was really tired but tossed and turned like a basting turkey till the early hours.

Exploring Andohahela Parc
I couldn't believe the cheek of our alarm clock when its shrill tones startled me out of my dreams and alerted me to the fact that 6:30 was almost upon us. A splash of cold water about the visage helped. I had to restrain Col from practising his French for, while his recall is passable, his pronunciation is not. Petit déjeuner consisted of yoghurt, bananas, rolls and croissants washed down with some strong coffee. Hari suddenly appeared with Dina in tow. She wished us a smiling, Bonjour. We had met Dina before, a small, dynamic woman who ran the Setam show with great efficiency. She presented us with the itinerary and entry tickets for the duration of our trip. She also gave us two data cards for l’Orange, the local network provider. We left with Hari for the airport. Tim and I sat in the back of the Land Cruiser watching the scenery move swiftly by. Many buildings in various states of repair, rice paddies, wooden stalls stacked high with oranges and other seasonal fruit. Women in colourful cloth and pork pie hats wandered along the paths, clutching either baskets of rice or children. Some of the baskets were laden with washing or wood for fires. The colourful wooden pousse-pousse's ambled by in huge numbers.  At passport control, there’s some delay because our hand luggage is overweight. Col kept repeating, 'Camera!' while I say, 'Computer!' They gave up and waved us through. At around ten to eleven, we boarded the small plane to Fort Dauphine, on the South East coast of Madagascar. They had just enough time to fling a poisson-flavoured roll and a bottle of water at us before we touched down in Fort Dauphin. 
We were met with thick humidity and struggled with our heavy hand luggage to the waiting bus. A few other couples join the bus and we set off through the seaside town of Jolagnaro to our hotel. The sea looked rough and waves were high. The roads were not at all good and the ride was very bumpy but the hotel was set in pleasant surroundings. A soft rain mixed with the heat to create a steamy jungle vibe. A tall, thin man wearing a grey cap joined us and introduced himself as 'Gooti' and declared his services as our local guide. He apologised for interrupting our lunch but said due to the recent heavy rainfall, the roads were now impassable to the forest camp, he suggested we visit the transition forest which included a spiny forest. Col was a little disappointed as he had some exotic butterflies in mind to add to his ever expanding collection, but we agreed it was better to change the plans, especially as climbing with heavy suitcases to the other camp would have been extremely unpleasant with the muddy paths.
Col and I went for a short walk down to the harbour after lunch. Small alcoved lean-tos housed locals huddled around coal braziers surrounding them in steam. A huge oil tanker bobbed gently in the grey sea while skeletons of ill fated vessels lay on the beach, bearing testament to the rough seas and inclement weather this side of Madagascar. We walked up to a small stone church where the sounds of a choir emanated from its holy depths and added to the general atmosphere. Monday, 3rd July: Camping in the Forest
Gooti, a driver, and a cook climbed into our hired vehicle so the seating was a bit squashed. I had my laptop on my knee, Tim lolling over me and Gooti next to him. The cook was buried under a mound of luggage in the back. The drive was interesting and we watched the change from lush rainforest to dry transition forest. Gooti speaks very good English; says he’s self-taught and we have some interesting conversations. After several stops along the way at local villages for extra water, more supplies, one stop revealed the presence of pitcher plants (Nepenthes madagascariensis.) Col and I jumped out to photograph these interesting plants. The road has deteriorated to mostly potholes with pure mud and dirt. The bumpy road did nothing to help Tim who sat grim-faced throughout the journey while his guts churned. We passed the area where the three-cornered palm is found (Neodypsis decary). It is the only place in the world where it exists. We arrived at last at the entrance to 'Parc Nationale Andohahela' where the 'office' was no more than a grass-roofed lean-to where two officials sat on plastic chairs next to a small wooden desk. 
Tim and I lay on a mattress on the riverbank while they set up camp. Col has disappeared into the nearby forest as was his wont. It was lovely and cool on the river. We were feeling quite peckish and I was wondering what would be offered for lunch. I haven't seen much more than various fruits in the back of the Land Cruiser. I sniffed suspiciously as a plume of smoke trailed towards us. We had brought some long-legged chickens but they looked very scrawny to me. I hear from Gooti that they’ll go to buy some wild hog meat from someone halfway up the mountain. Half an hour later we were called for lunch to find a makeshift wooden table and some rickety benches have been set up. The meal is surprisingly delicious, given the circumstances. A starter of potato, cucumber and boiled egg followed by a superb prawn and ginger dish with rice and some rolls. Gooti brought us a piece of wild hog to taste. I deferred and Tim didn't eat much as his tum was still delicate. After lunch I made some tea in a small bowl then Tim and I returned to our mattress next to the river and spent an hour relaxing with a cool breeze. I was busy transferring digital photos from camera to laptop when a shadow falls over me. I looked up startled to find a small minx smiling down at me. He’s wearing tatty shorts and a torn T-shirt, but his smile was wide and wholesome. He said his name was Constantine and he was 11 years old. He wasn't any taller than my elbow. His English was minimal and halting so I tried out some French. The result was an interesting conversation in Franglaise. He told me he lived nearby and that his father worked for ANGAP. He said he usually went to school by bus. He eventually tired of our conversation and slipped away. Later Col, Tim and I went for a long, circuitous walk around the river and into the transition forest. There’s a bit of rock hopping involved. We found baobabs and succulents en route. A beautiful waterfall and a few cool streams. After two hours we headed back to camp. The table has been moved closer to the car and candles stuck in sand-filled Coke bottles that have been cut in half which brought sufficient light to see what we were eating.

Spiny Forest and Lemurs

Around 3 a.m. I needed a wee so I crept outside barefoot and squatted the prerequisite distance from the tent. Bladder emptied, I walked gingerly back to the tent over the gravelly sand, bunching my toes as I walked to avoid sharp stones. As I got to the tent flap, my toes closed around a pile of sandy gravel. As I swung my leg into the opening, my toes unfurled and released their contents (roughly half a cup) of gravel over Col's face and hair. To say he was displeased is to make light of the moment, and he was startled into wakefulness with frantic scrabbling as he attempted to empty his mouth and hair of coarse grit. My attempts at apology were somewhat hampered by laughter.
It had become a lot cooler in the dawn and there was even some dew around the edges of the tent. The cups were so small as to be useless in my opinion so I resorted to drinking my tea out of a tin bowl. Following this repast, we walked through the spiny forest to the next camp. The car was to meet us there after packing away everything. It was a long, hot walk but not too strenuous. I enjoyed looking for giraffe beetles, chameleons and different foliage types such as an octopus tree (Didierea madagascarensis), fan palms and many others unique to the country. We arrived at the entrance to the spiny forest reserve after 8 km. There wasn't much here besides a small thatch-roofed office, a zebu pen and a demarcated car park. Water wasn’t available either but we had brought some from the other camp. After resting a while on some plastic chairs Col disappeared into the forest. Later they set up a large wooden table for lunch. Rice and vegetables with a spicy chicken and potato stew. This was followed by baked banana in a chocolate sauce. Amazing what they can do with a couple of charcoal burners. Several mangy hounds waited at our knees for a dropped morsel or two. The cook made us a huge plate of pancakes that evening. So much food! We went on a night walk after supper to walk off some calories. We headed into the darkness, head torches ablaze, and came across a white-fronted mouse lemur nestled in a spiny tree. Then we saw a red or rufous mouse lemur. I discovered the trick was to shine the torch into trees and pick up a glint of an eye, then focus, and usually another lemur was revealed. I spotted five lemurs this way. There were a few sleeping sifakas high up in a tree – too high to see clearly. Back in camp, we said we weren't hungry, just thirsty, but when supper arrived it was so delicious we devoured it all anyway. Zebu kebabs, tender and juicy with tomato, onion and aubergine with rice. We used a plastic bucket and cup to wash our hands and I sent out a hearty 'bonsoir' into the night and retired to our tent.

Berenty Bound

After entering the spiny forest we came to the camp we were supposed to have stayed in. A small group of sifakas perched high in a tree. Gooti pointed out a white-fronted mouse lemur curled up in its hole quite close to the path so we were able to take some good photos. The campsite was much nicer than the one we were in but totally inaccessible by car. In the continuing saga that involves me and my ablutions: I needed the loo and the guide pointed in the general direction. I couldn't find it so I squatted behind some crumbling fireplaces. I was just dusting myself off, so to speak, when the guide appeared and said,
‘Les toilettes sont ici!’
He pointed behind me, and half hidden by some Thilachium latifolium bushes was a proper flush toilet in a cement enclosure. I blushed and muttered that I didn't see it and awkwardly side-shuffled to the car.
Benoir, our new guide for the next three days, met us and introduced himself. He spoke good English with a strong American accent which I tried not to feel irritated by — how it is that most non-English-speaking people the world over choose to learn American English with its accompanying twang, which bears little resemblance to the original, is beyond me — Tim said I needed to ‘chill’ and he was probably right.
The bus rumbled over the uneven dirt roads and we gazed through the dusty windows at the passing sisal fields before arriving at Berenty Reserve, eager to see what the next few days would bring. The area appeared empty of visitors besides ourselves. Our cabin had two bedrooms and a sizeable shower. We had drinks at the restaurant and looked out over the red dust and sparse thorn trees before looking for lemurs and soon found the lovely ring-tailed lemurs in a tree.

Berenty Wildlife

Col and I joined Benoir for a walk around Berenty estate. It was a huge place. We saw our first sunning ringtails sitting in a row in yoga poses, arms outstretched, bellies exposed to the sun. Then we found a troop of sifakas with their dark brown faces and white furry bodies sitting in a large Didierea tree.
Col lent Tim his digital camera so he was snapping away in delight at some sifakas nearby. Around six, just as it was getting dark, we went for some hot chocolate then joined Benoir in the combi for a night walk. We had some good sightings of mouse lemurs, brown lemurs, lepilemur, white-footed lemur and a fat-tailed dwarf lemur that night. All very exciting and we chattered animatedly en route to the dining room where crab crêpes, chicken and banana awaited us. There was some traditional dancing that night around a huge bonfire. About eight dancers, dressed in red, with a few musicians playing an accordion, gave us a lively performance. At one stage, probably after my second coconut rum, I got up and joined them for a 'Malagasy Mamba' which was met with great hilarity.

Rare Sightings

I awoke to an intensely bright light shining down on my face. I'd forgotten to switch off the lights last night and the generators usually go off at 12. Breakfast was nothing startling as it consisted of cold sweet potato, hard French loaf cut into pieces and jams of indiscriminate origin. For some reason, stale Madeira cake was also part of the unappealing carb-laden fare. Luckily my tea didn't fail me. My milk-making process was watched in amusement by a British girl who passed by and announced that they did have milk here. I told her that since Tim was very ill in India from drinking unpasteurised milk, I was not taking any chances. She said that she worked in a zoo in Bristol, UK, working mainly with lemurs and had just returned from doing some research in the northern part of Madagascar.
Tim, Col and I joined Benoir for a forest walk. It was lovely and cool and we saw the stirrings of a few ring-tailed lemurs and sifakas. Then we came across a rare silky sifaka sitting high in the canopy. The silky sifaka is a large lemur characterised, as its name implies, by long, silky, white fur. It has a very restricted range and is known locally as the simpona or ‘ghost of the forest’. It is one of the rarest mammals on Earth apparently and a privileged sighting. Benoir said there were only four of them in this forest. We were ecstatic at seeing this beautiful creature. We then followed a group of red-tailed lemurs across the red earth road where they began their yoga ritual. They scampered around after sunning themselves and Tim, who was still clutching Col's digital camera, took some lovely photos of them jumping from tree to tree. I chatted to Benoir about his family. He had four children but only saw them every couple of weeks in high season. I said that must be difficult for him and he gave a small shrug.
We met up with the British girl, Sally. She had seen much the same as us but was very jealous of our silky sifaka sighting. Today's lunch was 'porc avec les légumes sauté’ (pork and sautéed vegetables). Benoir told me of the importance of ritual in death on Madagascar. After someone dies, they burn the house and kill all the zebu. I only knew of Famadihana; the Betsileo and the Merina peoples who have the custom of 'turning' the dead. The bones are rewrapped in new shrouds of silk, sometimes sprayed with either perfume or wine, after which they 'participate' in the festivities of music, dancing and food before they are replaced in their tombs.
Benoir met us at 4 p.m. for a walk to the bat forest. The Madagascan flying fox bats are the largest of the island’s species and they eat figs, flowers, leaves and other fruit. We came across a huge tree that was covered in them. They were a reddish-brown colour. Benoir cautioned us to keep still so as not to disturb them then he made a few sudden exaggerated movements which sent them flying, which confused us at first but we got some nice photos of them in flight.
Fort Dauphine


This morning we stop about 15 km outside a village to look for some tree frogs and are lucky enough to find three of them in the bushes. A yellow frog, a brown frog and a big lime green one. The next stop is down a dusty, sheer road to look for pitcher plants. We find a clump near the side of the road and look down their slippery trumpets to find captured beetles, butterflies and grasshoppers which are being slowly digested by enzymes which lie at the bottom of the cone-shaped plants.
As we arrive at the Lodge around 5km outside of Fort Dauphine, I notice several wooden cabins situated along the edge of a lagoon - ours is literally on its doorstep. There’s a good view of the lagoon from our table in the restaurant. I switch on my mobile phone and see I’ve missed a missed call from Gautier. I phone him back and tell him we have arrived. He arrives ten minutes later and says he’s unable to take Col to the St Marie coastal forest as planned but a friend will be taking his place.

Tim puts on Col's wetsuit and has a snorkel in the lagoon. He makes friends with a local dog who we call Flea. He’s a friendly looking golden lab. Tim feeds him some stale bread which he wolfs down. 
Flea slept outside our room last night and follows us to breakfast where he’s rewarded with some stale bread and omelette. I get a message from mum to say that John was on TV last night, talking about being a disabled artist in Clarens. Pity I missed that.

I phone John after breakfast ( chocolate croissants and tea) and we chat about his television debut. It’s raining softly this morning, shrouding everything in a grey mist.Tim goes for another row in the yellow canoe while I pack. Col disappears into the forest to try get some more butterflies in his net and to check the traps.  Flea jumps in the taxi so we turn round and drop him back at the hotel. He follows us again and this time we convince the taxi driver to take him back after dropping us off at the airport. We meet Benoit who explains that Flea always accompanies guests that he makes friends with, to the airport. Apparently his owner left Madagascar a year ago and the poor doggie was left behind. On seeing my tears welling up he reassures us that he’s well fed and looked after. 
Tulia Airport is a small, bustling little place. We notice at once, a man holding up a sign with our name (mis)spelt and head towards him. He’s to be our driver. We have a short tortured conversation in ‘Franglaise’. Tim buys some Cokes off an Indian woman selling drinks from a bucket. The driver seems anxious to get going but Col says we are to meet a man named Moosa. 

The driver shakes his head and says he doesn’t understand. I tell him,

‘Mon mari veut rendez-vous avec Moosa maintenant!’ ( My husband wants to meet with Moosa now).

Which seems to jog his memory. He says we’ll meet him there then drives like someone possessed. Occasionally he points to a passing blur and says it’s some l’oiseaux ( bird) or other but we have to take his word for it. Eventually we arrive at Moosa’s house which is more of a hut really, made of wood and dried leaves. A  thick-set, swarthy man arrives, hand outstretched and claims the title of Moosa. Col arranged to meet with him earlier as he’d heard he’s a good birder. They plan a rendezvous tomorrow 6:30am. 

Hotel Nautilus looms in the dark and we’re greeted by a pleasant woman of French origin called Natalie. Our room is cochlea-shaped, and lies right on the beach. The round inner sanctum contains the shower and toilet.
The shower also contains some wildlife in the shape of a giant cockroach which elicits a short scream from me (largely ignored by Col and Tim). We unpack and go in search of supper. This is a short walk away, down a sandy path to a huge verandah with tables and an outside bar. A lovely sea-themed mural has been painted against one wall. The waiters are not totally conversant with English but with a lot of miming, pointing and smiling, we get by and find ourselves tucking into an  'ecrevisse au grillé ‘ We’re also given a complimentary rum punch.. Thus replete, we waddle back to our room where Col prepares for his sortie tomorrow. He mentions that he’s going by quad bike. On hearing this Tim perks up and says he’ll join him.

A short night later, Col and Tim are fumbling around in the dark as the generator only comes on at 7am. I get dressed and decide to go down for brekky. I notice that the door lock is very flimsy, so I hide the laptops and some cameras under the bed. Even if the staff are trustworthy, which I’m sure they are, I reason that it’s never fair to tempt anyone by leaving items of value lying around. I then place a pile of pillows under the sheets to look like someone’s sleeping there. Sod dictates that this is a great moment for the chamber maid to walk by as I’m about to close the door, so I pantomime an elaborate waving goodbye to a lump of pillows. 

I discover, too late that the ‘lait’ in my coffee is of the zebu variety and feel a bit ill. I’m sure there’s nothing wrong with zebu milk but I’m fussy, ( you might have noticed.)  However my subconscious speaks to my stomach and whispers that the milk may not be, (shudder,) pasteurised and consequently find I have a runny tummy. I grab my camera and take the steps leading to the beach where I’m instantly inundated by kids wanting to sell me; boat rides, lengths of colourful cloth, hair-plaiting or just cash without all the unnecessary giving of something in return. I hastily retreat to the room and tidy up and to search for a spare lock which we have somewhere in our luggage. Can’t find it but when plunging my hand into a wash bag, my fingers encounter the sharp blade of Col’s razor. This slices off a good portion of digit and blood spurts forth, simultaneously my bowels clench, indicating the imminent release of their contents. I rush to the loo, grab some toilet paper and wrap half a roll around my finger, while lowering my drawers with the other hand. It is with absolute horror that I hear the chamber maid enter the door and ask if I have any dirty laundry. Her voice is approaching the inner sanctum, so I grab my trousers with one hand, trying not to bleed all over them, and bellow,



‘Un moment, por favor,!’ 

Which besides being half Portuguese, is spoken too late. So I’m caught with my pants half mast, my incomplete finger still dripping blood, and the surprise on the maid’s face is both comical and embarrassing. 

‘Le plaster! I look for le plaster!’  

I babble, casting round for the small box of Disney plasters. She retreats with a garbled, ‘Désolé!’.

I find a box of Elastoplast instead of the Mickey Mouse plasters and dress my finger as best as I can. Col and Tim return half an hour later. Col says he saw the rare Ground Roller and Tim rode the quad bike so both are happy and not the least bit interested in hearing about my bowels or bleeding finger.

I’d  left the curtains open last night so the view of the sparkling azure lagoon is lovely to wake up to this sunny day.   Tim and Col leave for a boat dive; Tim in a hired wetsuit. I walk along the water’s edge, actually I wade in the water as that takes more effort which is what I’m aiming for having consumed too many croissants au beurre. I pass local people searching under rocks and in the damp sand for molluscs and small shells. There are children with buckets, and fishermen gathering in their catch from rickety wooden boats. I watch a lone fisherman stand and cast his net wide from his small dhow which makes for a good photo. 
Monsieur et Madame ‘Le Suave’ greet us regally and show us to our table for lunch. We order zebu steaks fois trois. Two with mustard, which is delicious.
After an indecently short time later, it’s time to reassemble my dry stiff locks into something resembling hair.  Tim is sporting a bouffant and Col, a straight, sleek cap of hair. This interesting trio of gens troop off for a pre dinner drink. We discover the chairs and sun loungers have been pushed into a sort of semi-circle in preparation for our evening’s entertainment. Col tries to avoid both the entertainment and accompanying payment by sitting on one of the tables, but is soon ushered briskly to the chairs by Madame ‘Nautilus’. 
So we’re going to watch the entertainment whether we want to or not. An eager waiter prances over to us and provides a ‘free’ rum punch. Our glasses and those of other captive guests are filled almost before we’d finished half the contents. This ploy works well as, by the time a troupe of half-naked, bum wiggling, bosom thrusting dancers come on, there’s much cheering, shouts of rather premature ‘encore!’ and general encouragement from the already inebriated audience. We’ve endured only half an hour of shuffling around and singing when a small woven basket comes around for collection. We slide a few notes of the lowest denomination into the basket, feeling this adequately pays for the ‘free’ drinks and miniature garlic clams that have been consumed. Col’s smile slips somewhat when Monsieur ‘Le Suave’, overcome with rum punch and the sight of his beloved Madame shuffling around with the dancers, enthusiastically grabs the Ariary-laden basket, empties it into his pockets and energetically tosses the empty basket back into the audience with a loud, ‘Voila!’. He’s also  flung several ‘mille d’argen’ into its depths and encourages us to place ‘plus d’argent encore’ (more money) within.
The second round is fraught with miserliness fighting with guilt. Guilt wins the toss and notes pile up again as men fumble in their wallets and are forced to extract the folded 20,000 Ariary note they’d tried to hide the first time round. Col takes so long with the basket as he fumbles with a fan of high denomination notes, while searching for a lower amount, that I’m forced to lean over his knee to hide the miserly attempt. Now thoroughly disgruntled and hungry in equal measure, we sip on the last of our ‘free’ rum and wait for the entertainment to cease. 
Tim and Col have the last available zebu steaks so I order,

‘Une crayfish avec fromage?’

It arrives sans fromage but tastes delicious anyway. I’m watching with interest at a French couple at the next table with lips pursed in a permanent ‘moue’ as if constantly prepared for speech.

None of us sleep well. Col has a cold, that he ‘knew’ he’d get after being sneezed on by a passenger on the plane. I’d just missed being sprayed with virulent bugs as my head was buried in an Air Madagascar sick bag at that moment. I hadn’t been nauseous; I was just trying it out. I’d also stomped outside wearing nothing but a hand towel around two am in search of the source of music I could hear. The moon was full and bright but no sign of music. It later turns out that Tim fell asleep with my iPod on and so it continued to play till the battery ran out.

I leave Tim on the verandah swinging in a hammock and go for another wade to the other side of the beach where I exchange a free pen from a well known antibiotic company for a leopard-shell conch shell with a small hole in it. The old woman was ferreting in the damp sand at low tide, looking for clams, when she found it. Despite being ninety years old, myopic and probably illiterate she seems well pleased with the pen. I figure the exchange is fair as she can sell it to the highest bidder (pens are a popular item) and I get my shell without damaging any reef. Win-win. I wade back in time to see Col alighting from a small wooden dhow that he’d certainly not sailed off in. It seems he was transferred ‘sur la mer’ from the other boat. Although it was a great dive, his head is still full of cold which makes diving a little difficult so we have an early tea then Tim and I go to look at some tombs nearby. 
I’m hoping it isn’t considered ‘fady,’ (taboo) to have us walking all over the sacred burial ground. Famadihana or ‘Dancing with the dead’ occurs when the Malagasy people open the tombs of their dead every few years. They rewrap the bodies in fresh burial cloth then members of the family take their ancestors for a ‘dance’ near the tomb with great ceremony before returning them to the tomb. The bodies are sometimes sprayed with perfume or even wine before they’re wrapped in their new shroud.This turning of the bones ceremony, held every two to seven years, is a vital part of helping to maintain links with their revered ancestors, who still play a very real role in daily life. We aren’t able to be part any such ceremony unfortunately.

Lunch is, (be still my beating heart), …Zebu steaks ‘á trots sans moutarde’ which is a pity as I rather like mustard on my zebu. I figure we have again consumed an entire zebu over the course of this trip. 
Col walks to the forest to collect butterflies with his net and to check the traps, redolent with ‘eau de  rotten banane’.
The car is packed and we head off in the direction of Isalo National Parc. It seems Saturday is market day for most of the villages that we pass. They have their wares on colourful display as we pass by. In town Hari helps me choose some provisions- I buy bananas, long French loaves, cream cheese, water, Coke and chocolate then walk into some stalls that smell strongly of soap and cleaning fluid to buy some cans of juice (soda). 
As we continue towards Isalo, the surrounding seaside scrub and dry bush becomes grassy then mountainous. Outside a small village, we stop for a picnic lunch. Realising we don’t have a knife to smear on the cream cheese, I improvise with small twigs and the back of a keyring. The French loaves are quite long so I end up giving the rest of mine to a passer by. 
I carry Tim on my back to the car as the ground is very thorny and he’s barefoot. He’s nearly as tall as me now, and we’re in stitches of laughter as I stagger as quickly as I can to the waiting vehicle. It is hot and very dry so we swig at our water and Hari turns on the air con. We stop to look at a huge baobab next to the road. it’s root-like branches almost welcoming us. It isn’t long before we’re surrounded by youngsters brandishing startled looking chameleons on the end of large sticks. Col shakes his head indicating that chameleons are not on our list of purchases to take home. They stand and press their noses against the car windows till we leave. There’s a row of small round huts with thatch roofs with a much larger one in the centre when we arrive at the hotel late afternoon and announce ourselves at reception.
Hari brings the car around and we set off to see ‘la Fenêtre’ (the window), which is a large sandstone formation that has a window-like section through which you can photograph the setting sun. Numerous tour busses and 4X4’s have people pouring out and walking briskly up the incline towards the ‘window’ before sunset. We climb to the top and wait our turn, noting that it is indeed a spectacular view from the portal as we step up for our turn at photographing. 

Neither of us have a great night- we’re blaming the vinegary Clos Malaza we consumed last night. After packing all 100 pieces of luggage into Haris 4X4 and we set off to pick up the guide and follow him into the forest. He cautions us not to point our fingers at anything, as this is considered ‘fady’ with the local Bara tribe. I suddenly feel an urgent need to point at a number of things as soon as I hear I shouldn’t.  Although it’s not a difficult hike, my terrible sense of balance makes it challenging at times when it’s particularly rocky. We wade across a small stream and climb many rocks till we come to a lovely waterfall with a clear pool around three metres deep. In winter when the rains fills the area, the entire area is impassable. Tim and I follow the guide to the next piscine then we turn back to the car and wait half an hour for the others to join us. The guide brings us a picnic lunch after taking us to another site nearby. He produces some cheese and omelette rolls, bananas, jus and water from the back of the vehicle. 
Tim and I are dropped off at the next hotel which is set in rock and is mostly built of stone from the surrounding mountain. It looks a bit like a medieval castle. Beautifully manicured grounds with small ponds dotted around. After a shower we head for some ‘welcome’ drinks of cherry juice (called carcardie).

Today we begin the long drive to Ranomafana, which is 350km away. It becomes very mountainous and verdant the closer we are to the Parc. Col pops into a bank with Hari leaving Tim and I to wait in the car which soon feels very hot and stuffy so I ask Tim to open the door. He does so and in falls a cackling, wizened old woman who resembles the witch in Snow White. Tim and I cower in the corner as she spouts forth waving thin, long nailed hands. I’m wondering what to do, then decide to extract a 1000 Ariary note ( practically useless) and thrust it at her. That seems to do the trick and off she goes clutching the note to her skinny chest and cackling to herself. 

The outskirts of Ranomafana which was created in 1997, come into view. The Lodge where we will stay for a few nights, is set against the mountain side and has a spectacular view of the surrounding rainforest. Hari joins us at the bar then starts playing the electric piano which is a huge surprise. An impromptu band follows, with someone banging on a wooden bowl and the receptionist has made an appearance to sing. We figure she doubles as evening entertainment to supplement her income.
 Not long after setting off in the forest, we come across the rare (and recently discovered), Golden Bamboo lemur. This is very exciting and we watch it for some minutes before continuing down the path. 
Later Tim joins us for a night hike and the five of us set off into the forest. Emile shows us a huge Leaf tailed gecko, it’s head pointing down 
so as not to drown when it rains because they spend their lives in incredibly humid, high-rainfall environments, water can easily pool in their nostrils if they are facing upwards during a torrential downpour so gravity naturally drains any water away from their respiratory openings, keeping their airways clear.

Brown Lemurs with red eyes, scamper up high in the branches. Then at a look out point, there’s a small group of tourists staring at three Fossa (pronounced ‘foosh’) which are the largest predator in Madagascar. Despite having a dog-like face and cat like body, it is in fact a member of the Mongoose family. They eat small rodents, fish and even birds. As we watch, they silently slink back into the forest and disappear from view. It was exciting to see them in the wild. Next we see some Mouse lemurs sleeping in the crook of a large tree. Emile points out some more chameleons. It’s a very interesting walk and we’re almost reluctant to return to the Lodge for supper. Tonight in the dining room, the receptionist breaks into song again. She’s wearing a grey suit for the occasion and has put on a very bright red lipstick.

At  two pm, we leave with Hari to a part of the river that’s surrounded by caves. With the use of some torches we look for the bats that reside there. Also try to find some local frogs but don’t see any and Hari reckons they’re buried under the huge pile of rubble left by the Roadworks department. 

Our next stop is Antsirabe. I notice the local huts are a little different from the usual dark brown mud and wattle huts, having grass roofs of a more distinctive ochre colour, some dwellings are even double storey. There are many rice paddies and vegetable gardens. People have set up simple bamboo lean to’s on which their colourful ware is displayed. Bright orange carrots fight for supremacy with vivid green lettuce, crimson red tomatoes, bright yellow lemons and oranges make colourful pyramids of fruit and vegetable. 
Hari drives on via muddy roads till lunch time stopping at a restaurant close to Antsirabe where we pass a small group of women pounding maize from a large wooden bowl.This will be used to make bread and a kind of gruel or porridge.  Later we book into a hotel that resembles a railway station with its brick, green painted walls and wide stairs. The dark interior and opulent furnishings were reminiscent of the 1940’s.

I spend most of the journey staring out of the window watching life play itself out in day to day activities; cutting, buying, selling, carrying water in pots on the top of heads, dogs ambling around looking for stray morsels of food to eat, and children who run around playing in the dusty street with tyres and tin cans. We stop at a Zebu factory where they make beautiful birds out of bone from zebu horns. We’re led to a wooden bench to watch the process which is fascinating. First the horn is heated over an open fire which softens the bone and easily dislodges  from the horn by hitting on it gently with a small wooden mallet. The inner piece is heated again to make it more malleable, then it is fashioned into a bird shape. A hot poker provides the eye sockets, and a hot chisel defines the feathers. The whole piece is smoothed over using a combination of sandpaper, hot ash and water. The end result is really lovely. Some of the birds are dyed with paint. A  slight cough and the words ‘Voila!’ alert us to the fact  some of the factory staff have set up a wooden table with the finished products for sale. Naturally we have to buy a piece and we we’re given the rough, unfinished bird to keep as well. 
Col has arranged to meet with Monsieur Andre Perreras to buy some butterflies off him that he’d not managed to catch himself. He’s in his element and spends ages going through all the wooden drawers of beautifully set papillae before choosing a few for quarter of the going price.. Hari drives down a narrow cobble stoned road to Hotel La Verangue where we check in.

Sleep is again punctuated with dreams about being back at work, naked and in charge but knowing nothing which wakes me up with a start. I hate these naked dreams - they stay with you for a few hours while you remain vaguely embarrassed. After our last petit dejeuner in Madagascar I take a last wander down to the local market place inundated with ankle biters wanting ‘les cadeux,’ (presents) and sellers of anything from post cards to vanilla pods. Hari drops us off at the airport. We thank him for all his driving, assistance and guiding. We’re overweight again ( in more ways than one after all the high carb meals,) so Col has to pay extra for the camera bag. Air Madagascar stands in wait on the runway and we turn and wave a fond farewell to the place. For diversity in animal life, it’s hard to beat.
