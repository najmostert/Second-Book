# Chapter 11: Saudi Arabia 1999

Col and I worked in Saudi Arabia for almost three years. It was a whole new cultural experience—and not always in a good way. At this time, between 1999 and 2001, the Kingdom strictly allowed outsiders into the country only if they worked in essential services, such as teachers, doctors, and nurses. Menial labor, the hospitality industry, and shopkeeping were undertaken almost exclusively by expatriate workers from the Philippines, Pakistan, and India. Col worked as a pathologist at one military hospital while I worked at another facility some thirty-five kilometres away, holding a senior tutor’s position in the nursing education department. Women were strictly prohibited from driving at this time so I relied on local taxis for travelling to and from work, occasionally caught the shared bus from our residential compound into the city centre. This was the Saudi Arabia of the deep desert, royalty, and vast oil wealth, governed by strict Islamic rules that dictated exactly how we lived and dressed. The dress regulations affected me most of all; I was required to wear a flowing black abaya and a full head cover whenever I stepped outside the perimeter of our compound. Unlike most Western expats who lived in open suburban complexes, our living quarters were located within a strict, heavily guarded military hospital compound. I visited the Saudi ARAMCO compound a few times; stepping through those gates was like magically transporting from the Arabian desert straight onto a typical suburban street in small-town America! I was so envious of the women there, who were free to wear skirts and T-shirts, and ambling around comfortably in shorts across bright green lawns. They had their own Western-style stores, a movie theatre, and even an official church building. I learned how to adapt and even how to use the strict laws of the land against the bureaucracy when I wanted to achieve something—such as successfully forcing management to change our teaching uniforms from a stiff, high-necked Victorian affair into a far more practical white jacket and trousers. I also discovered how to brew a very passable red wine utilising grape juice, a twenty-five-litre water canister and a repurposed hospital chest drain. I made wonderful friends with people from all over the world and was introduced to the strange expatriate custom of the Hash House Harriers. The running club had originally originated in Kuala Lumpur back in 1938, founded by a group of British colonial officers and expatriates who wanted a structured way to burn off the excesses of their indulgent lifestyles. The underground Saudi version was simply a brilliant excuse for expats to meet up secretly in the middle of the desert, run trails and drink weak homemade beer far away from the strict, watchful rules of everyday life. On one occasion, I was unwittingly caught getting a lift from a British male friend of a colleague, who had forged a military gate pass to enter the facility. As a result, I spent almost an entire day locked in a room being interrogated by the Saudi military police. Tim was only six years old at the time and had to adapt the most of all, often being left on his own for hours, attending an international school, and making friends whom he could only play with if we could successfully secure an official military gate pass for them. The positive side of the expatriate contract was the financial freedom; we could easily afford to take long, vacations in exotic corners of the world and send substantial savings back home to South Africa. It was inherently easier for Col as a male professional and he thoroughly enjoyed the short working hours, dealing with a third less clinical work than he faced at home for twice the pay. I found it far more difficult to live there as a woman. I was constantly harassed by local men when out shopping, found the summer heat oppressive—especially when covered from head to toe in heavy, dark garments—and I deeply missed home, the freedom of being able to drive and my personal independence. When we finally returned to Cape Town in late 2001, I wrote a book about our time spent in Saudi Arabia as a therapeutic way to readapt to civilisation and as a practical means of letting other non-Muslim women get a true, unfiltered idea of what to expect before signing a contract. After a few years, ‘The land of lost logic’ was published.
