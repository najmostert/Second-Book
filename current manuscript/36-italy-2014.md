# Chapter 33: Italy 2014

I thought Italy would be a lovely place to go during the final throes of a divorce. There were a few hectic weeks prior to leaving as it was a last-minute thing; a bit like the divorce itself. Tim was in the middle of university exams and no-one was sleeping much, least of all myself. I was also still reeling from the recent untimely death of my dear mum and subsequent changes to the house as John and his carer came to live with me. Shortly after that one of our three dogs, Shadow, died after falling in my small pond. To say I’d been stressed was to make light of my disposition.
That day saw me tearing around after seeing my ex off to work with his sandwiches and yoghurt, (some habits take  to change) and Col was still living at home until he could buy a house for himself and his girlfriend.

The taxi arrived a few minutes early so Tim and I had  time at the airport to stop at The Mug and Bean and drink endless cups of coffee while catching up, like me he was still processing the imminent divorce. Just before leaving, a message came in from Col wishing us both a safe flight. I know he felt some guilt as there was still an element of caring between us.
The train from Fiumicino took us straight into the centre of Rome. I gazed out of the window at old crumbling buildings, as in poor state of repair, rather than historically old. Also a lot of graffiti.
At Termini we headed towards the The Beehive back packers. 
An old-fashioned lift creaked its way upwards to the fifth floor where our room was situated.
At the door, I began frantically searching for the keys which we’d just been given at reception. I spilled the contents of my rucksack all over the floor but couldn’t find them so ran down the five flights of stairs again to see if I’d dropped them en route. Back at the top stairs, I sat down, wheezing with exhaustion. I was out of breath and red with exertion and heat. Tim looked anxious till I regained my breath and we both looked through my rucksack again and there they were — smiling maliciously up at us from the depths of my rucksack.
Tim wisely said nothing, just opened the door which led to a small square hallway.
 A few green trams trundled by as we were waiting for lunch. Our Indian waiter didn’t allow us to finish our sentences, completing them for us which was both amusing and annoying, especially when he got it wrong.

Tim:       ‘Hmm, I’ll have the garlic and…’
Waiter:    ‘Aubergine al gricia!’
Tim:       ‘Er, no pizza el pollo’
Me:        ‘I’ll have the spaghetti …’
Waiter:    ‘Prego! Spaghetti Puttanesca.’
Me:        ‘Um, no, the spaghetti carbonara.’

Afterwards we took a short walk, passing a huge green-hued, copper statue of a priest with a long flowing ‘cloak’. Tim said it had once been a fully rounded cloak where children could hide but this was considered un-PC, so they’d chopped a part of it away. Sad to have to desecrate statues because of a small percentage of sick humans but here we are.

 I got up to make some black coffee and sat drinking it at the small formica table that faced the open window from which only the grooves of buildings were visible.
We planned to visit the Colosseum on Palatine Hill; the largest amphitheatre ever built. As we arrived at the entrance to the Forum and the Arco di Costantino, I stopped to gaze at the beautiful carved relic from the 2nd century of Ancient Rome dedicated to the emperor Constantine the Great. Thin slices of red bricks marked out the Forum as we walked past to the Colosseum. Unfortunately for us, they were busy renovating it and was marred somewhat by construction beams.
 Along the wide open pavement buskers sang and performed, horse and carts rode by and pavement artists displayed their art. A number of large hairy males dressed up in red togas, sandals and helmets, posed with tourists for a fee. Grassy patches lay in between the ruins with only two columns with the original marble at the base. The entire forum is built on top of older palaces and courtyards. Crumbling dusty rooms belonging to Augustus overlooking Circus Maximus. The reason for so much frantic renovation was to mark the bimillennium of Emperor Augustus’s death (19th August 14 AD). We walked through the ‘Audience’ chamber, a huge room designed for meeting with the populace. The House of Augustus was very simple and austere compared with the rest of the palace.

I read that it’s estimated there are more than 120,000 stray cats in Rome and protecting them has been tradition since the days of the ancient Roman Empire. Historically they were considered sacred to the goddess Diana and were kept as a way to control the pest population as the city grew as well as having them as pets for companionship. I liked them even more after hearing that.

 I awoke to the sound of rain — lots of it so ran to the window, opened the shutters and took in a deep breath of fresh cool air before making a pot of coffee.


After breakfast we headed for the Vatican over the bridge that crossed the River Tiber while the weather alternated with sun and soft rain. When we got to the entrance to the Vatican a man wearing long trousers and a soft shirt came up to us and introduced himself as the ‘best guide in Italy with the best deal’. How could we resist such confidence.
 At the appointed hour we joined the tour group to join  Paulo whose pants pocket bore the legend, ‘sex and great jeans’. He greeted us warmly. His English wasn’t great; garbled in fact and he used the word ‘actually’ with gay abandon.
 We were joined by two British girls wearing Islamic garb, a young Irish lad and a middle-aged couple from Israel. The small group listened to Paulo’s strangled explanation of Leonardo’s painting on the Sistine Chapel in his tortured English and liberally peppered with ‘actually’s’.

After almost an hour outside, it was time to ‘actually’ enter the Vatican and the Sistine Chapel. It was difficult trying to hear what was being said while following Paulo’s disappearing figure holding a purple umbrella upright between numerous other tours. Paulo led us through long and short corridors, up and down stairs, past statues, paintings and items of such sumptuousness of colour that it was hard to take in. Old tapestries covered the walls with frail and faded beauty, gold painted friezes glowed in the dim light.

We followed Paulo through the dome and the Basilica, past more mosaics and water features and huge paintings depicting simple Greek design vs Leonardo’s realism. Roman marble busts of Emperors, intricately painted friezes shone with gold paint, huge black marble baths from Egypt sat motionless, awaiting naked royalty to gently lower themselves into their smooth, cool depths.
I was almost hysterical with laughter at one point as Tim and I having dawdled, lost the group and tried to find Paulo among hundreds of other tour groups when suddenly we heard a loud, ‘Actually,’ and knew we’d found him. We passed huge rooms housing famous paintings by Dali, Matisse, and Leonardo and I was excited to see these famous works of art in person — ‘actually’ we wanted to stop and look at them properly, but Paulo was tired by now, his English becoming more garbled and less coherent, so he hurried us along. The Sistine Chapel was a little disappointing – as recognisable as it was, it seemed to lack the sumptuous splendour of the previous rooms and there were so many rules: no photography, no talking and no loitering, so we were ushered along quickly to allow the hordes behind us cthe chance to look. I felt this conveyer belt viewing kind of defeated the purpose somewhat. Paulo left us rather abruptly having collected his fee outside the Basilica and hurried off towards the exit, probably towards a large glass of vin rouge.
We dutifully trooped back inside. The Basilica is a beautiful building, also full of colour and expensive gold, but I was too tired to appreciate it fully, so didn’t stay too long and trudged back towards the station, stopping briefly at a restaurant to have a look at the menu. I would have liked to say their fare was mouth-watering, but it was the prices that were eye-watering, so we beat a hasty retreat. Down a few more side streets until we found a suitable cafe, both groaning gratefully as we sank into our seats. Even Tim’s legs were hurting but more due to his previously injured leg than the tour. Col sent me a message saying he couldn’t get the TV sound to work and I had to laugh. Matters of a technical area had always been my domain.
Despite our late night, I was awake early for coffee which I drank while watching the city awaken. A few workmen began their work before the heat of the day suffocated the city. As I watched, I mused that it all looked so familiar yet simultaneously so different from every place I had been to.
Our plan was to take the metro to Naples for one night. As we wandered the Via Cavour looking for a place for breakfast, a small cafe declared rather boldly that it sold the ‘best’ coffee in Rome, so we decided to give it a try. I got stuck in the loo because the light went out and I kept locking rather than unlocking the door in the dark.

‘What took you so long?’ Tim enquired, not unreasonably as he’d heard me say that I was going to pay a good forty minutes ago.
After packing we walked in the warm sunshine with our bags to Termini station and bought two tickets to Napoli Centrale.
As the train arrived, we rushed to get a window seat. Unfortunately, the train filled up fast and soon Tim was squashed up against a large, buxom woman. He tried his best to keep his six-foot frame within his seat but too much of hers was spilling over into his space. We both gazed fixedly out of the large window. Urban sprawl soon gave way to verdant countryside. Despite the many grape vines, it was not a pretty area. We arrived at Napoli and exited the cool confines of the train into the intense heat to stand blinking in the sunlight then performed our usual death-wish dash (try saying that after a few negroni cocktails), across the road, dragging two suitcases so fast they nearly burst into flames with the friction. The traffic seemed worse than in Rome. Cars did not so much as stop at the pedestrian crossings as slow down to within an inch of your flesh before speeding off again.
Grand Europa was round the corner so after hoisting our luggage into the small reception area it appeared happily, that I had already paid, so we were shown to our room by the concierge via a maze of corridors. Our room nestled in the corner of the warren.
I photographed some of the famous Naples-yellow painted buildings. As mentioned, crossing the roads in Rome took a certain amount of bravery and not a little faith in humanity. Some drivers looked as though they would love to take out a few pedestrians. Nevertheless we took that risk several times and managed to arrive in a student hangout palazzo relatively unharmed and ordered a Peroni from a side cafe. It had started drizzling a little, so everything was cool and damp. Refreshed we continued walking through the narrow cobblestone lanes, between high-rises in various stages of disrepair. Lines of washing flapped overhead between buildings like rows of beheaded ghouls. I marvelled at how Italians were able to park their Fiats so close together that the only way they could have vacated their vehicles was to slither out of the windows and across the roofs of the other cars as though devoid of a skeleton.
 It was really difficult trying to sprint across the busy roads with the suitcases after leaving the hostel and several times I felt the whistle of hot air as another vehicle narrowly missed hitting me. 
 After twelve stops on the train, we got to Vesuvius, Pompeii. I'd wanted to visit since I read about it as a teenager so was very excited to be there. A maze of dark cobblestones wound its way around the ruins of what was once a thriving village before Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD. During the excavations in Pompeii, the remains of over one thousand victims of the eruption have been found. During the first phase of the eruption, those who couldn’t leave the city in time were trapped in their homes or shelters, buried by a shower of pumice stones and lapilli (small lightweight volcanic stones and ash) or killed by the roofs and walls collapsing under the weight of falling volcanic debris which reached about three metres in height. Afterwards, a high-temperature pyroclastic flow hit the city at high speed and filled all the spots not yet engulfed by other volcanic materials, so that anyone still in the city died at once of thermal shock. The bodies of these victims remained in the same position as when the flow of ash hit them and as they were covered in calcified layers of ash, the form of their bodies was preserved even after the bodies decomposed. Over a hundred casts have been made by pouring plaster into cavities left by the decomposing bodies in the volcanic ash.
Wandering around the dusty rooms housing amphoras with pointed bases, wooden carts and of course, the grotesque ‘bodies’ of people captured in the final throes of a suffocating death was fascinating. The artefacts were left intact, old fountains — long since dried of any water — were distributed throughout the area. An extensive water system existed in the days prior to the eruption. The remains of wealthy family homes with beautifully intact painted frescoes featured with faded mosaic floors, bakeries, wineries and tree-lined avenues at the top of the village with a maze of lanes in between the houses. It was like getting a snapshot of life from those times. Columns with traces of marble at their bases, intricately patterned furniture, bird baths and statues. After climbing stone stairs to the top we looked out over the village remains and the surrounding countryside, presided over by the errant Vesuvius.
Olive trees lined the streets and cast a little shade. Skinks and lizards ran up and down the rocks in search of heat. We found crypts and patterned floors and walls, more mosaics and circular auditoriums that echoed with the sound of ancient plays as we walked the entire village eventually making our way down to the entrance again.

The narrow road clung to the cliff’s edge that fell away to the sea below. When the bus pulled up in Ravello we disembarked but had to wait for yet another bus to take us to Furore and our next accommodation. I hailed a taxi and the seven-minute journey cost €45 but I was past caring. We were quickly deposited on the side of the narrow road at Epoi. Our B&B was across the road, so negotiating the never-ending traffic to the other side was nerve-wracking. A young Italian woman welcomed us and showed us to our room. A pretty green terrace sat above the rooms where we had a coffee.
The weather was good and the view spectacular, with a royal blue sea and white-sailed yachts bobbing on the ocean below. A short while later, it suddenly bucketed down with rain. Tim and I set off in the deluge with cars, buses, and lorries whizzing by as we squelched our way into Minori, down some steep stone steps. At a seaside restaurant the waitress quickly whipped up a table and some cushions for our chairs. She shook hands with us in welcome, which was rather quaint. We ordered a buffalo cheese salad. Under the light of the half-moon, we returned to the B&B.
Although the bed was comfortable, I couldn’t sleep and tossed and turned my sheets into a tornado by morning and woke with a start to find Tim peering down at me with a concerned expression. Apparently I was shouting, ‘Fire!’.
Tim and I wandered down to the harbour to enquire about  hiring a boat for the next day then ambled around the town square of Minori, walked up some side streets before coming to a small museum of Roman artefacts. We found a guide to explain the Villa Romana e Antiquarium to us. This ancient site dates back to the first century. The ancient Roman villa is considered to have been the residence of a high-ranking member of the imperial court. The rooms contain some mosaics and paintings with a viridarium, or garden and a large swimming pool surrounded by rooms. The guide was very informative and earned his €3 tip. His phone went off a few times and was answered with a brusque, ‘Pronto!’ and peppered with ‘prego’s’. I thought he was hungry, but Tim reminded me that one is a greeting and the other, ‘please’.
Later that afternoon we took a bus to the gardens in Ravello and stopped at a nearby cafe to order coffee and a pistachio milkshake from one of those unsmiling waiters whose sole purpose is to get the maximum tip from minimum effort, so after asking for the bill twice we almost missed the bus. Thus began what should have been a pleasant trip through beautiful scenery, but turned into forty minutes of horrendous close shaves, near misses and swerving madness that typifies Italian driving. Narrow, winding streets snaked their way up the mountainside. The driver had probably been given burnt spaghetti by his wife because he was in a fierce mood, shouting at all the other drivers even though, seconds before he was the one swerving around tight corners with no thought as to what might be coming.
The views were beautiful though; a Mediterranean blue sea and old buildings. I took several shaky photos as we swerved around yet another narrow lane into oncoming traffic with the bus driver cursing loudly before arriving at the top of the mountain.
While passing a small wild garden I noticed around twelve stray cats. They’d been given a plates of spaghetti to eat. I couldn’t believe they’d eat pasta but one rotund tabby was doing just that.
Juicy (her name tag was prominently displayed over ample bosom), served us breakfast and our table overlooked the valley. The weather was warm with no sign of imminent rain, so we hired a boat for an hour. The owner gave us a crash course on how to drive the boat and Tim seemed confident so we waded into the blue sea and hopped on board. Tim navigated with ease and we set off around the coast. I was pleased we'd taken the opportunity before the long trip back to Rome. I bought tickets to Salerno and we arrived in Roma with crowds of people milling around.
I trotted down to the brightly coloured breakfast nook and ordered an Italian coffee. My only other companion was a lady of some advanced years with a shock of snowy white hair. She was wearing frilly white socks that looked like crumpled toilet paper. Tim and I returned to the same place we'd been before the Amalfi coast for coffee. The owner burst into song when I went to pay, which was both disconcerting and quite pleasant though I did wonder if I’d overpaid him.
I photographed all the old buildings and graffiti, before another view of the Colosseum came into view. We were ambling slowly up a path, enjoying the gardens and the brassy sounds of a trumpet, when I suddenly remembered we were supposed to check out of our room by 11 a.m.- it was already ten past. We were a long way from the hostel. So we picked up speed and ran back to The Beehive in the heat, huffed and puffed to the other side and arrived at reception, sweating and glowing with exertion at ten to 12. My first thought was to tell the receptionist that we were captured by Italian Ninja Turtles and I started to do so, then decided to come clean at the last minute and told her we were chased by rabid dogs and needed anti-tetanus injections. Humour seemed to pay off; she proffered a glimmer of a smile and said Italian Ninja Turtles would not have captured us as they would be too busy smoking and drinking. She didn’t say anything about the rabid dogs, but I don’t think she bought my story.
While sitting at the platform awaiting the train, Tim turned to me and idly asked if I could remember what time the plane was leaving for Amsterdam. I said I thought it was 4 p.m. and checked. The ticket stated that 3 p.m. was boarding time.

Suddenly we were panicking again. Would we make it on time? Yes, we had bought an express ticket to Fiumicino airport. So when we took our seats, we sat back confidently, preparing for the scenery to whizz past the window. All I can say is we would have got there faster if we'd hopped to the airport on one leg. We exchanged worried glances and became more agitated as the train slowly rumbled past the countryside as though on a Sunday stroll.
‘If we miss this flight, I miss seeing M (his friend), who's  driving for two hours to meet us,’ said Tim desperately.
‘And then we miss our hotel booking and the subsequent connecting flight to Cape Town,’ I finished.
We both sat forward, willing the train to go faster. If anything, it seemed to slow down as if to say, ‘Look at those cute sheep over there!’. It was already twenty to two and no sign of the airport. Ten minutes later, I saw a sign that said ‘Airport Ahead’. We gathered up our straining bags with our equally straining backs and both stood impatiently, waiting for the doors to slide open, then walked as fast as we could with our bulging luggage to the airport terminus.
‘Let’s hope we don’t need to walk to the furthest terminal,’I muttered unhelpfully.
I looked up at the board displaying flights and gates. Yup, the plane for Amsterdam was taking off from gate 1 – the absolute furthest point from where we now stood. Tim hoisted his bag further up his shoulder and we rushed up long corridors, along escalators and down stairs. It seemed to go on forever but eventually we arrived at the departure area. I let a few choice expletives rent the air as I hobbled down towards the desk past busy check-ins with people spilling out all over the place. We were both sweating profusely and of a panicky disposition — actually only I was. Tim was his usual unruffled self. Having checked in, there were actually fifteen minutes to look for coffee. My tongue was cleaving to the roof of my mouth. Tim nipped into the loo. The coffees scalded my clenched fingers slowly and painfully with each step. I couldn’t find Tim and panicked that I’d got the wrong gate so with fingers turning a deep red, I wandered around, looking in desperation for him and clutching the boiling liquids which continued to slop over my digits. I hurried, scalding my tongue on hot coffee as I tried sipping as much of the liquid as I could. Just as someone knocked past me, spilling more boiling coffee over my red and swollen fingers, I spotted Tim who ambled over, took one look at my third-degree burns, and quickly removed the offending cups.
‘Where were you!’ I shouted through swollen blistering lips.
‘I saw you but you just looked straight through me,’ answered Tim calmly taking another sip.
A sign at the gate said, ‘Athens’. I was worried we’d got the wrong gate, wishing at this point I could just curl up on the airport floor and wake up in Holland. As we waited in line for our row to be called, I held up my burnt fingers, blowing ineffectually on them.
Just then a loud high-pitched yapping came from behind us. I turned round to find an old woman wearing a woolly hat and a brightly coloured jersey. She had a huge cloth handbag from which appeared a small white head, yapping its distress at being bundled into her bag. The little white terrier did not let up and yapped persistently. Tim and I exchanged glances, could this woman and her dog be flying with us? A baby on the opposite side started crying loudly. Okay, so we had a yapping dog and a crying baby, no need to panic.

Tim laughed, wouldn’t it be funny if they were flying with us and we had them on either side; a horrible stereo. I smiled and said life wouldn’t be so cruel. The call for   boarding had us all shuffling forward with a little more speed. It was fifteen minutes late.
We found our seats and sat down with a sigh, I could relax for the first time since morning. A few minutes later the sound of a terrified dog rent the air and the terrier, who was indeed seated in front of us, started yapping and simultaneously the baby who was seated behind us started wailing. We couldn’t believe our bad luck and I immediately vowed to order two bottles of wine. As the plane took off, the yapping increased and didn’t stop once the entire journey; only the intensity changed. So we sat miserably with a yapping dog, a wailing baby and when the intercom crackled to life and a barrage of Italian filled the cabin, it was a veritable cacophony of noise. We laughed at the absurdity then Tim clapped his noise-cancelling headphones firmly over his head while I shoved a crumpled piece of toilet paper into my ears. The poor traumatised terrier didn’t stop till touchdown. It really should have been given a tranquilliser (well if not the dog, then definitely me). One got the impression there were more than a few passengers who would happily have placed a pillow over its little muzzle ever so gently. The owner seemed oblivious to the sound and was gently snoring with her trembling basket on her lap.
