# Chapter 33: ITALY 2014

I thought Italy would be a lovely place to go during the final throws of a divorce. There are a few hectic weeks prior to leaving as it was a last minute thing; a bit like the divorce itself. One minute we were married, next we were parting ways, taking me quite by surprise. Tim is in the middle of university exams and no-one is sleeping much, least of all myself. I’m still reeling from the untimely death of my dear mum and subsequent changes to the house as John and his carer came to live with me. Shortly after that one of our three dogs, Shadow dies after falling in my small pond. To say I’ve been stressed is to make light of my disposition. Today sees me tearing around after seeing my ex off to work with his sandwiches and yoghurt, (some habits take a little time to change) and Col is still living at home until he can buy a house for himself and his girlfriend. Nelly, my indispensable home executive, messages me to say her father has been killed and her Gran a few hours later from the shock. I send her some taxi money so she can go to the Eastern Cape for the funerals. The ‘we’ is going to be my darling boy and I. The taxi arrives a few minutes early so we have extra time at the airport to stop at The Mug and Bean and drink endless cups of coffee while catching up. He isn’t taking the divorce well and is still in the angry stages. I’m at great pains to portion no blame. As they say in the movies, ‘Shit happens’ and we remain good friends (Col that is, I’ll always be best friends with Tim). Just before leaving, a message comes in from Col wishing us both a safe flight. The train from Fiumicino will take us straight into the centre of Rome. I gaze out of the window and see lot of old crumbling buildings, as in poor state of repair, rather than historically old. Also a lot of graffiti. We arrive at the termini and stagger with our heavy backpacks and suitcases towards the backpackers called The Beehive. It turns out we’re at the wrong end of the station so have to turn and walk back to where we’ve just come from. I crane my neck at all the ruins that are already visible just outside the station. An old fashioned lift creaks its way upwards to the fifth floor where our room is situated. At the door, I begin frantically searching for the keys which we’ve just been given at reception. I spill the contents of my rucksack all over the floor but can’t find them. So I run down the five flights of stairs again to see if I’ve dropped them en route. Back at the top stair, I sit down, wheezing with exhaustion. I’m out of breath and red with exertion and heat. Tim looks anxious till I regain my breath and we both look through my rucksack again and there they are smiling malevolently up at us from the dark depths of my rucksack. Tim wisely says nothing, just opens the door which leads to a small square hallway. A few old green trams trundle by as we’re waiting.It turns out there’s no difficulty finding an outside restaurant as there are many and this one has a small area next to the tables filled with plants that is both visually pleasing and somewhat cooling. It’s a good place to sit people watching for a while and getting used to the idea that we’re in Italy. Our Indian waiter doesn’t allow us to finish our sentences, finishing them for us instead which is both amusing and annoying, especially when he gets it wrong. 

Tim: ‘Hmm, I’ll have the garlic and…' 
Waiter: ‘Aubergine al gricia!' 
Tim: ‘Er, no pizza el pollo' 
Me: ‘I’ll have the spaghetti …’ 
Waiter: ‘Prego! Spaghetti Puttanesca.’ 
Me: ‘Um, no, the spaghetti carbonara.’ 

Afterwards we take a short walk, passing a huge green hued, copper statue of a priest with a long flowing ‘cloak’. Tim says it had once been a fully rounded cloak where children could hide but this was considered un-PC, so they’d chopped a part of it away. Sad to have to desecrate statues because of a small percentage of sick humans but here we are. This morning I get up to make some more black coffee and sit drinking it at the small formica table that faces the open window from which only the grooves of buildings are visible. Today we plan to visit the Colosseum on Palestine hill. It’s the largest amphitheatre ever built. As we arrive at the entrance to the Forum and the Arco de Constantino, I stop to gaze at the beautiful carved relic from from 2nd century Ancient Rome which was dedicated to the emperor Constantine the Great. Thin slices of red bricks mark out the Forum as we walk past to the Colosseum. Unfortunately for us, they are busy renovating it. It’s an interesting experience into some of the lives of Ancient Rome. Along the wide open pavement there are buskers singing, horse and carts, pavement artists, a few mimes. There are a number of large hairy males dressed up in red togas, sandals and helmets who also pose with tourists for a fee. Grassy patches lie in between the ruins and only two columns with the original marble at the base. The entire forum is built on top of older palaces and courtyards. Crumbling dusty rooms belonging to Augustine overlooking Cirus Maximus. The reason for so much frantic renovation is to later mark the bi-millennium of Emperor Augustine’s death (19th August 14 AD). We walk through the ‘Audience’ chamber, a huge room designed for meeting with the populace. The House of Augustus is very simple and austere compared with the rest of the palace. I read that it's estimated there are more than 120,000 stray cats in Rome and protecting them has been tradition since the days of the ancient Roman Empire. Historically they were considered sacred to the goddess Diana and were kept as a way to control the pest population as the city grew as well as having them as pets for companionship. I like them even more after hearing that. This morning I awaken to the sound of rain - lots of it. I run to the window and open the shutters and take in a deep breath of fresh cool air before making a pot of coffee. Afterward breakfast we head for the Vatican. Walking over the bridge that crosses the River Tiber while the weather alternates with sun and soft rain. It’s a lovely walk and when we get to the entrance to the Vatican, a man comes up to us and introduces himself as the, ‘best guide with the best deal’. The tour starts at 2pm so we wait at a nearby cafe, drinking expensive bottled water and bickering about the cost of the tour. At the appointed hour we join the tour group where an affable guy called Paulo whose jeans pocket bears the legend, ‘sex and great jeans’, greets us warmly. His English isn’t great; garbled in fact and he uses the word,’actually’ with gay abandon. He turns out to be our guide which doesn’t bode well. We’re joined by two British girls wearing Islamic garb, a young Irish lad and a middle aged couple from Israel. The small group listens to Paulo’s strangled explanation of Leonardo’s painting on the Sistine chapel in his tortured English and liberally peppered with ‘actually’s’. After almost an hour outside, it’s time to ‘actually' enter the Vatican and the Sistine Chapel. It’s difficult trying to hear what’s being said while following Paulo’s disappearing figure holding a purple umbrella upright between numerous other tours and the general crowd. Paulo leads us through long and short corridors, up and down stairs, past statues, paintings and items of such sumptuousness of colour that it’s hard to take in. Old tapestries cover the walls with frail and faded beauty, gold painted friezes glow in the dim light. The group follows Paulo through the dome and the Basilica, past more mosaics and water features and huge paintings depicting simple Greek design vs Leonardos realism. Roman marble busts of Emperors, intricately painted friezes shine with gold paint, huge black marble baths from Egypt sit motionless, awaiting naked royalty to gently lower themselves into its smooth, cool depths. I’m almost hysterical with laughter at one point as we’ve lost the group and are frantically trying to find Paulo among hundreds of other tour groups when suddenly we hear a loud, ‘Actually,’ and know we’ve found him. We pass huge rooms housing famous paintings by Dali, Matisse, and Leonardo and I’m excited to see these famous works of art in person ‘actually’ we want to stop and look at them properly, but Paulo is tired by now, his English becoming more garbled and less coherent, so he hurries us along. The Sistine Chapel is a little disappointing – as recognisable as it is, it seems to lack the sumptuous splendour of the previous rooms and there are so many rules: no photography, no talking, and no loitering, so we’re ushered along quickly so that the hordes behind us can also have a look. I feel it kind of defeats the purpose somewhat. Paulo leaves us all rather abruptly having collected his fee outside the Basilica, and tells us to look inside and hurries off towards the exit, probably towards a large glass of vin rouge. We dutifully troop inside, but by now my legs are weak with aching feet. It is a beautiful building, also full of colour and expensive gold, but I’m too tired to appreciate it fully, so don’t stay too long and trudge back towards the station, stopping briefly at a restaurant to have a look at the menu. I would like to say their fare is mouth-watering, but it is the prices that are eye-watering, so we beat a hasty retreat. Down a few more side streets until we find a suitable cafe, both groaning gratefully as we sink into our seats. Even Tim's legs are hurting but more due to his previously injured leg than the tour. Col sends me a message saying he can’t get the TV sound to work and I have to laugh. Despite the late night, I am awake early and boil some water in the microwave for coffee and drink at the table while watching the city awaken. A few workmen begin their work before the heat of the day suffocates the city. As I watch, I muse that it all looks so familiar yet simultaneously so different from every place I have been to. Our plan is to take the metro to Naples today and stay over there for one night. As we wander the Via Cavour looking for a place for breakfast, a small cafe declares rather boldly that it sells the ‘best’ coffee in Rome, so we decide to give it a try. I get stuck in the loo because the light goes out and I keep locking rather than unlocking the door in the dark. ’What took you so long?’ he enquires, not unreasonably as he’d heard me say that I was going to pay a good forty minutes ago. After packing we walk in the warm sunshine with our bags to Termini station and buy two tickets to Napoli Centrale. As the train arrives, we rush to get a window seat. Unfortunately, the train fills up fast, and soon Tim is squashed up against a large, buxom woman with an enormous bust. He tries his best to keep his six-foot frame within his seat, but too much of hers is spilling over into his space. We both gaze fixedly out of the large window. Urban sprawl soon gives way to verdant countryside. Despite the many grape vines, it is not a very pretty area at all. We arrive at Napoli and exit the cool confines of the train into the intense heat to stand blinking in the sunlight then perform our usual death-wish dash ( try saying that after a few negroni cocktails), across the road, dragging two suitcases so fast they nearly burst into flames with the friction. The traffic seems worse than in Rome. Cars do not so much as stop at the pedestrian crossings as slow down to within an inch of your flesh before speeding off again. Grand Europa is round the corner so after hoisting our luggage into the small reception area it appears, happily, that I have already paid, so we are shown to our room by the concierge via a maze of corridors. Our room nestles in the corner of the warren. I photograph some of the famous Naples-yellow painted buildings. As we have already experienced, crossing the roads in Rome takes a certain amount of bravery and not a little faith in humanity. Some drivers look as though they would love to take out a few pedestrians. Nevertheless, we take that risk several times and manage to arrive in a student hangout palazzo relatively unharmed and order a Peroni from a side cafe. It has started drizzling a little, so everything is cool and damp. Refreshed we continue walking through the narrow cobblestone lanes, between high-rises in various stages of disrepair. Washing flaps overhead like so many beheaded ghouls, hung out on lines between buildings. I marvel at how the Italians are able to park their Fiats so close together so that the only way they could have left their vehicles is to slither out of the windows and across the roofs of the other cars as though devoid of a skeleton. After paying we leave the hostel. It is really difficult trying to sprint across the busy roads with the suitcases and several times I feel the whistle of hot air as another vehicle narrowly misses hitting me. After twelve stops on the train, we get to Vesuvius, Pompeii. I have wanted to visit since I read about it as a teenager so I’m very excited to be here. A maze of dark cobblestones winds its way around the ruins of what was once a thriving village before Vesuvius erupted in 79 AD. During the excavations in Pompeii, the remains of over one thousand victims of the eruption have been found. During the first phase of the eruption, those who couldn’t leave the city in time were trapped in their homes or shelters, buried by a shower of pumice stones and lapilii (small lightweight volcanic stones and ash) or killed by the roofs and walls collapsing under the weight of falling volcanic debris, which reached about three metres in height. Of these victims, only the bones have been found. Afterwards, a high-temperature pyroclastic flow hit the city at high speed and filled all the spots not yet engulfed by other volcanic materials, so that anybody still in the city died at once of thermal shock. The bodies of these victims remained in the same position as when the flow of ash hit them and as they’re covered in calcified layers of ash, the form of their bodies was preserved even after the bodies decompose. Over a hundred casts have been made by pouring plaster into cavities left by the decomposing bodies in the volcanic ash. Wandering around the dusty rooms housing amphoras with pointed bases, wooden carts and of course, the grotesque ‘bodies’ of people captured in the final throes of a suffocating death is fascinating. The artefacts are left intact, old fountains—long since dried of any water are distributed throughout the area. An extensive water system existed in the days prior to the eruption. The remains of wealthy family homes with beautifully intact painted frescoes feature with faded mosaic floors, bakeries, wineries and tree-lined avenues at the top of the village with a maze of lanes in-between the houses. It’s like getting a snapshot of life from those times. Columns with traces of marble at their bases, intricately patterned furniture, bird baths, and statues. After climbing stone stairs to the top we look out over the village remains and the surrounding countryside, presided over by the errant Vesuvius. Olive trees line the streets and cast a little shade. Skinks and lizards run up and down the rocks in search of heat. We find crypts and patterned floors and walls, more mosaics and circular auditoriums that echo with the sound of ancient plays and we walk the entire village eventually making our way down to the entrance again. The narrow road clings to the cliff's edge that falls away to the sea below. When the bus pulls up in Ravello we disembark but have to wait for yet another bus to take us to Furore and our accommodation. I decide to hail a tax and the seven-minute journey costs €45 but I’m past caring. We’re quickly deposited on the side of the narrow road at Epoi Ravello. Our B&B is across the road, so negotiating the never-ending traffic to cross over is nerve wracking. A young Italian woman welcomes us warmly and shows us to our room. A pretty green terrace sits above the rooms where we have a coffee. The weather is still lovely and the view is spectacular, with a royal blue sea and white-sailed yachts bobbing on the ocean below. It suddenly buckets down with rain. Tim and I set off in the deluge with cars, buses, and lorries whizzing by as we squelch our way into Minori, down some steep stone steps. At a seaside restaurant, the waitress quickly whips up a table and some cushions for our chairs. She shakes hands with us in welcome, which is rather quaint. We order a buffalo cheese salad. Under the light of the half-moon, we return to the B&B. Although the bed is comfortable, I can’t sleep and toss and turn my sheets into a tornado by morning and wake with a start to find Tim peering down at me with a concerned expression. Apparently I was shouting, ‘Fire!’. Tim and I wander down to the harbour to enquire about taking out, or rather hiring, a boat and book one for tomorrow then amble around the town square of Minori, walk up some side streets, and come to a small museum of Roman artefacts. We pop in and spend an interesting half an hour there. A bit later we find a guide who explains the Villa Romana e Antiquarium to us. This ancient site dates back to the first century. It is an ancient Roman villa that is considered to have been the residence of a high-ranking member of the imperial court. The rooms contain some mosaics and paintings, with a viridarium, or garden, and a large swimming pool surrounded by rooms. The guide is very interesting and informative and earns his €3 tip. His phone goes off a few times and is answered with a brusque, ‘Pronto!’ and peppered with ‘prego’s’. I think he is hungry, but Tim reminds me that one is a greeting and the other, ‘please’. Later this afternoon we take a bus to the gardens in Ravello and stop at a nearby cafe to order coffee and a pistachio milkshake from one of those unsmiling waiters whose sole purpose is to get the maximum tip from minimum effort, so after asking for the bill twice, we almost miss the bus. Thus begins what should have been a pleasant trip through beautiful scenery, but turns into forty minutes of horrendous close shaves, near misses, and swerving madness that typifies Italian driving. Narrow, winding streets snake their way up the mountainside. The driver has probably been given burnt spaghetti by his wife because he is in a fierce mood, shouting at all the other drivers even though, seconds before, he was the one swerving around tight corners with no thought as to what might be coming. The views are beautiful though: a Mediterranean blue sea and old and interesting buildings. I take several shaky photos as we swerve around yet another narrow lane into oncoming traffic with the bus driver cursing loudly and arrive at the top of the mountain. While passing a small garden I notice around twelve stray cats. The‘ve been given a plate of spaghetti to eat. I can’t believe they’d eat pasta but one rotund tabby is doing just that. Juicy (her name tag is prominently displayed over ample bosom), serves us breakfast and our table overlooks the valley. The weather is warm with no sign of imminent rain, so we hire a boat for an hour. The owner shows gives us a crash course on how to drive the boat, and Tim seems confident so we wade in the blue sea and hop on board. Tim navigates with ease, so we set off around the coast. I’m pleased we have taken the opportunity before the long trip back to Rome. At the bus terminus I buy tickets to Salerno and we arrive in Roma with crowds of people milling around. I trot down to the brightly coloured breakfast nook and order an Italian coffee My only other companion is a lady of some advanced years with a shock of snowy white hair. She is wearing frilly white socks that look like crumpled toilet paper. Tim and I return to the same place we went to before the Amalfi coast for coffee. The owner bursts into song when I go to pay, which is both disconcerting and quite pleasant, though I wonder momentarily if I’ve overpaid him. I photograph all the old buildings and graffiti, before another view of the Colosseum comes into view. We are ambling slowly up a path, enjoying the gardens and listening to a man playing the trumpet, when I suddenly remember we are supposed to check out of our room by 11 a.m., and it is already ten past. We are a long way from the hostel. So we pick up speed and run back to The Beehive in the heat, huff and puff to the other side and arrive at reception, sweating and glowing red with exertion at ten to 12. My first thought is to tell the receptionist that we were captured by Italian Ninja Turtles, and I start to do so, but I decide to come clean at the last minute and tell her we were chased by rabid dogs and needed anti tetanus injections. Humour seems to pay off; she proffers a glimmer of a smile and says Italian Ninja Turtles would not have captured us as they would be too busy smoking and drinking. She doesn’t say anything about the rabid dogs, but I don’t think she buys my story. While sitting at the platform awaiting the train, Tim turns to me and idly asks if I can remember what time the plane is leaving for Amsterdam. I tell him I think it is around 4 p.m. but I check. The ticket confidently states that 3 p.m. is boarding time. Suddenly we are panicking again. Will we make it on time? Yes, we have bought an express ticket to Fiumicino airport. So when we take our seats, we sit back confidently, preparing for the scenery to whizz past the window. All I can say is we would have got there faster if we hopped to the airport on one leg. We exchange worried glances and become more agitated as the train slowly rumbles past the countryside as though on a Sunday outing. 'If we miss this flight, I miss seeing M (his friend), who is driving for two hours to meet us,' says Tim desperately. 'And then we miss our hotel booking and the subsequent connecting flight to Cape Town,’ I finish. We both sit forward, willing the train to go faster. If anything, it seems to slow down as if to say, ‘Look at those cute sheep over there!’. It is already twenty to two and no sign of the airport. Ten minutes later, I see a sign that says 'Airport Ahead'. We gather up our straining bags with our equally straining backs and both stand impatiently, waiting for the doors to slide open, then walk as fast as we can with our bulging luggage to the airport terminus. 'Let’s hope we don’t need to walk to the furthest terminal,’ I mutter unhelpfully. I look up at the board displaying flights and gates. Yup, the plane for Amsterdam is taking off from gate 1 – the absolute furthest point from where we now stand. Tim hoists his bag further up his shoulder and we rush up long corridors, along escalators. It seems to go on forever but eventually arrive at the departure area. I let a few choice expletives rent the air as I hobble down towards the desk past busy check-ins with people spilling out all over the place. We are both sweating profusely, red in the face, and of a panicky disposition - actually only I am. Tim is his usual unruffled self. Having checked in, there’s actually fifteen minutes to look for coffee. My tongue is cleaving to the roof of my mouth. Tim nips into the loo. The coffees scald my clenched fingers slowly and painfully with each step. I can’t find Tim and panic that I've got the wrong gate, so with my fingers turning a deep red, I wander around, looking in desperation for him clutching the boiling liquids, which slop over my digits. I hurry along, scalding my tongue on the hot coffee as I try to sip as much of the liquid as I can. Just as someone knocks past me, spilling more boiling coffee over my red and swollen fingers, I spot Tim, who ambles over, takes one look at my third-degree burns, and removes the offending cups. ‘Where were you!’ I shout through swollen blistering lips. ‘I saw you but you just looked straight through me,’ answers Tim calmly. A sign at the gate says, ‘Athens’ I’m worried we’ve got the wrong gate and wishing at this point I could just curl up on the airport floor and wake up in Holland. As we wait in line for our row to be called, I’m holding my burnt fingers up and blowing ineffectually on them. Just then a loud high pitched yapping emanates from behind us. I turn round to find an old woman wearing a wooly hat and a brightly coloured jersey. She has a huge handbag from which appears a small white head, yapping its distress at being bundled into her bag. The little white terrier does not let up and yaps persistently. Tim and I exchange glances, could this woman and her dog be flying with us? A baby on the opposite side starts crying loudly. Okay, so we have a yapping dog and a crying baby, no need to panic. Tim laughs, wouldn’t it be funny if they’re flying with us and we have them on either side of us, a horrible stereo. I tell him life wouldn’t be so cruel. The call for us to begin boarding has us all shuffling forward with a little more speed. It’s fifteen minutes late. We find our seats and sit down with a sigh, I can relax for the first time since this morning. A few minutes later the sound of a terrified dog rents the air and the terrier, who is indeed seated in front of us starts yapping and simultaneously the baby who is seated behind us starts wailing. We can’t believe our bad luck and I immediately vow to order two bottles of wine. As the plane takes off, the yapping increases and doesn’t stop once the entire journey; only the intensity changes. So we sit miserably with a yapping dog, a wailing baby, and when the intercom crackles to life and a barrage of Italian fills the cabin. It’s a veritable cacophony of noise. We laugh at the absurdity but then Tim claps his noise-cancelling headphones firmly over his head while I shove a crumpled piece of toilet paper into my ears. The poor traumatised terrier doesn’t stop till touchdown. It really should have been given a tranquilliser ( well if not the dog, then definitely me). One gets the impression there are more than a few passengers who would happily place a pillow over its little muzzle and press ever so gently. The owner seems oblivious to the sound and is gently snoring with her trembling basket on her lap.
