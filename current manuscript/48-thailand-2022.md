# Chapter 49: THAILAND 2022

My alarm brings  me out of an interesting dream where I’m giving a lecture to a room full of eighty year olds. None of them have teeth! Knew I shouldn’t have eaten that last piece of cheddar.

I have a cold shower and tear my nose ring out of position while rubbing soap out of my eyes and step out of the shower looking like a scene from the New York massacre with blood flowing all over my face and body. I manage to reposition the ring but my schnozzle is pretty sore and red. I’m flying to Thailand and no injured nostril will remove the frisson of excitement I’m feeling at the prospect.
After rushing downstairs to feed Fish and Chips, I bend down to shovel pellets into their bowls and hear a tearing sound; the crotch of my cotton Harem pants has taken the full brunt of my ample buttocks. Cursing, I run back upstairs, grab a mirror and shove it underneath. The upside down view shows a small tear but there’s absolutely no time to change before my Uber arrives so I leave the tear, vowing to keep an eye on it and rush around grabbing last minute essentials, slurping tea from my big mug when I hear the doorbell. Mr Uber is early. I grab my huge suitcase (why oh why can’t I learn to pack lightly), shove my equally heavy backpack over my back, give both kitties a squish and open the gate.

‘ Hello, I am Remote!’ Shouts the Uber driver over the gate.
‘ Yes, I have a remote.’ I shout back and he looks puzzled.
 
I close the gate and wheel the case towards the back of the car, lift the case into the open boot and hear another tear in my nether regions. My electric gate has already closed so I have no choice but to climb into the back and chat to the driver who has repeated that his name is ‘Remote’ from Zimbabwe. We chat about the traffic, Zimbabwe and some politics, as one does. 
Thanks to Remote, I’m early for the flight but there are lines snaking all the way round the lower floor from the Qatar Airways desk, because naturally only one is open. I go for a large mug of coffee and return to the line which is only a modicum shorter and chat to two Afrikaans speaking ladies in the queue behind me. One is flying and is very nervous and I promise her friend I’ll look after her in Doha. They look after my case while I rush to the loo. I discover the tear in my crotch is now about 5cm wide, so I affect a tight shuffle with my thighs pressed together and hope no one can see my underwear. After just over an hour of shuffling forward it’s my turn to approach the desk. I move carefully forward and lift my suitcase onto the scale. I hear another tear in my pants. At this rate I’ll be wearing a ‘skirt' by the time I get to Doha. My passport is handed back so I take it and tie my cardigan as low around my hips as it can go and adopt a strange shuffle towards the X-ray section. 

I have two empty seats next to me after boarding and I’m really hoping it stays that way but at the last minute I notice a couple approaching with that rapid eyeball swivel that indicates they’re looking for seat numbers. 

‘Please don’t sit here, please don’t sit here’, I silently plead but the god of the airways sniggers and the woman bends down and says, 

‘ Hello, these are our seats', with a nod and a smile. I smile back weakly and remove my hand bag from her seat.

The rest of the flight passes uneventfully, except that every time I get up and stretch over their legs to go to the loo, my trousers tear a little more. The entire area is now open and flapping and there’s not a thing I can do except tie my jersey even lower on my hips and try not to make any sudden movements.
We arrive in Doha and I look around for the Afrikaans tannie I promised to look after but can't see her anywhere. I hope she’s okay and not wandering around lost luggage. I head to the transit lounge. At the x-ray machine, I lift my things extra slowly onto the conveyer belt in order to preserve what’s left of my harem pants and public decency. Something beeps as I walk through the body scanner. It always does despite removing everything but my tooth filling.

I shuffle back in the ‘hands up or I’ll shoot position’ and it beeps again. I then have to endure the embarrassment of security running their mobile scanner up and down my body. There’s a pause around crotch level but I maintain a steady focus ahead and I’m waved on.

I’m booked into a sleeping pod as my next flight is early tomorrow morning. As time has moved on without my knowledge, it is now 10pm so I begin my search for the pods. It takes me nearly an hour to locate them and suffice to say it is far from where we landed. It is now 11pm. I’m shown to my pod by a tall man in a white jacket with a gravy stain on the sleeve. I ask him for water but they only have that ghastly sugary ‘7 Up’. 
There’s a thin blanket in the pod that covers only half my body and a hard pillow. I quickly charge my phone, locate my emergency sewing kit and set about sewing up the tear. I’m finished by midnight as the pants have indeed obtained a skirt-like appearance by now. I try to sleep but toss and turn for ages. It’s chilly in my container and with a blanket no bigger than a face cloth, I can’t get warm. I find my airways socks - those horrible machine knitted things that look like a grey tube with no heel or toe section. It helps though and I eventually doze off.

My alarm shrills me awake at 5:30am and I put on the newly sewn pants and stumble to the bathroom.
My flight is in block B and as with last night, no one seems to know where that is, so I wander around trying not to panic as every letter in the alphabet is on the overhead board except B. Eventually I find someone who says I need to take the airport shuttle to another building.
We board half an hour later than stipulated and have just been given lunch; for some reason my cheese omelette is a complete square.
We arrive in Thailand much later than expected and after the long wait for customs and luggage retrieval I wander outside with my outsized suitcase to hail a taxi. A British man sees me standing and tells me I have to grab a ticket first. After the usual haggle with various taxis, I take one that will deposit me at the ‘Don Muong Airport hotel’ which means I’ll be at the right airport for my flight tomorrow to Chiang mai.
The hotel receptionist is rude, unhelpful, monosyllabic and abrupt and after taking my passport, shoves a key into my hand and waves somewhere in the direction of my room. Why do I always get the rude staff I wonder; I’m always extra polite to them.
I locate the room and find it is basic but nice. The air-con works which is great because Thailand is not at all wintery! I have a small bar fridge and a kettle and set about making a nice cuppa using the small carton of milk and teabags I brought with me from Cape Town. I lie on the bed cooling off and sipping tea, absentmindedly pulling the small fine hairs around my upper lip until it forms a red, patchy moustache.

BANGKOK

I hop out of bed full of the joys of spring and make myself some tea and look outside the hotel window at passing planes taking off from nearby DMK airport. The sunrise is painting yellow and lilac hues across the sky. It is already hot and humid even at this early hour and I contemplate my continuous choice of destination. 
After a cold shower I go down to reception to enquire about breakfast. Not that I’m hungry but it will help pass the time. Unfortunately the rude receptionist is still here, her mask perched defiantly over her nose and mouth so that everything she says is a mumble. I ask her if breakfast is included and she says,’Yes’. I attempt to extract more information by asking where and she points vaguely outside so I wander outside and find a room that could pass for a dining room if you squint a bit, but it’s closed! So not a cuppa or a bun in sight. 
I translate my query into Thai as to where and when I might be able to break my fast. The ‘friendly’ receptionist looks up briefly and says, 

‘No brehfas.' 

So that’s that then. At least I’ve managed to waste half an hour. I wander back to my room and send a few messages, make another cup of tea. I’m starting to feel really tired ( jet lagged obviously) but have to stay awake if I’m to sleep tonight. I decide to go for a walk around the local area, stepping over cracked, uneven pavements where small tufts of bright green grass grows between the concrete, detritus of a crumbling city. It’s hot and humid but bearable. I ask ‘happy’ at the front desk to order me a taxi. It costs a hundred Thai Baht but the airport is only 3km away I discover as five minutes after climbing into the taxi, I’m climbing out again.

DMK is one of the oldest international airports in the world, operating since 1914. It is small but has a few restaurants so will be ok for the short time I have until my flight to Chaing Mai.
I  find a side kiosk in the airport to buy another pair of Thai pants to replace my torn ones. 
The lovely Thai lady that sells them to me says she likes the flower in my hair. I tell her it’s my trademark but she looks puzzled, so I just add a ‘khaapkhunka’ (thank you) and leave clutching my new pants. 

My seat is at the front of the small Air Asia plane which takes only 45 minutes before landing. As we disembark, it already feels cooler than Bangkok. I wait for my suitcase to arrive and wheel it outside to wait for Tim who has taken a Grab to get here. It’s so nice to see my tall, handsome boy again. We find another taxi to take me to my hotel, a short distance from the apartment building that Tim is staying in. 
I set off for Tim’s place after my tea. A small coffee kiosk is open outside his apartment. It is square shaped with a low wall covered in plants. It’s cozy and welcoming so I stop and order a black coffee. Tim comes down a short while later and we head off in search of breakfast and find a spot called Roast8ry for coffee and they have oat milk for me (they pronounce it, ’Oh meeoo’). 

PAI

We’re off to Pai today so I pack my foldable travel kettle and mini fridge (can sized) in my rucksack (I travel with these Temu acquired items to third world countries because without my morning cuppa, there is no holiday). Tim joins me and we go for coffee, this time with ‘rye meeoo’ (rice milk). The driver of the van has long hair and wears an earring in one ear. He takes us to some friends of Tim’s.  A petite girl with blond hair comes out and introduces herself as Laura. She’s Canadian and a travel blogger. The other dark haired girl Tania is quiet and somewhat sullen but she greets me and climbs aboard the
van. We head to another place to pick up Martin who’s from Northern Ireland and from all accounts, fond of sleeping late. He keeps us waiting for ten minutes but soon we are off towards Pai which is north west of Chiang Mai. It sits in a valley on the banks of the Pai River and is known for it's nearby gorges, hot springs such as the Tha Pai Hot Spring and waterfalls. 

There’s a lot of banter in the van, especially between Martin and Tim. The road takes us up into the mountainside and becomes very twisty. We have another break halfway up at a restaurant with a lovely view of the valley. Pai is also known for its rice paddies and is close to the Myanmar border. I can feel it is much cooler here which is good for me. After finding our Hostel; Cha-Li’s we wander into the restaurant area which is designed a bit like old American milk bar. There’s a king size bed in our room and another canopy bed overhead which Tim claims. We walk down with the others to look for scooters to hire and find a place nearby. A tall serious looking man with a dark pony-tail shows us the scooters he has available and Tim chooses a big black and silver model and takes it for a quick spin. Mr Ponytail is ponderous and meticulous which bodes well for the condition of the bike. 

En route we stop off to see the huge white Buddha on the mountainside which sits at the top of the wide, white painted steps; 353 in total. It’s a bit of a climb in the heat but I make it up glowing with exertion. The Buddha itself is magnificent, an enigmatic smile plays on its large white stone lips and he sits crosslegged on his white stone throne. We have to remove our shoes before stepping onto the area where he sits. 
A  notice requests tourists to, ’please dress politely’ so I’m really annoyed to see two young women arrive wearing shorts and a crop top. I glare at them but they’re too busy taking a thousand selfies, rather than photograph, or better still, look at the Bhudda or the beauty below. I’m disgusted at this disrespect for the culture and it annoys me so much I stand with my hands on my hips and stare at them from close up which only makes me look suspect. The rest of us walk around the base marvelling at the artistry.
Back on the bikes we head on to the canyon. A paved stairway here culminates in an elevated lookout over high rock cliffs and the Pai valley. 
The paths are twisty geological formations carved out of the ochre sandy rock, they are very narrow in places and drop steeply into the canyon below. I venture as far as I feel safe to do so without losing my footing. Tim and the others climb the entire canyon paths. There are quite a few tourists here so at times the narrow pathways are crowded to the point where you have to just patiently wait for the path to clear. Tim and I climb over a short section and sit on a rough bleached-wood bench watching the sunset deepen and colour the canyon in reds and yellows. We leave just as it’s getting dark and climb onto our respective bikes heading towards the night market back in town. 
At the market, four long intersections have been closed to traffic and each road is lined with tables and kiosks selling everything from food to clothing. Aside from Western type food, there’s sushi, dried fish, sweet potato, grilled black sticky rice with a sesame seed topping (which I have wrapped in a banana leaf), fried chicken, Octopus, noodles in bowls fashioned from banana leaf, and various meats cooked over simple coal stoves. For those for whom normal food is passé,  a variety of other ‘delicacies’ are available such as live shrimp, red ants and their eggs served on a bed of lettuce, chai Khao which is cooked baby ducklings still in the egg, an assortment of fried insects such as grasshoppers, worms, beetles sautéed in a little soy sauce, raw veal in congealed blood, chicken feet, fried frogs on a skewer, ditto bamboo rats, and huge blackened scorpions on a stick. I dry heave a few times on my way down the middle of the market.
Tania is meeting a friend from Vietnam who’d recommended a place down a side street for traditional Thai food. We walk there through the throngs of people and find an middle aged Thai man sitting in a small kiosk frying noodles. There’s a wooden table in front of him, so we seat ourselves and chat to her friend who has joined us. I have a huge plate of chicken and fried noodles which is delicious. We leave around 9pm and walk back to the hotel.. For my evening entertainment, a singer with a terrible voice lets rip across the road accompanied by his guitar. He keeps me awake till well after midnight.

‘Get off quickly mother!

It looks like a small bear to me so I’m not surprised to discover it’s also known as a bearcat. It has big pale whiskers and a thick tail. A sweet young pigtailed Thai girl feeds an elephant carrots under the careful watch of her father. We are exhausted by the time we’ve traversed the entire zoo so hail another red taxi van to take us to meet Martin and Tania at a Korean restaurant. The Korean waiter leads us inside to sit at a long wooden table with a shallow depression down the middle. This where the meat is barbecued by another waiter. It’s a nice authentic touch. I’m planning a trip to Korea soon so this is a fun taste of Korean history.

The interior is sumptuous with its rich gold, silver and opulent jewels. We spend nearly an hour there then a short while later stop at ‘Eat Phra Sing’, another beautiful gold temple. King Mahidol gave the temple status when he declared it a royal temple in 1935. There are huge Bhudda statues inside and a section of the old red brick city wall outside from when Chaing Mai was the capitol of Thailand. We have a roasted coconut rice and mango and a foot massage as one does. After chatting with the Dutch owner, we sit in a beautiful plant filled courtyard for our massages while seated on comfortable chairs. 
Following the advice of the locals at the temple, we pop into the office of a small middle aged Thai woman and ask her about booking a visit to the elephant sanctuary. She’s quite amusing and chatters continuously, pausing occasionally to say how handsome Tim is then telling me I’m beautiful and ‘her idol’! All around are bushes of colour and it’s quite breathtaking. Two German girls already seated in the back of the truck on one of the metal benches that is taking us to the elephant sanctuary. Three more people, from  Mexican are collected a short while later. Alessandro and his sister Mariana and a school friend. They are a lot of fun. Alessandro regales us with amusing stories and we’re all laughing as we bump our way over the dirt road to the sanctuary.After turning off at another muddy dirt track, we come to the Elephant sanctuary located next to the Mai Ping river. The guide leads us to a long wooden trestle table where a row of bowls are filled with various substances. Pink lanterns adorn the upper beams. He tells us that we are to make up a ‘banana loaf’ of medicines for the elephants. The bowls of tamarind paste, ripe bananas and salt are mushed together in a pestle and roughly shaped into blocks. In several other buckets are pieces of sugar cane, more bananas and long green cucumbers. A nearby enclosure close to the river houses six elephants. The mahout or whatever the Thai equivalent is called, calls the elephants who announce their arrival with loud trumpeting. There are three large adults, two medium and one teenager. 
We all stand somewhat nervously with our buckets of food and watch as the elephants speed up towards us. They know we have food and are excited. Each one greedily pushes forward towards a bucket. Their long trunks feel inside the buckets for a delicacy with one fingerlike tip, (in contrast to African elephants who have two ‘fingers’ or lips). So, the Asian species wrap their trunk around a food item and squeeze it into its mouth, rather than grasping with the tip. I look over at Tim and find him surrounded by elephants. Two have wrapped their trunks around his waist while a third has her trunk firmly in the bucket. He sees me and smiles before thrusting a cucumber at one. Smiles and laughter from the group as we feed the greedy pachyderms.
Meal time is followed by a mud bath in a nearby pond. Each of us are given a large scrubbing brush and told to find an elephant, scoop up mud, smear it over them and scrub with the hard bristled brushes. I squelch my way into the pond, mid-thigh deep, find an elephant and set about smearing mud over its rough, hairy, grey hide. Our small group smear mud over themselves as well. The guide takes photos of us then approaches me and starts drawing on my back. I ask what he’s drawing and he takes a photo. I have to laugh, he’s drawn quite an accurate rendition of an elephant pooping using a twig and thick mud as paint. 
Next, we’re called over to the river to wash the mud off the elephants and ourselves of course. This part is fun too, scooping water up in cupped hands and washing off as much mud as we can off their hairy backs. One of the elephants has consumed too much roughage because he drops a few large balls of dung into the water. The guide calls us to stand together in the water for a few photographs surrounded by elephants and floating balls of dung. 
Showering takes place under an ingenious set-up nearby. A row of thick metal pipes are hung under an roof and a plastic bottle has been cut and attached to the end of each pipe. Holes in the base of the plastic bottles allow water to shower down after opening a simple attachment. While we were washing the elephants, a simple lunch was prepared for us and this is now set up on a small narrow table. Stir fried vegetables, rice, pieces of omelet, chicken and watermelon. Later the guide takes us through the forest down to a small but powerful waterfall called the Mai Wang waterfall.

This morning we take a taxi to take us to ‘Art in Paradise 3D’ museum. I’m excited for this. After paying at reception we begin an amazing tour of interactive features. Bicycles that you climb on but aren’t real, Statues painted so lifelike you can ‘walk around' them, you can sit in bubbles, become part of oil paintings, meet dragons, ‘walk tight ropes’ ‘hang’ from trees, ‘swim’ with dolphins etc. Eventually we make our way to their cafe which is also a work of art; everything in the room has been painted to look like a cartoon café. It is so effective that you sit at tables that come out of a comic book. Luckily the drinks and a brownie we order are edible. I muse that they must have used thousands of litres of paint.  A morning well spent I think as we climb into a taxi to Kai Soy Local for supper.

We arrive in Krisdadoi resort, Ban Pong which is set in the mountains and is really beautiful. Its original Swiss-styled chalets remains well maintained amidst the mind-blowing scenery of floral gardens which are a seasonal inspiration. It’s a refreshing oasis as the garden also has many water features amid the colourful flowers. Christmas music is piped through various speakers around the garden.
After our awful Christmas meal last night, we aren’t expecting much at breakfast and this proves to be the case and we end up eating only half of it before asking reception to call us a cab. When it arrives, Tim asks the  female driver if she will make some stops en route so we can see a little more of the area. She agrees and stops at a temple called, ‘Nong Kwai’. It is an old wooden temple, very simply furnished inside with wooden beams and painted red and gold. Hang Dong is a small rustic village area with a garden enclosed with a low wooden gate. There's a small shop and a café where we share a slice of chocolate cake and a lemon slice. 

Today is my last day in Chiang Mai. I’m up at 5:30, finish packing then wait outside  for the company bus to fetch me and Tim for our last excursion. The bus arrives and we find some seats at the back. No one seems very friendly despite smiling at them so we just sit back and look out of the window. After forty minutes, we stop for a toilet break at ‘Ueang Fa’ market in Wiang Pa Pat. There’s a natural hot spring contained in a round stone wall here and several hawkers are selling fresh raw eggs that you dip into the bubbling water to boil. We buy an egg each which come pre-packed in small, locally woven bamboo baskets. Each basket features a long, looped handle.
After dropping them into the bubbling steam for a few minutes. They come out hard boiled and tasting a bit of sulphur.
I find the public toilet, it is the squat variety. I use every ounce of thigh muscle to remain in an upright, hovering position while holding my white trouser legs up from the wet floor, simultaneously holding my backpack. My legs are like jelly as I walk back to the bus and my right trouser leg has a large damp spot that I’m not prepared to investigate too closely. After another hour, we stop at the White Temple which is on the outskirts of Chiang Rai. It is breathtaking! Pure opulence in pristine white marble that glows against the clear blue sky. It’s almost magical. Like a giant Christmas cake covered in bright, sparkling icing. There’s a moat around the main temple and the magnificent building is reflected in the still water. Gold fish swim along the moat and water features spout fountains of water into the warm air which adds to the fairytale experience. As you walk along the narrow pathway to the entrance, in contrast to the beauty around you, there are multiple carved hands reaching out as though drowning and begging you to save them. A few appear to almost grasp at my ankles. As we wander around, mouths open in amazement at the sparkling white stone and mosaics, it’s almost too much to take in. Intricate carvings around the entrance features faces and bodies of deities. We stay for forty minutes and are totally enamoured of the experience. After clambering on board the bus, we head for the next temple, ‘Wat Rong Khun’ This one is painted gold and is called the Dhamma Building. It is much smaller than the nearby White Temple, but is equally as sumptuous and opulent. The gold painted structure stands out because of its colour and is actually the public toilets building.
We set off again and stop for lunch in a typical Thai open air restaurant. Long wooden trestle tables with benches are crowded together. The place is teeming with people and sweating waitresses rush around with trays of food. One Thai woman stands screeching orders at the servers and strides around collecting plates, all the while ordering others around in shrill Thai. They bring us bowls of some unidentifiable soup with suspicious floating grey matter. I am unable to bring my lips to the soup and the chicken and rice look equally ‘sus’. I sit chatting to the others at our table. Aside from the family from Singapore, the others aren’t very friendly. It may be a language thing though. 
Our next port of call is called ‘The Blue temple’ and as to be expected this is because it is very blue. Wat Rong Sura Ten is a modern structure - built to replace an old abandoned temple. The entire building is a rich, deep blue with gold paint added in places as decoration. Huge mythical beasts rise out of the low walls on either side. Inside is also predominantly blue and gold. The ceiling is also magnificent with blue and gold inlay and intricately patterned. A huge glowing white Buddha sits bathed in blue light at the back of the temple. I think these three temples have been some of the best I have ever seen with perhaps the famous Taj Mahal taking top position. Our next and second last port of call is the Baan Dam Museum on the border between Thailand and Myanmar (Burma). This is a private art museum open to the public. It’s also known as the ‘Black temple’ by visitors, perhaps after viewing the White, Gold and Blue temples, the natural inclination is to name it with a colour. The buildings are indeed a dark wood colour. Inside the traditional looking temple is a collection of taxidermied animals. Forty buildings make up the museum with the temple as the biggest. The other small buildings are dotted around a large garden area and make for some interesting viewing with buffalo horns, animal skins, monuments, and futuristic domes. This museum showcases the Thai artist, Duchanee’s collection of sometimes controversial art. The toilet for example is decorated with an array of wooden phalluses.
Our  last visit is to ‘Kayen Village’ to see the long neck tribe (Kayan Lahwi). The Padung, as they call themselves, fled Myanmar or Burma in the 1980’s to escape the civil war raging in their country. Their tradition dictates that from as early as five years old, girls are given brass rings to wear around their necks. These rings are replaced as they age until their necks are abnormally long. This is achieved because the pressure of the heavy rings on their shoulders and rib cage, forces down and thus makes the neck appear longer. I hear that local government made a deal with these refugees so that they have a safe place to live as long as they allow being photographed by the many curious tourists. I remember seeing photographs of their long necks in old National Geographic magazines as a child and I’m hesitant at first, feeling the whole thing borders on turning them into circus exhibits but I notice they are selling goods and I want to support them. There are numerous stalls inside the village and I immediately notice all the beautiful women and children with their distinctive copper neck rings. I will only photograph those whose produce I have bought and only if they are amenable to being photographed first. There are few sweet young girls wearing two bands. Some of the women are very beautiful and colourful in their traditional dress. It’s with mixed feelings that I leave the village with a handful of crafts. We all board the bus and set off back to Chiang Mai and arrive back at 7pm. 

BANGKOK

Tim joins me and we head to the airport. There’s a beautiful sunrise. We don’t wait long to board the small plane. In Bangkok airport, I collect my suitcase and get a taxi to the hostel (Poshotel) which is an hours ride into a suburb of Bangkok. My room is small but comfortable with a green painted verandah. We leave my luggage in the room and go for some coffee. It is definitely hotter and more humid here than in Chiang Mai. The canal route is good for breakfast before walking to the Royal Palace ( also known as the Grand palace). It  is made up of a complex of buildings and is the official residence of the Thai Kings since late 1700’s. 

The Wat Phra Chetuphon is a glorious mix of colourful tiles and mosaics. One of the buildings has a mock up of a typical bedroom in the palace with wooden walls and floor with a narrow bed. We venture on to see the Giant Lying Buddha. You can only see parts of him at a time by walking the length of the room. Another tourist takes a photo of Tim and I at mid stomach area. The feet are as big as several men lying end to end and all in glowing gold. There’s an intricate inlay of ivory on the soles of his feet. 
A busy open air restaurant next to the canal serves us cashew chicken and rice, then we walk to the ‘red light’ district of Bangkok afterwards to see some 'lady boys’. I’m curious as I’ve heard they look amazing dressed up in their finery. Noisy bars with loud music blare onto the pavements, scantily dressed women (or maybe men) walk up and down looking for tourists. A sign offers, ‘Grilled alligator and charred scorpions’ to nibble on. I’ve eaten crocodile before in East Africa but decide to pass on the scorpions. 
We decide to have an impromptu leg massage on the side of a busy market area. As our respective limbs are massaged and oiled, we gaze around at the crowds of people. They end the massage with a quick prod to the neck area.  A tuk-tuk takes us to the other side of town. Lots of brightly coloured lights, men and women of the night, food on sticks and loud music. It’s quite atmospheric.

After checking out back at the hostel I say goodbye to Tim who is taking a Bolt back to Chiang Mai. We hug tightly and he leaves. I decide to have another thirty minute massage, more to pass the time than any real need to be prodded again. 
Along the Bang Lamp canal I notice a huge monitor lizard waddling out of the water to sun itself on a metal beam. They are carnivores and eat a wide range of foods including fish, frogs, rodents, birds, crabs, and snakes. This one looked to be around half a metre in length.

I sit next to a Hungarian girl on the plane who is supposed to start work at a new job tomorrow. 
We watch the safety demo, fasten seatbelts, the plane engine starts and I settle down to watch a movie on the screen about ‘Elvis’. 
Imagine my surprise when, hours into the movie, the pilot interrupts to apologise for the delay in take off due to ‘technical difficulties’! I honestly thought we’d been airborne for at least two hours. We wait another hour on the runway with my companion becoming more and more agitated as she will miss her connecting flight. We eventually take off after a three hour delay. Service takes ages and just as we are about to be served at the back, the pilot asks all airline staff to take to their seats due to turbulence.  I find a packet of salted nuts and give them to my ‘hangry’ companion. We land in Doha at 3:30 am and I wish my new friend luck with her job and wander off to look for my sleeping pod.
My companion to Cape Town is Mary, a 55 year old woman who is returning home after a stint doing caring work in the UK. She says she’s very hungry so feeling a sense of deja vu I rustle around my backpack and find another packet of nuts ( note to self; always bring extra supply of nuts for hungry passengers).
