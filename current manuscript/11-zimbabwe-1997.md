# Chapter 9: Zimbabwe 1997

We were driving all the way up to Zimbabwe; a journey of some 2,452 kilometres from Cape Town. It was bitterly cold and misty when we set off in the early hours; the Cape mountains were wearing thick caps of winter snow and the surrounding vegetation looked dry and brittle. Recent photographs of the planet Mars sent back by NASA showed a suspiciously similar landscape to the Karoo— bare, brown scrub stretching for uninterrupted miles. Several food and loo stops later, I phoned Mum from Col’s Nokia cell phone. I marvelled at the sheer wonder of technology, little knowing how all-invasive this particular miracle would become in later years. Col’s pathology partner Paul had suggested he take the phone along for emergencies and Col had only complied because he mistakenly believed it wouldn't transmit from Zimbabwe. We had just crossed the Orange River; the longest river in Southern Africa with a total length of 2,200 kilometres. It’s colonial name was bestowed in honour of Prince William of Orange in Holland. We were trying to make Kroonstad by nightfall. It was a bit hairy driving in the fading twilight alongside massive pantechnicons, heavy lorries and other motorists who were all equally intent on reaching their destinations before it became too dark to see. It was therefore with a great sense of relief that we finally reached Winburg and located the hotel. Our room was basic and lacked an en-suite bathroom, but we were exhausted, so it was perfectly doable. Our travel alarm clock rang in the new day and we awakened stiffly, hurrying to dress in the very chilly light of dawn. This morning we ventured down to the communal bathroom for another hit-and-miss affair with the taps which required a specific degree of brute strength to open; if you weren't careful, the water hit you full in the face. I got hit; shocked into instant alertness with the powerful spurt of ice-cold water. Johannesburg was every bit as awful as I remembered it—flat, brown, dusty, with a myriad of vehicles zooming around in a frantic hurry. The urban smog was thick and smelly, encouraging a bout of coughing. The roadside was heavily littered with plastic bags and other debris. The landscape became significantly more interesting after we bypassed Pretoria. Tim, who was always so adept at entertaining himself on long journeys, alternated between snoozing and reading his comic books in the back. 
The countryside Zimbabwe was absolutely lovely, dominated by the red and orange hues of the autumn msasa trees and magnificent, sprawling baobabs. While driving directly towards the setting sun, a massive transport truck suddenly materialised out of nowhere on the wrong side of the road, forcing Col to swerve violently to the left. It missed our wing mirror by mere centimetres. The sudden rush of extra adrenaline must have helped because we managed to make it to the Gwayi River by 6 p.m. It was freezing cold in the early hours of the morning despite all the blankets we had piled on top of us. One easily forgets that despite the African location, the high altitude above sea level means Zimbabwean winters are incredibly chilly. The bathroom taps were so ancient that when we opened them, the water came out with a violent gush of black iron filings. My toothbrush was instantly stained an unappetising, rusty brown colour. After a refreshing glass of Mazoe orange juice, we set off for Hwange National Park, which is the largest natural reserve in Zimbabwe. It is around 14,600 square kilometres in area and boasts a spectacular variety of wildlife; we were both excited to be back. After entering the park gates, we spotted our very first elephant standing not far from the track. 

We unpacked our gear quickly and set off for a quick evening game drive. The park roads were dusty, winding through woody vegetation, open scrub, and thick acacia thickets. We spotted kudu, hyenas, giraffes, zebras and many beautiful birds, including bee-eaters, glossy starlings, a magnificent kori bustard and some secretary birds. We set off for an early game drive the next morning. It is usually the best time to see nocturnal predators as they make their way back to their dens to sleep. It was so cold in the vehicle that I wrapped one of Tim’s spare T-shirts over my bare feet to keep them warm. Our patience paid off; we spotted scarlet-chested and lilac-breasted rollers, elephants in both small family units and larger breeding herds, secretary birds, giraffes, and zebras. It was quite a long game drive, proving to be a matter of either feast or famine in terms of game viewing. After a quick stop at one of the natural pans, a massive group of grumpy looking buffalo wallowed in the afternoon light. Red-billed ox peckers hung on various parts of their heavy bodies, casually munching on ectoparasites and ticks, occasionally picking at small wounds. Col had used up nearly all our bread rolls to feed the smaller birds around the camp so he could secure a good close-up photograph. I only hoped the birds appreciated it, because we would have absolutely nothing left for lunch at this rate. He even had me boiling potatoes for them at lunchtime. I muttered to him that I would draw up a formal restaurant menu for the local avian population next time we visited. After a magnificent morning of game viewing, we logged sightings of giraffes, warthogs, a black-backed jackal, bat-eared foxes, baboons, zebras, crocodiles basking in one of the pans, kudu, buffalo, blue wildebeest, impala, and steenbok. The birdlife was equally spectacular: herons, ducks, a marsh owl, starlings, purple and lilac-breasted rollers, yellow-billed hornbills, plovers, oxpeckers, long-tailed shrikes, a lappet-faced vulture, a bateleur eagle, secretary birds, pale chanting goshawks, sandgrouse, francolins, and guineafowl. 
Later, Col was spotted in various hunched, uncomfortable positions around the campsite grounds, trying desperately to photograph the elusive crimson-breasted shrike. The sun kept darting in and out of the thick clouds. He used up three bread rolls and a slightly green potato as bait before finally getting a decent shot. As we approached another large pan, we noticed a lone bull elephant having a swim. Col and I were photographing him when suddenly, he lurched his massive frame out of the water and headed straight for our vehicle, irritably flicking his tail. He took a terrifying mock charge towards us. As he ears spread out to charge a second time, we decided to vacate the area in case the next charge proved to be a real one. Col had to reverse the car quickly, scattering gravel and red dust into the air as he went. He continued to reverse wildly down the small pathway with the angry bull trumpeting his displeasure behind us. We parked and watched again from a respectable distance. It was a beautiful evening, with the setting sun reflecting gold over the pan water while silhouetted elephants drank and frolicked in the shallows. 

I awakened with a start from another one of my vivid, chloroquine-induced nightmares. Col left for the forest early, loaded with camera equipment and boiled potatoes in equal quantity to search for his beloved crimson-breasted shrike. I made sandwiches for our lunch and then packed the car to sardine capacity. God only knew what the family presents would look like by the time we finally reached Harare. As it was, we had already made severe inroads into Andy’s chocolates. Nay, we had totally polished them off. This was because Tim had inadvertently sat on top of the box earlier, squashing them far beyond the point of being ‘giftable’. I marvelled at the dramatic difference in the vegetation as we drove northwards towards the Sinamatella campsite. The typical scrubby Kalahari vegetation gradually transitioned into rocky granite kopjes and beautiful, tall mopane trees. Our cottage there overlooked a magnificent valley where we met the resident caretaker, Matheus, who informed me that the kitchen was currently overrun with mice. He proudly announced that he had successfully caught one and instructed me to let him know if I spotted any more. I was not at all happy about this piece of news and just hoped the little darlings would sense my natural animosity—read: absolute terror—and keep their distance. I possess an irrational fear of mice. I am not entirely sure why, but such is the nature of phobias; they don’t always make logical sense.
 
By the time I got my video camera ready, all I managed to film of the wildlife was the parting of the grass as it disappeared into the thicket. Suddenly, around a sharp bend, we came across a sparrowhawk guarding its fresh kill—a hapless pigeon on the side of the road. That, unfortunately, was the extent of our excitement; despite driving for a further two and a half hours with nary another animal in sight. The spectacular views of the escarpment made up for it though. Today, we were ready for a final night drive in the hope of spotting some nocturnal animals. However, the wildlife situation was just as dire as it had been the day before. We saw a couple of impala, a few elephants, and a tremendous number of helmeted guineafowl. It was therefore no surprise when reviewing our photos that we each possessed perfect front, back, side, and arse views of guineafowl, and not much else. We stopped at Robins Camp for lunch—a pretty campsite shaded by flowering mopane and msasa trees. I asked the waiter for ‘Chai mbili’ and was met with a completely blank look. I keep forgetting that we are not far enough north for the local people to understand Swahili. The drive to Vic Falls only took two hours, as the road surface was better than usual. Col proudly produced his old Zimbabwean identity card so that we could gain admission at the cheaper local rates. The falls were absolutely magnificent—a massive, roaring panorama of plunging waterfalls that caught the sunlight in rainbows and sent up a permanent shroud of thick spray. Col and I kept our camera lenses carefully covered most of the time. Tim ran up and down the pathways, becoming steadily more drenched by the mist. He confidently chatted to total strangers, telling them they really should get raincoats like his as he was completely dry. Col presented a highly interesting sight with his rucksack bulging with camera gear and tripods, whilst carrying a massive, bright orange underwater camera in his hands. This proved useful though, as he was able to take as many photographs as he wanted in the heavy spray without damaging the equipment. We walked around the cataracts, taking in the views of the falls from many spectacular angles.  As the day drew to a close, the mosquitoes came out in full force. Since it was the height of malaria season, we prudently returned to camp. Numerous curio craft shops abounded. The  problem being, while the wooden carvings are getting bigger and bigger, the depletion of the indigenous hardwood forests is increasing exponentially. It was quite sad to witness; you can understand the economic need to make money but at what ecological cost? Back in camp, we washed and dressed for supper held at the A’Zambezi River Lodge. A massive buffet was laid out on the open verandah and a group of marimba players entertained the guests on the periphery. Tim stared transfixed at these rustic wooden musical instruments and later wandered over to inspect one, to the delight of the musicians. A little girl of around six came over to chat with us about the music and I left her and Tim playing together while we went to fetch dessert. I looked back at the display a moment later and nearly choked on my crème brûlée; Tim and the young girl were in the middle of a ‘sword fight’ utilising long strands of thatch they had pulled from the carefully crafted construction on display. Although both appeared to be enjoying themselves, I had to intervene when they started yanking great handfuls of thatch from the exhibit. 
After morning tea we set off hopefully for an early game drive. For the first hour we saw absolutely nothing—not even a bird of interest—and were saddened to realise this must be due to heavy poaching in the area. Just as we were thinking of giving up and returning to camp via the Lunga loop, we came across a pack of African wild dogs. They are an incredibly rare sight, sitting on the endangered species list, so we were doubly excited to watch them. There were ten or eleven in the pack sloping casually down the middle of the sandy road track. We returned to camp making sure to inform the rangers where we had seen the ‘painted wolves’ as they are also known. 
En route to Kariba, Col decided to drop in on the Gorges Lodge to see if there were any magnificent birds of prey flying over the deep chasm below. It was a fantastic spot, built right over the sheer edge of the cliff and located only 11 kilometres from the main turn-off. The spectacular setting offered wide, panoramic views of the Zambezi gorge below, dotted with vegetation clinging to the rim. I purchased some cold drinks and was just raising the bottle to my mouth when a black eagle swooped from the sky above, soaring straight past our faces—so close you could see what it was thinking. We watched it soar effortlessly up and then plunge down again into the valley below. The guest rooms were perched precariously over the lip of the gorge with wooden verandahs. The hotel was part of the African Sun hotel group that we had first encountered back in Kenya. Part of their commercial proceeds goes directly to the local communities, similar to the operations at Rocktail Bay and the Maasai Mara. We decided to stay there next time we visited the country. We drove on towards Mlibizi, stopping briefly along the roadside to collect some mukhwas seeds. These are a mix of aromatic seeds and nuts, often including fennel, sesame, coriander, and sometimes shredded coconut, which is widely used as a digestive aid and mouth freshener after heavy meals. 
At 7:30 a.m. we stood at the jetty waiting for the arrival of the ferry, The Sea Lion. I discovered to my dismay that we should have packed an overnight bag as there would be no passenger access to our car once it was parked on board the vehicle deck. A frantic few minutes ensued as I stuffed clothes into bags. Tim and I boarded first and had the uncomfortable experience of watching Col reverse the car onto two incredibly narrow, flimsy metal planks bridging the gap to the deck. It was very pleasant on the ferry which was equipped with all the necessary amenities for the long crossing—a restaurant, clean toilets, private cabins, and comfortable seating on the upper deck. The trip was a 272-kilometre voyage along the entire length of Lake Kariba and we were scheduled to travel straight through the night, arriving in Kariba the following morning. I noted the location of a lifeboat and some shiny safety jackets should the need arise. Passengers could swim in the lake for a short while. The day passed slowly as the ferry cruised at a leisurely pace of around 10 knots. Tim found a little playmate called ‘French’. We thought this a strange name for a young boy until we realised he meant the boy came from France and spoke no English. Tim spoke even less French, but as is so often the case with young children, there are many non-verbal ways to communicate and they were soon happily building ‘houses’ out of the lounge cushions. The setting sun was beautiful and the rising moon spectacular. 
Dinner was served at 7 p.m., and we sat out on deck with a large man, his wife, and a young German couple. Tim found himself nestled comfortably in the ample confines of the wife’s lap for a while. Breakfast was served from 5:30 a.m. followed by a frantic packing of our scattered belongings before docking at the Kariba harbour. We watched cars and small trucks carefully reversing back down the flimsy-looking ramp onto the concrete dock until it was finally time for us to do the same. Col hired a motorboat for the day. Securing Petrus, our driver, plus a boat equipped with a canvas canopy cost a few hundred Rands. We zoomed out to Fothergill Island located about 45 minutes across the water. Hippos snorted and cavorted in the lake as Petrus anchored the boat near a dead tree stump and spent the next hour patiently showing Tim how to fish. With a good dollop of beginner's luck on his side, Tim reeled in a good-sized bream in less than three minutes. He was absolutely delighted and went on to catch a further ten fish. Petrus threw the under sized fish back into the water. I enjoyed sitting in the boat as it gently bobbed up and down with each small wave; the tranquil setting was incredibly peaceful. After some time Petrus moved the boat closer to the shoreline where a small herd of elephants stood munching on thick water hyacinths. It was a beautiful sight as the red-backed elephants— a result of an earlier mud bath in the Kalahari-style earth—chomped away against a backdrop of green-leafed bushes, purple hyacinths and yellow grass. Petrus guided the boat towards the harbour and later we set off for Harare. A large road sign along the highway warned motorists to ‘Beware of farm implements crossing’ and I immediately pictured a few large spades and a couple of rusty pitchforks casually strolling across the tarmac. 
We arrived at Cecilia and Andy’s house the following day and dished out the very crumpled presents, before Cecilia, Andy and the rest of us piled into their Land Cruiser for Bally Vaughan, a small game sanctuary nearby. President Bob Mugabe’s massive, palatial palace loomed as we approached the more affluent side of Harare. The law stated that no one is permitted to travel down that particular road between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m. Apparently, some unfortunate local man had cycled past recently after having a few drinks and was promptly arrested by the guard regiment. 

Col borrowed the kids' garden tent, wrapped a thick layer of rush matting around the structure for camouflage and set up camp inside the mulberry bush. All that was visible from the outside was a fraction of his camera lens poking through a small hole in the foliage. Later, Andy and Tim got the fright of their lives when they went to pick fresh mulberries and suddenly the bush spoke to them. Well, I say spoke, it was more of a muffled curse as they had inadvertently scared away a rare bird Col had been trying to photograph. Andy was telling us that despite the massive size of their grounds, the current municipal law dictated that no one may build an extra residential dwelling on their property without an immense amount of bureaucratic red tape. The clever way around this regulation is for residents to officially build ‘changing rooms’ for their swimming pools. Strangely enough, these changing rooms come complete with a bathroom and a fully equipped kitchen. 
We returned the Beitbridge border post later that week and endured the usual frustrating, drawn-out situation with the customs officials who heavily intimated we were attempting to illegally smuggle our car over the border. We eventually found a pretty guesthouse for the night and were warmly welcomed by Justice who showed us around the property. There were massive baobabs and beautifully flowering jacarandas growing in the garden. Across the border the surrounding geographic area became dry and flat. I knew I was going to be comparing parts of South Africa unfavourably to Zimbabwe for a little while to come, as I had loved the beauty of the country and the warmth of the people there. Aside from a few quick wee stops and grabbing snacks for the road we pushed on steadily until about 6 p.m. Near the Free State, the vegetation changed dramatically yet again and was now full of flat, vibrant yellow canola fields with dark blue mountains rising in the background. While driving through the middle of the arid land locked Karoo, Col spotted a cormorant and a seagull flying overhead; either we were severely sleep-deprived or those poor seabirds were way lost!
