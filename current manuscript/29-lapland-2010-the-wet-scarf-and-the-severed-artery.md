# Chapter 26: Lapland 2010: The Wet Scarf and the Severed Artery

It is a little difficult trying to logically pack your suitcases for an expedition to the Arctic Circle when the current temperature is a blistering 35°C back home in Cape Town, but I managed to locate a local Chinese shop that sold oversized, thick padded jackets and purchased one for each of us. They were full of deep utility pockets too, which I reasoned would be incredibly useful for stuffing in thick gloves and woolly hats. We departed for the airport terminal with plenty of time to spare. All went perfectly smoothly with our check-in, until Col suddenly realized he hadn't officially checked his camera bodies and accessories through customs control. He abruptly left Tim and me standing at the gate and dashed back to the counters. I honestly do not know why he insists on doing this, because in all the years I have known him, we have never once needed to declare his cameras upon our return to South Africa, but he is an incredibly careful chap, so off he went. We threw some hot coffee down our throats and ate a full bar of chocolate, which left the three of us with a severe sugar high. The Lufthansa ground crew informed our gate that there would be a delay in our take-off because they were still refuelling the wings. I was perfectly happy with that; they can refuel the aircraft as much as they like as far as I'm concerned. Inside the ladies' cloakroom, I somehow managed to accidentally flush half of my long winter scarf straight down the toilet bowl, and had to painstakingly extricate the sodden fabric using my eyebrow tweezers, washing it thoroughly in the sink afterwards using copious amounts of liquid hand soap. Consequently, I now possessed a freezing, damp neck. As I returned to the family pews with half a wet scarf trailing down my spine, I noticed that the rubber sole of one of my brand-new shoes was slowly parting company from its mother shoe. I flapped my way awkwardly to my seat and looked around the terminal shops for a place to buy some superglue. When I didn't see any hardware available, I simply ripped the sole completely off the shoe. I was now walking around the international terminal on thin, soleless leather with a long, damp patch trailing down my back. I am a proven danger to myself and quite honestly shouldn't be allowed out in public. After boarding the cabin twenty minutes later, I discovered to my absolute delight that we possessed a free seat on either side of our row—I mentally thanked Denise, our long-time, long-suffering travel agent, for her brilliance. There was a large stash of magazines packed inside the overhead locker above our heads, so I carefully tried to extricate one from the pile. The magazine slipped out of my fingers and clobbered poor Col straight on his forehead, after which his skin began to bleed profusely! I felt absolutely terrible and frantically asked a passing air hostess for some plasters. She returned to our row carrying a mini surgical pack. Col covered his head wound with the dressing and stared blankly ahead miserably for a while, milking the medical situation for as long as he humanly could. I handed him the remnants of our chocolate bar as a peace offering. An American couple were sitting directly next to our empty row seat. I asked them politely if it would be OK if our family utilised the space. They were perfectly happy to comply, until they watched me slowly fill the entire seat up with various travel paraphernalia, books, and bags until there was absolutely no room left for anything of theirs. The sun shone severely through the porthole windows until we were all sweating profusely. The flight featured at least five toddlers on board. It’s going to be a long, hot, noisy flight methinks, removing a few layers of clothing and placing them on the seat next to me. The American woman scowls crossly at my layers, and I smiled sweetly in return. It was exactly as I had feared: a loooong night punctuated by loud, agonizing wails from one of the children—actually, truth be told, that might well have been me. I watched three full movies throughout the night, although by ‘watching’, I mean my eyes were propped open and completely bloodshot with tiredness. To pass the time, I consumed two small bottles of airline wine and a Baileys on ice. Supper was a choice between beef or fish that had probably last seen the ocean when Adam was a lad. I opted for the beef. Col managed to snooze in his chair, and Tim lay sprawled right across his seat. I wriggle into several different physical positions under the blanket, but abandoned them just as quickly, getting up several times to refill my plastic glass with water from the galley station. Morning arrived with a fresh, loud bellow from two of the toddlers. Their parents were all sitting with that classic, glazed expression of someone who has reached their absolute psychological limit and are now channeling what little energy remains merely into breathing. I reached into my handbag for a small cosmetic packet and patted some cool gel around my tired eyes, but the liquid just dried to an unbecoming, tight glaze on my skin. Our breakfast trays were served: a very life-like omelette and a pale sausage, both of which proved completely inedible. I decided they had actually been stolen by the airline from a Japanese sampuru plastic food display factory before take-off! 

The Baton Gymnastics 

It was pitch dark, freezing cold, and powdery snow was falling softly outside Frankfurt Airport when we landed. A woman suddenly came walking up to me and asked if the black woollen scarf in her hand belonged to me. I nodded my head quickly and thanked her, only to find a few minutes later that I was still actively wearing my original scarf around my neck. Furthermore, I don't even own a black scarf—well, I do now. In fact, I now possessed two separate scarves. We shuffled tiredly towards passport control, then through the security X-ray machines, where my wristwatch and every single sore joint in my body lines set off the alarm. I was patted down several times by the guards after removing another layer from my person, and was forced to perform the requested gymnastics that always accompany these security occasions: spread your legs apart, now jump them back together with arms outstretched, now fling a leg up high and shake it all about. I was so utterly exhausted that I completely lost my physical balance and nearly clipped the security official straight on her jaw! The unsmiling security woman ran her electronic, beeping baton up and down my legs with grim, silent determination. She was clearly convinced she was going to locate that massive wad of cocaine I had hidden between my toes, come hell or high water. Eventually, she admitted defeat and shoved me to the side of the lane to re-attach all of my previously dismantled winter armour. I joined Col and Tim and we slouched over to the nearest terminal café, exchanging a pile of Euros for some awful coffee and a dry croissant. We sat in total silence, slurping our hot drinks surrounded by mounds of our luggage. The inherent problem with travelling to an Arctic country is that the volume of heavy clothing one has to travel with instantly doubles. Tim and I walked around the terminal concourse for a bit to stretch our legs and restore some blood flow to our calves. Our connecting flight was soon announced over the speakers, and soon our ‘Danke schöns’ transformed into Swedish ‘Tack så mycket’ (thank you very much). Our next travel frustration occurred when the automatic electronic ticket reader at the gate decided I simply didn't exist. It turned out the ground staff back at Cape Town Airport had accidentally printed two duplicate boarding passes for Tim, but absolutely nothing for my name! An official eventually shuffled over to help resolve the computer glitch. Tim had to sit two rows ahead of us on the aircraft, which I highly suspect didn't worry his heart too much. Gazing out of the window as we flew north, I could see there was a massive amount of snow blanketing the landscape below. I was only hoping the pilots had done their due diligence and cleared our destination runway properly. Suddenly, there was an announcement over the cabin intercom: they were waiting for one final passenger who had failed to board the plane, and they were now actively removing their luggage from the hold. Col and I exchanged worried glances, hoping fervently that the missing person wasn't me. Seeing as they hadn't officially printed my name on the ticket back in Cape Town, this was a distinct structural possibility. I mentally calculated how many ways I could wear what garments I currently had on my person, deciding that wearing my padded jacket completely backwards on day two might be a viable fashion option. Watch this space. 

Upplandsgatan and the Snug Apartment 

I looked out of the porthole as we came into our final landing at Arlanda Airport in Sweden. The snow lay thick and white on the ground. We endured a rather fast, incredibly bumpy landing on the tarmac, creating a few white knuckles among the passengers. After successfully collecting our massive mounds of luggage from the carousel, I tried to phone Anne-Sofie from my cellphone, but soon discovered it wasn't connected for network roaming in Sweden. Col tried to use a public phone box in the terminal, but the machine just swallowed all his coins, belches a few back into the tray, but flatly refused to connect the call. I spotted an information desk and walked purposefully towards the counter with a friendly smile plastered over my face. I was so tired it was probably far more of a grimace, but the nice man assisted us beautifully, and Col was soon chatting happily to his mother. We were soon hurtling towards her apartment in Upplandsgatan street in the back of a municipal taxi. The apartment was noticeably smaller than the previous one she had shared with Sven Erik (who has sadly passed away), but this new home was incredibly cosy. The rooms possessed a beautiful pinkish glow due to the wallpaper selection and the festive lights which adorned the apartment everywhere. The next day, we caught the metro train down to Gamla Stan (the Old Town) to watch the changing of the guard ceremony at the Royal Palace, arriving just a few minutes too late having missed most of the parade, and had to contend with seeing only the backs of the guards and the retreating bottoms of the horses. It was incredibly slippery walking on the icy cobblestones, and I found myself walking like a constipated tightrope walker just to avoid falling flat on my back. We visited the beautiful Stockholm Cathedral (Storkyrkan), which had been originally constructed back in 1279. The cathedral houses unique historic treasures, including the famous, monumental wooden sculpture of St George and the Dragon. Situated in the heart of the Old Town between the Royal Palace and Stortorget, the building's high turrets and beautiful stained-glass windows glowed magnificently in the dim winter light. 

Kiruna and the Twelve Yelping Huskies 

The taxi manoeuvred its way smoothly back to Arlanda Airport the next morning, heavily weighed down with our massive bags of ‘hand’ luggage—backpacks, jackets, heavy winter scarves, and gloves—to board the small Boeing 737 aircraft bound for Kiruna in Lapland. The flight crew announced that it would only remain light for about an hour and a half when we reached our destination at 1 p.m. It was apparently a very bearable -10°C today. The plane landed right on time with a bit of a heavy bump on the thick snow. Kiruna Airport is famously one of only five airports in the world that regularly allow commercial aircraft to land on a runway with thick snow pack, as the extreme latitude means they cannot physically keep a runway entirely free of snow for the eight and a half months of winter. We disembarked down the stairs straight into the lovely, silent world of Arctic snow. It lay thickly everywhere you looked, blanketing the landscape. A local man and woman were holding up a cardboard placard welcoming our family. After exchanging warm greetings, we were escorted to a long wooden locker room and handed a pair of bright blue, heavy snow overalls to wear, along with thick blue insulated gloves, heavy thermal boots, and woollen balaclavas. The seven of us were subsequently led outside to a narrow wooden sled waiting on the track. Thick reindeer pelts lined the wooden planks to soften the seats for our bottoms. Attached to the front of the sled with long leather harnesses were twelve yelping, frenzied huskies, raring to go. Our guide asked our group to sit tightly behind each other, and I was given the dubious position of occupying the very front seat of the sled. This position proved, let’s say, incredibly interesting as we set off suddenly after a loud exclamation of, ‘Hike!’ The huskies lunged forward, and we bumped aggressively against each other like clothes inside a tumble dryer. The exact second we set off down the track, several of the huskies simultaneously released their bowels directly into the wind, and unfortunately, due to my vulnerable position right in the front seat, a few errant faecal pellets hit me full force across my face! I had to cling onto the wooden railings for dear life, as these powerful dogs can run at a staggering speed of up to 50 km/h (30 mph). After spitting a cold particle of husky poo from my frozen lips, I managed to relax and look around in awe at the spectacular winter wonderland unfolding before our eyes. Thick, powdery snow glistens beautifully on the tops of the white spruce and Scots pine trees as we whizzed past the branches. The tireless huskies led our sled through narrow paths of thick snow drifts, wide open expanses, and small ice tunnels with total ease. Some sections of the ride were incredibly bumpy, especially when the sled came to a short rise and bumped heavily down the reverse slope. I was very glad of the thick insulated clothing that had been provided by the outfitters. It was exhilarating, and only my face felt the intense cold, as my woollen scarf had slipped down my neck and my face mask was a bit loose in the wind. After a fantastic hour and a half of sledding across the wilderness, we finally arrived at the Ice Palace, where we were scheduled to spend the night. The Arctic sky was slowly darkening, and our very first sight of the Ice Palace provided a wonderful, pale, gleaming entrance of solid ice, beautifully highlighted in green light emanating from nearby oil lanterns. 

The Ice Palace and the Wolf Paw 

The dog sled came to a standstill, and we disembarked stiffly onto the snowy walking path. What an incredible vision! Like a childhood fairy story come to life, the palace shone and glittered in icy splendour against the dark sky. A huge, shining ice column welcomed guests to the main entrance of the hotel, where we checked in at the desk. My eyes darted everywhere, taking in the many magnificent, intricately carved ice sculptures decorating the halls. The check-in counter was actually situated in a section of the ‘warm’ building, constructed from pale timber, which felt intensely hot—too warm, in fact, and I couldn't wait to remove some of my heavy layers. A short man dressed in leather trousers and a crisp white cotton shirt gave our family a brief tour of the facilities. He showed us our allocated lockers, where we stashed our suitcases and excess heavy clothing. Then, we met up with a few other international guests to hear a mandatory safety briefing inside one of the ice rooms. The manager informed our group that we could not take a single item of personal luggage into our designated ice rooms, and were permitted to sleep wearing nothing but our thermal underwear. We were instructed to collect our specialized, fleece-lined expedition sleeping bags from the reception desk right before bedtime. These bags, he explained proudly, would keep the human body perfectly warm in ambient temperatures as cold as -15°C; the actual temperature of the ice rooms was maintained strictly between -5°C and -8°C. He demonstrated exactly how to climb into the bags and zip the canvas up until only the nose was exposed to the air. He then took us on an extensive tour of all the ice sculpture suites, which were absolutely breathtaking. Each room he led us through featured a completely different artistic theme. We came to a suite containing a beautifully carved sports car, including all the intricate extras like rear-view mirrors and an ice licence plate! Entirely hand-carved from solid river ice, the sculptures were fantastic. One room featured a massive polar bear emerging from its ice cave, and sleigh beds constructed of solid ice topped with only reindeer pelts for covering. The tables and chairs were also fashioned from blocks of ice and were structurally strong enough to take an adult's full weight. We eventually came to an amazing ice church, complete with an ornate altar temple, ice pews, and a magnificent, massive ice chandelier that hung somewhat precariously from the frozen ceiling. It was truly an amazing experience. Our allocated bedroom featured three single beds of solid ice topped with a thick layer of reindeer pelts across the base; two ice chairs and a square ice table completed the sparse, frozen furnishings. Col, Tim, and I trooped off to visit the famous ‘Ice Bar’, which adjoined the main entrance to the palace. As you would expect, the entire bar was constructed from solid ice: ice counters, ice bar stools, ice tables, and a frozen shelf that held dozens of bottles of booze ranging from gin to schnapps. We ordered a round of drinks, and received the colourful liquor served inside ready-made glasses constructed entirely of solid ice! We carried our ice glasses over to a table. It felt surprisingly warm inside the lounge, although all the rooms are maintained strictly at a comfortable -5°C. A pretty blonde barmaid served us our choices of vodka, and I ordered a local cocktail called a ‘Wolf Paw’—which was essentially Swedish vodka mixed with tart cranberry juice. There are beautiful ice pictures carved directly into the walls, and dozens of lights set into frozen alcoves. Col eventually left our table to look for his mother, who was staying in a room inside the ‘warm’ timber section tonight. Our dinner was scheduled to be served at 10 p.m., so we possessed a lot of spare time. We eventually picked up Col from the Ice Bar, finding him chatting amiably to an Indian couple; he was currently on his fourth vodka and was feeling very merry indeed. After locating a few more intricately sculptured ice features in the corridors, we headed into the main dining room. The restaurant was full of pale wooden furniture and featured a huge traditional linen wall hanging. Candles burned everywhere on the tables, and the design theme was a beautiful white and silver colour palette. Our meal was spectacular: fresh reindeer steak, salmon, wood grouse, duck, seasonal vegetables, and a bottle of rich red wine. A five-star meal for a five-star Arctic experience! We left Anne-Sofie safely in her warm room next door, and prepared ourselves for our night inside the ‘fridge’. 

Red Noses in the Fridge 

After dressing in our matching thermal underwear for the night, Tim left for our room carrying his special expedition sleeping bag, and I kept Col company in the corridor while he waited for his bladder to fill up so he can empty it before bed. It is absolutely no fun getting up in the middle of an Arctic night for a wee when the room is freezing cold. The guest toilets were also located inside the warm wooden building next door, for obvious structural reasons. After successfully changing into our thermals, Col and I joined Tim inside our ice room. Luckily, the blocks are subtly lit from within so we could see exactly what we were doing. We removed our heavy fur boots very quickly at the foot of the bedstead, leaving the footwear on the frozen icy floor, then climbed into our sleeping bags and very quickly zipped the canvas over everything but our noses. I turned my head to look at Col and Tim next to me, and just saw two isolated red noses protruding from the nylon, which set me laughing hysterically into my hood. 

A Saami Experience 

I cannot say it was the absolute best night's sleep of my life, as the ice beds by necessity were a bit hard beneath the pelts, but it was definitely one of the most unique experiences we have ever had. A young hotel girl woke our room with a gentle call at our ice door at 7:30 a.m., carrying a tray of warm, sweet lingonberry juice, which the three of us sipped gratefully while we retell our chilly night’s experiences. Today, we were scheduled to go on a snowmobile tour to meet some of the local Saami people. The Saami are the indigenous Finno-Ugric people inhabiting the Arctic region of Sápmi, which today encompasses the northernmost parts of Sweden, Finland, Norway, and the Kola Peninsula of Russia. They are historically renowned as semi-nomadic reindeer herders, small-scale farmers, hunters, and coastal fishermen. The boys dressed up warmly in their gear and headed off for a long snowmobile ride across the drifts, leaving Anne-Sofie and me to take a walk to a nearby Saami village. The weather was bitterly cold but beautifully bright, with glistening, fresh powdery snow blanketing everything around our boots. It was not a long walk, and soon we reached a collection of traditional Saami huts constructed from timber logs, alongside several reindeer-skin tents. We located a small traditional restaurant built with reindeer-covered wooden walls. A blazing campfire logs in the absolute centre of the tent warmed up the entire room beautifully. Anne-Sofie and I sat down on a long wooden bench at a large timber table, and our waitress, dressed in traditional fur skins, brought our table some hot reindeer soup—which turned out to be a type of rich, clear consommé packed with meat and root vegetables. It was incredibly tasty and warming for our bones. We were served a plate of tunnbröd (traditional Swedish flatbread) and smör (butter) with the broth, following the meal with hot coffees, before taking a look around the craft shop. I was thoroughly enjoying the fresh, crisp Arctic air as the two of us walked back towards the Ice Palace. The afternoon sky was beautifully streaked with pink and gold, looking postcard pretty over the white pines. Our conversation came out of our mouths in frozen puffs of steam. The boys had successfully returned from their snowmobile trek. After checking into our alternative ‘warm’ room next door for the evening, I dressed warmly for supper, and we walked back to a historic restaurant close to the Saami village for our evening meal. This restaurant building historically dated all the way back to the early 1400s, and served pretty much the same traditional meal of reindeer meat and winter root vegetables. It was highly atmospheric to sit inside a timber building with over six centuries of history, watching the logs crackle in the hearth while our bodies slowly thawed from the day's outdoor excursions. 

Reindeer Racing 

The next morning, Col and I got togged up in our heavy snowsuits, hats, gloves, and eye shields in preparation for a reindeer ride. Our Saami guide, Joonas, was resplendent in his colourful traditional clothing. He seated us on a long, narrow wooden sled and towed our group about a kilometre across the drifts behind his snowmobile to where his reindeer were standing waiting. The journey took roughly twenty minutes through the glistening snow as we rode over the frozen Tornio River. I managed to take a few hurried photographs along the way, which required removing a glove, causing my hand to instantly freeze. It was quite painful, and I fumbled clumsily with the glove and camera as I desperately tried to cover my frozen digits. As we disembarked, Joonas proceeded to explain how we were to ride the reindeer. My face must have registered absolute shock, because I had naively assumed he was going to ride the animal himself whilst towing us behind. Instead, he assured me it was not at all difficult and promised he would be watching closely. I was instructed to move into a strange kneeling position on a small, narrow sled. Deciding discretion was the better part of valour, I allowed a few other people to go in front of me so I could carefully scrutinise the exact technique of reindeer racing. While waiting for my turn, I chatted to an attractive Asian woman who told me she was the tour guide for three Japanese tourists standing in the line. She mentioned she had been up all night watching the Northern Lights alongside a Japanese film company. I asked if the lights had been good, and she replied enthusiastically that they had been spectacular. I then watched intently as a German woman was forced to take a second lap around the huge circular track because she simply could not get her reindeer to slow down. Eventually, it was my turn. I slowly positioned myself on the sled, holding on tightly with one hand while waiting for the exact moment Joonas tossed me the lead rope. The reindeer set off at an immediate gallop, and we whizzed around the snow. It was exhilarating until the point where the animal suddenly stopped dead and casually walked the last few yards, which was a bit of a let-down. Nevertheless, it was a great experience. I patted my reindeer on his soft, brownish head before Joonas led us to a nearby enclosure to feed the herd some green lichen from a large linen bag. Reindeer husbandry is a cornerstone of Saami culture and economy. The herders pay an annual tax to the Swedish government which grants them legal grazing rights to maintain specified tracts of crown land for their migratory herds. Within Saami tradition, white-coloured reindeer are deeply revered and considered highly auspicious symbols of good fortune. Joonas pointed out a beautiful white specimen within the herd. Then, with a mischievous twinkle in his eyes (well, both of them, really), he smiled and announced, ‘I have let you ride the reindeer and feed the reindeer, and now I am going to show you how to cook them!’ 

The Smoky Teepee and the Field Mouse 

I must admit the idea of eating our mount did not sit well with a few of us in the group; nevertheless, we followed him inside a huge, wigwam-style teepee tent made of—you guessed it— reindeer skin. A massive campfire burned in the absolute centre of the tent, making the interior incredibly smoky. Birch branches were laid out in a neat circle on the dirt floor, topped with several thick animal skins. We sat around the hearth and watched Joonas demonstrate how to prepare a traditional Saami meal using smoked reindeer meat. ‘It can take a few days to properly smoke reindeer meat,’ Joonas explained, leaning over to grab a huge, fire-blackened frying pan and adding a generous dollop of clarified butter. ‘A long time ago, they used goat's milk and butter to rich the pot,’ he continued, picking up a platter of sliced meat. He fried the cubed meat in the pan, deftly turning the pieces this way and that. Water was boiling merrily inside a huge, battered tin kettle sitting directly in the embers, ready for coffee. Traditionally, he told us, one is never to cross directly through the cooking area but must always walk around the perimeter of the hearth. Just then, I spied a little brown field mouse scurrying out from a pile of straw and heading straight for the exit. Normally I would be on my feet screeching, but it was a very cute little mouse, and it was heading the right way—that is to say, away from the tent. The German woman mentioned she absolutely loved mice and kept several as pets at home, but I just smiled politely at her. ‘The meat is now ready,’ Joonas announced, slicing it into bite-sized pieces and handing them around so we could all have a taste. He handed out pieces of tunnbröd flatbread to eat alongside the venison. Each of us also received a small, traditional wooden cup of coffee, which was black, incredibly strong, and entirely unsweetened. As we sat in the dark, smoky teepee eating reindeer meat and drinking smoky black coffee, Joonas spoke of the ongoing difficulties the Saami experience living in modern Sweden, where the language, laws, and cultural traditions are so vastly different from their own. I got the distinct impression he was not telling us the full story, but I reasoned it was probably best not to bring heavy politics into our holiday experience. After the meal concluded, we were taken by sled back to the Saami village that Anne-Sofie and I had walked to yesterday. It was already almost pitch dark by the time we returned to the hotel by snowmobile. All in all, it was an amazing experience. 

Departure from Kiruna 

Our departure from Kiruna went smoothly, and we arrived back in Stockholm late in the afternoon. I finished packing our gear then waited in the lobby for our airport taxi. The car arrived incredibly late, leaving us with very little time to spare at the terminal. To make matters worse, my suitcase was actively disintegrating before our eyes—first the wheels fell off on the pavement, then the retractable handle came apart completely, and finally, the zip burst open down one side. I was in absolute agony of fear that I would be left holding the loose contents of my wardrobe in my arms before we even reached the check-in counter.
