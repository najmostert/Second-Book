# Chapter 26: Lapland 2010: The Wet Scarf and the Severed Artery

It was a little difficult trying to pack for the Arctic Circle when the temperature was 35°C in Cape Town, but I found a Chinese shop that sold oversized, thick padded jackets and bought one for each of us. They were full of pockets too, which would be useful for stuffing in gloves and woolly hats etc. We left for the airport with time to spare. All went smoothly till Col realised he hadn’t checked in his cameras and accessories at customs, so he left Tim and me already checked in and dashed off. Dunno why he does this, because, in all the years I’ve known him, we have never needed to declare the cameras on our return, but he’s a careful chap so off he went.

We threw some coffee down our throats and ate a bar of chocolate which left all three of us with a sugar high. Lufthansa informed us there would be a delay in take-off because they were refuelling. I was happy with that; they can refuel as much as they like. In the ladies I managed to flush half my long scarf down the loo, and had to extricate it using eyebrow tweezers and wash it thoroughly in the sink afterwards using copious amounts of hand soap. I now had a damp neck. As I returned to the family with half a wet scarf trailing down my back, I noticed that the sole of one of my new shoes was slowly parting company with its mother shoe and flapped my way to my seat after looking around for a place to buy some glue. When none was available I removed the sole altogether. I was now walking on thin ‘soleless’ leather with a long damp patch down my back. I’m a danger to myself and quite honestly shouldn’t be allowed in public.

After boarding twenty minutes later I discovered we had a free seat on either side of us. There was a stash of magazines in the overhead locker and as I carefully tried to extricate one, it slipped from my grasp and clobbered Col on the forehead, after which he began bleeding profusely. I felt terrible and asked a passing hostess for some plasters. She returned with a mini surgical pack. Col covered his wound and stared ahead miserably for a while, milking the situation for as long as he could. I gave him the remnants of a bar of chocolate as a peace offering. An American couple were sitting next to the empty seat. I asked them if it would be OK if we shared the seat. They were happy to comply until they saw me slowly filling it up with various paraphernalia till there was no room for anything of theirs. The sun shone severely through the porthole windows so we were  sweating unbecomingly. The flight had at least five toddlers on board. It was going to be a long, hot, noisy flight I thought, removing a few layers of clothing and placing them on the seat next to me. The American woman scowled at me and I smiled sweetly in return.

It was as I feared, a looong night punctuated by loud wails from one of the children (actually that might have been me). I watched three movies throughout the night, although by watching, I mean my eyes were open and bloodshot with tiredness. To pass the time I had a small bottle of wine and a Baileys on ice. Supper was beef or fish that probably last saw the sea when Adam was a lad. I went for beef. Col managed to snooze and Tim lay across his seat. I wriggled into several positions, but abandoned them just as quickly and got up several times to refill my plastic glass with water from the galley. Morning arrived with a fresh bellow from two of the toddlers. Their parents were all sitting with that glazed look of someone who has reached their limit and are now leaving what energy remains into breathing.

I patted cool gel around my tired eyes but they just dried to an unbecoming glaze. Breakfast was served: a life-like omelette and sausage, both of which were inedible and I decided they’d actually been stolen from a Japanese sampuru factory before take-off.

It was dark, cold and snow was falling softly outside Frankfurt Airport. A woman came up to me and asked if the black scarf in her hand was mine. I nodded and thanked her only to find I was still wearing my original. Also I don’t own a black scarf — well, I do now. In fact I now had two scarves.

We shuffled tiredly towards passport control then X-rays where my watch and every sore joint set off the alarm. I was patted down several times after removing another item from my person and performing the requested gymnastics that accompany these occasions: spread legs apart, now jump together arms outstretched, now fling a leg up and shake it all about. I was so tired that I lost balance and nearly clipped the security person on the jaw. The unsmiling security woman ran her beeping baton up and down my legs with grim determination. She was going to find that wad of cocaine I’d shoved between my toes come hell or high water. Eventually she admitted defeat and shoved me to the side to re-attach all previously dismantled armour. I joined Col and Tim and we slouched over to the nearest café to exchange a pile of euros for some awful coffee and a dry croissant. We sat in silence, slurping our coffee surrounded by mounds of luggage, the problem with going to a cold country is the amount of clothing one has to travel with doubles. Tim and I walked around the airport a bit to stretch our legs and restore some blood flow.

Our connecting flight was announced and soon our ‘Danke schöns’ became ‘Tack så mycket’ (thank yous). Our next frustration occurred when the automatic ticket reader decided I didn’t exist. Seems the ground staff in Cape Town airport printed two boarding passes for Tim but nothing for me. An official shuffled over to help.

Tim had sat two seats ahead of us on the plane which I suspected didn’t worry him too much. Gazing out of the window I saw there was still a lot of snow around. I was hoping they’d done due diligence and cleared the runway properly. There was an announcement. They were waiting for one passenger who had not boarded the plane and they were now removing their luggage. Col and I exchanged glances hoping that person wasn’t me. Seeing as they hadn’t printed my name on the ticket in Cape Town, this was a distinct possibility. I mentally calculated how many ways I could wear what I had on now and decided wearing my jacket backwards on day two may be an option.

I looked out the porthole as we landied at Arlanda Airport in Sweden. Snow lay thick on the ground and we had rather a fast and bumpy landing creating. After collecting our mounds of luggage, I tried to phone Anne-Sofie from my cellphone but discovered it wasn’t connected for Sweden. Col tried a phone box but it just swallowed all his coins, belched a few back but didn’t connect. I spotted an information desk and walked purposefully towards it with a friendly smile plastered over my face. I was so tired it was probably more of a grimace but the nice man assisted us and Col was soon chatting to his mum.

we were hurtling towards her apartment in Upplandsgatan street in a taxi. The apartment was smaller than the last one she shared with Sven Erik (who had sadly passed away) but this one was cosy. It had a pinkish glow due to the wallpaper and lights which adorned the apartment everywhere.
We took the metro to Gamla Stan (the old city) to watch the changing of the guard at the palace, arriving just too late having missed most of it and had to contend with seeing the backs of the guards and the horses’ retreating bottoms.

It was very slippery on the icy cobblestones and I was walking like a constipated tightrope walker to avoid falling.

The beautiful Stockholm Cathedral was built in 1279, and houses unique objects such as the St George and the Dragon sculpture. The cathedral is in the Old Town, in between the Royal Palace and Stortorget. High turrets and beautiful stained-glass windows glowed in the dim light.

The taxi manoeuvred its way smoothly to Arlanda Airport, weighed down with ‘hand’ luggage — backpacks, bags, jackets, scarves and gloves — to board the small 737 for Kiruna in Lapland. It would only be light for an hour and a half when we got there at 1 p.m. It was apparently a bearable −10°C today. The plane landed on time with a bit of a bump on thick snow. Kiruna is only one of five airports that allow planes to land on the runway with snow around, as they can’t keep a runway free of it for eight and a half months of winter. We disembarked into the lovely silent world of snow. It lay thickly everywhere you looked. A local man and woman were holding up a placard welcoming us. After exchanging greetings, we were taken to a long wooden room and given a pair of bright blue snow overalls to wear and thick blue gloves, boots and balaclavas as well.

The seven of us were led to a narrow wooden sled outside. Reindeer pelts softened the wooden seats.

## The Husky Sled and the Ice Palace

Attached to the front of the sled with long leather harnesses were twelve yelping, frenzied huskies, raring to go. Our guide asked us to sit tightly behind each other and I was given the dubious position of having the front seat. This position proved, let’s say, interesting as we set off suddenly after a loud exclamation of,

‘Hike!’

The huskies were off, and we bumped against each other like clothes in a dryer. As soon as we set off, several huskies released their bowels to the wind and unfortunately, due to my position in front, a few errant faecal pellets hit me full force. I had to cling on for dear life, as these dogs can run at a speed of up to 50 km/h (30 mph). After spitting a particle of husky poo from my frozen lips, I relaxed and looked around in awe at the absolute winter wonderland before us.

Thick, powdery snow glistened on the tops of white spruce and Scots pine trees as we whizzed by. The tireless huskies led us through narrow paths of thick snow, open spaces, and small tunnels with ease. Some of the ride was bumpy, especially when we came to a short rise and bumped down the slope. I was very glad of the thick clothing that had been provided. It was exhilarating and only my face felt the intense cold, as my scarf had slipped and my mask was a bit loose. After a fantastic hour and a half of sledding, we arrived at the Ice Palace, where we were to spend the night. The sky was slowly darkening, and the first sight of the Ice Palace provided a wonderful, pale, gleaming entrance of ice highlighted in green light from nearby lanterns.

The sled came to a standstill and we disembarked stiffly onto the snowy path. What a vision! Like a fairy story come to life, the palace shone and glittered in icy splendour. A huge shining column welcomed us to the entrance of the hotel where we checked in. My eyes darted everywhere, taking in the many ice sculptures. The check-in was actually situated in part of the ‘warm’ section where the building is made from pale wood, and was very warm — too warm, in fact, and I couldn’t wait to remove some layers. A short man dressed in leather trousers and a crisp white cotton shirt gave us a brief tour. He showed us our allocated lockers, where we stashed our suitcases and excess clothing. Then, we met up with a few other guests to hear a short safety talk in one of the ice rooms.

He told us we could not take anything into our ice room and may only sleep in thermal underwear. We were to collect our fleece-lined sleeping bags from reception before bedtime. These, he explained, would keep us warm in temperatures as cold as −15°C. The temperature of the rooms ranged between −5 and −8°C. He explained how to climb into the sleeping bags and to zip them up until only the nose was exposed then took us on a tour of all the ice sculpture rooms which were breathtaking. Each room he led us through had a different theme. We came to a room with a beautifully carved sports car, including all the extras like rear-view mirrors and a licence plate. Entirely made from ice; the sculptures were fantastic. One room had a ‘polar bear’ emerging from its ice cave and sleigh beds made of ice with only reindeer pelts for covering. Tables and chairs were also made from ice and were strong enough to take your weight. We came to an amazing ice church, complete with an ornate temple, ice pews, and a magnificent ice chandelier that hung somewhat precariously from the ice ceiling. It was truly an amazing experience. Our room had three single beds of ice with a layer of reindeer pelts across the base; two ice chairs and a square table completed the sparse furnishing.

Col, Tim and I trooped off to the ‘Ice Bar’, which adjoins the entrance to the Ice Palace. As you would expect, the entire bar is made from ice: ice counters, ice bar seats and tables, and a shelf that holds many bottles of booze, from gin to schnapps. We ordered a drink and took the colourful liquor in ready-made ice ‘glasses’ to a table. It was surprisingly warm inside, although all the rooms are maintained at a comfortable −5°C. The pretty blonde barmaid served us our choice of vodka, and I had a ‘Wolf Paw’, which is essentially vodka and cranberry juice. There were ice ‘pictures’ carved into the wall and many lights set in icy alcoves. Col left to look for his mum, who was in the ‘warm’ section that night.

Supper was served at 10 p.m., so we had a lot of time. We picked up Col from the Ice Bar and found him chatting to an Indian couple. He was on his fourth vodka and was very merry. After finding some more intricately sculptured features, we headed for the dining room. The room was full of pale wooden furniture and had a huge linen wall hanging. Candles burned everywhere and the colour theme was a pretty white and silver. Our meal was reindeer steak, salmon, wood grouse, duck, vegetables, and red wine. A five-star meal for a five-star experience. We left Anne-Sofie in her ‘warm’ room next door and prepared for our night in the ‘fridge’.

After dressing in our thermals for the night, Tim left for the room with his special sleeping bag, and I kept Col company while he waited for his bladder to fill up so he could empty it. It’s no fun getting up in the middle of the night for a wee when it is freezing. The toilets are also located in the wooden building next door, for obvious reasons. After changing into our thermals, Col and I joined Tim in the ice room. Luckily the rooms were lit so we could see what we were doing. We removed our fur boots very quickly at the foot of the bed, leaving them on the icy floor, then climbed into our sleeping bags and very quickly zipped them over everything but our noses. I turned to look at Col and Tim and just saw two red noses, which set me laughing.

## A Saami Experience

I can’t say it was the best sleep of my life as the bed by necessity was a bit hard, but it was definitely one of the most unique. A young girl woke us with a gentle call at the ice door, with a glass of warm lingonberry juice at 7:30 a.m., which we sipped gratefully as we retold our chilly night’s experience. That day, we were going on a snowmobile tour to meet some Saami, who are the indigenous people of the northernmost parts of Sweden, Finland, Norway, and the Kola Peninsula of Russia. They are traditionally reindeer herders, small farmers, hunters, fishermen and gatherers and I was excited to meet them.

The boys dressed up warmly and headed off for a snowmobile ride, leaving Anne-Sofie and me to walk to a nearby Saami village. It was cold but bright, with glistening fresh snow all around us. It was not a long walk and soon we reached some Saami huts made from wood and reindeer-skin tents. We found a small traditional restaurant, also made with reindeer-covered wooden walls. A blazing fire in the centre of the ‘tent’ warmed up the entire room. Anne-Sofie and I sat down on a long wooden bench at a large table and the waitress, dressed in fur skins, brought us some reindeer soup, which is a type of consommé with meat and vegetables. It was tasty, and we had a plate of tunnbröd (flatbread) and smör (butter) with it and followed this with coffee then took a look around the shop.

I was really enjoying the fresh, crisp air as we walked back to the Ice Palace. The sky was streaked with pink, and was postcard pretty. Conversation came in frozen puffs. The boys had returned from their snowmobile experience. After checking into our ‘warm’ room next door, I dressed warmly for supper and we walked to the restaurant close to the Saami village for supper. This restaurant dates back to the early 1400s and serves pretty much the same meal of reindeer meat and salmon.

## Reindeer Racing

Col and I got togged up in our snowsuits, hats, gloves, and eye shields for a reindeer ride. Joonas was our Saami guide, and he was resplendent in his colourful traditional clothing. He seated us on a long, narrow wooden sled and towed us about a kilometre on his snowmobile to where his reindeer were stood waiting. It took roughly twenty minutes through the glistening snow as we rode over the frozen Tornio River and I took a few hurried photos, which required removing a glove, so my hand instantly froze. It was quite painful, so I fumbled with the glove and camera to cover my frozen digits.

As we disembarked, Joonas told us how we were to ride the reindeer. My face must have registered shock because I thought he was going to ride the reindeer whilst towing us. He told me it was not difficult and he would be watching. I was told to move into a strange kneeling position on a small, narrow sled. I allowed a few people to go in front of me so I could scrutinise the exact technique of reindeer racing. While waiting my turn, I chatted to an attractive Asian woman who told me she was the tour guide for three Japanese tourists standing in line. She said she was up all night watching the Northern Lights with a Japanese film company. I asked if the lights were good, and she said they were. I watched intently as a German woman took a second go around the huge circle because she couldn’t get her reindeer to slow down.

Eventually, it was my turn. I slowly positioned myself on the sled, holding on with one hand while waiting for the moment Joonas tossed me the rope. My reindeer set off at a gallop and we whizzed around. It was exhilarating until the point where he suddenly stopped and walked the last bit, which was a bit of a let-down. Nevertheless, it was a great experience. I patted my reindeer on his soft, brownish head. Joonas led us to a nearby enclosure to feed the reindeer some green lichen from a large linen bag. He explained the importance of reindeer to the Saami community and that they pay a tax to the Swedish government every year which allows them to keep land for their herds. He told us that white-coloured reindeer are revered by the Saami, and he showed us one in the herd. With a twinkle in his eye (well, both of them, really), he said,

‘I have let you ride the reindeer and feed the reindeer, and now I am going to show you how to cook them!’

I must admit the idea did not sit well with a few of us in the group; nevertheless, we followed him inside a huge wigwam-style tent made of… you guessed it, reindeer skin. A huge fire burned in the centre of the tent and it was smoky inside. Birch branches were laid out in a circular position on the dirt floor, on which several skins were laid. We sat around the fire and watched as he showed us how to prepare a Saami meal using smoked reindeer meat.

‘It can take a few days to smoke reindeer meat,’ explained Joonas, leaning over to grab a huge, fire-blackened frying pan and adding a generous amount of clarified butter.

‘Long time ago they used goat’s milk and butter,’ he continued, picking up a plate of reindeer meat.

He fried the cubed meat in the pan, deftly turning it this way and that. Water was boiling in a huge, battered tin kettle that sat in the fire as well, ready for coffee. Traditionally, he told us, one is not to cross the cooking area but to walk around it. I spied a little brown field mouse scurrying to the exit from a pile of straw. Normally I would be on my feet, but it was a very cute little mouse, and it was heading the right way, that is to say away from the tent. The German woman said she loved mice and had several at home. I just smiled at her.

‘The meat is now ready,’ said Joonas, slicing it into bite-sized pieces and handing them around so we all had a taste. He handed out pieces of tunnbröd to eat with the meat. Each of us received a small wooden cup of coffee, which was black, strong, and unsweetened. As we sat in the dark, smoky teepee eating reindeer meat and drinking smoky black coffee, Joonas told us of the difficulties the Saami experience living in Sweden, where the language and traditions are different. I got the impression he was not telling us the full story, but it was probably best not to bring politics into the experience. After the meal, we were taken by sled to the Saami village that Anne-Sofie and I had walked to yesterday. It was already almost dark as we returned to the hotel by snowmobile. All in all, an amazing experience.

## Departure

We left Kiruna and arrived back in Stockholm late afternoon.

I finished packing then waited for the taxi for the airport which arrived late, so we had little time to spare. My suitcase was disintegrating — first the wheels fell off, then the handle came apart, and finally, the zip went on one side. I feared I’d be left holding the contents of my suitcase before getting to the airport.
