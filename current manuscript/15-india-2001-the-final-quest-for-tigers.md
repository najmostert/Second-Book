# Chapter 13: India 2001: The Final Quest for Tigers

As we were preparing to leave Saudi Arabia for good, Col suggested we visit India again en route to South Africa. We were hoping to finally see tigers in the wild this time around. The transit airport in Oman was small, cramped and we were faced with a long three-hour wait for our connecting flight to India. After locating a few hard plastic seats in a quiet corner of the departure lounge, we collapsed alongside our mountain of gear. Tim fell asleep across three chairs while Col and I simply stared stonily ahead, our eyes red and gritty with exhaustion after the madness of the preceding week. We silently contemplated the sheer insanity of moving our entire lives from one country to another whilst simultaneously choosing to holiday with eleven heavy pieces of luggage.
We exited the airport terminal in Calcutta and walked out into the bright sunshine. It was amazingly cool and crisp however which boded well for the trip. Calcutta is, of course, the former name for Kolkata, the grand capital of India's West Bengal state, globally renowned for its rich British colonial history, vibrant arts scene and majestic architecture. We met our designated tour guide near the exit gates. He greeted us warmly and informed us that he had already organised a secure place for us to store our massive suitcases at the Ashok Country Manor. 
Both Col and I had worked like crazy in our respective military hospitals right up to the very day of our departure and we desperately needed this vacation. Our new driver met us outside the terminal and we climbed into the back seat of the ubiquitous Ambassador taxi. He drove us to an impressive, tall, sand-coloured monument: Humayun’s Tomb where we walked into the expansive gardens and found ourselves a quiet bench to sit on. Humayun's Tomb is a magnificent Mughal mausoleum located in Delhi as an official UNESCO World Heritage Site. It’s famous for being the first garden-tomb constructed on the Indian subcontinent and historically the architectural precursor to the Taj Mahal. For an hour or so, Tim, Col, and I just parked off on the bench, looking around at the many birds and squirrels that abounded in the green park surrounding the tomb. India felt incredibly green and lush, especially when compared to the barren desert of Saudi Arabia where we had lived for the past two and a half years. The driver was asked to move the car, so we pulled up next to an Indian snake charmer. He was right out of a storybook, sporting a white turban, a lungi, and holding a round grass basket containing a waving cobra head, while a fat, yellow python lay coiled around his slender neck as he played a flute-like instrument. He performed the Indian rendition of a few bar ditties, then uncoiled himself, flung the basket lid back onto his startled cobra and moved towards us. Suddenly, I was wearing a long, cold python around my neck. I stood in shocked silence for a few minutes while Col did the required thing with his camera. I felt the tiniest pressure around my gullet and gulped furiously. ‘Take this snake off my neck, please,’ I begged him in what sounded like a squeak. Tim was then shaken awake, dragged out of the cab and ‘Kaa’ was wrapped quickly around his neck. He stood there half-asleep wearing a python necklace, trying to figure out what had happened to his dream and why his parents were trying to kill him. I nodded frantically to the sapera and he gently uncoiled the serpent from my son’s delicate neck. We paid 200 rupees for this dubious privilege, though Tim was now thoroughly excited by the experience. 
My trip to the loo was a hole in the floor affair. A man sat on a rickety wooden chair positioned directly between the male and female toilets but didn't appear to have any specific function other than to make sure no one walked off with a roll of toilet paper tucked under their lungi. We drove to the Delhi railway station, which turned out to be everything I had ever read about and more. A busy, smelly, completely disorganised chaos of people, boxes, animals, crates and food piled haphazardly all over the platform. There were hundreds of people lying huddled in groups, sprawled across their luggage and bags, standing, talking or shouting their wares. Mangy dogs in various stages of advanced decay sniffed around the piles of rotting vegetables and fruit. The crowds milled aimlessly around the platform edge, railway lines and the fast-food stands. Street vendors sold anything from fly-ridden fruit to padlocks—the latter appeared to have their entire commercial collection of brass locks festooned about their persons or strung together on heavy bicycle chains. There were people everywhere—piles of people—but not many people with piles, judging from the numbers openly squatting and urinating, puking, and defecating all over the railway tracks, seemingly oblivious to either approaching trains or anything else around them. Not surprisingly, there were also rats the size of small dogs scuttling boldly around the iron rails. I felt distinctly queasy, as did Col and Tim, judging by their green-tinged faces. We spotted an empty bench and sat down gingerly, terrified of coming into contact with anything unsavoury. The smell was indescribable and the mosquitoes and oppressive heat nearly unbearable. Col and I contemplated our massive pile of heavy luggage wearily and with a sense of foreboding. How in God’s name were we going to travel around India with this massive lot for four weeks and still maintain the same number of pieces? I was momentarily distracted by a nearby fruit vendor crying out his wares, consisting solely of a few sad piles of oranges, tomatoes and bananas. I purchased three bananas and two oranges off him for a few rupees. He watched us peeling and eating the fruit, and edged imperceptibly closer, calling out his wares. 
We were starving and the fresh fruit seemed to help, so I bought some more bananas, effectively depleting him of his entire stock. We were anxious that the amount of luggage we possessed would hamper our safe deposition when the train finally arrived, so located an ‘official’ helper to hoist all our gear onto the carriage at fifty rupees a piece, while I remained on the platform to keep a sharp eye on the remaining hand luggage. Our compartment was shared with four other Indian families. We quickly took up the remaining physical space with all our gear which meant contorting our bodies into some incredibly odd shapes within what little space was left. Our cabin-mates included a medical supplies salesman with his wife, along with what appeared to be his mother, father, and three young children; we exchanged polite nods and smiles. The train slowly rattled its way out of the station with a series of loud, piercing whistle blasts and continued down the tracks. Commerce continued unabated throughout the train and the corridors remained continuously busy. People walked around incessantly, pausing to chat with various acquaintances en route. Vendors walked up and down the narrow corridors selling ‘sanwitses’ or cold pani in tin buckets, kept chilled with rapidly melting cubes of ice. The thought of what virulent organisms that ice might be harbouring rendered any desire for water very fleeting. Chai-wallahs called out, ‘Chai, chai, chai!’ in high-pitched voices in order to be heard over the general clatter and cacophony of the tracks. A thin man wearing a dirty blue lungi came through the carriage carrying a large, steaming canteen strapped over his bony shoulder, from which a rather vile orange soup was poured into proffered cups. This last treat was apparently on the house as no money appeared to change hands. A remarkably sociable atmosphere prevailed. Aside from all the selling and chatting, the air continually reverberated with the sound of deep spiritual chanting. The Buddhist families sang melodiously, a simple ditty that ran along the lines of ‘Om Namah Shivaya’ repeated ad nauseam. Another group of Christians sang some well-known hymns, while the group positioned next to us clapped and sang their way into a collective trance. Col and I were worried about our logistical preparedness when we finally reached our stop at Bharatpur. We wanted to make sure to remove all eleven pieces of luggage whilst maintaining a sharp eye on their whereabouts at all times, doing so timeously to prevent the train from suddenly lurching off with either a portion of our gear or ourselves still on board. We decided that all the suitcases should be waiting in the narrow corridor between the carriages, ready for a quick removal. In Bharatpur, everything went ahead as planned, with me hoisting the heavy cases quickly out onto the platform and into the waiting arms of Col. Tim stayed inside with the hand luggage until this was accomplished. We paid another hundred rupees to have everything hoisted onto rolled-up cloth pads positioned on the heads of two local porters, hired on the spot. It was a nightmare to observe our familiar pieces of luggage bobbing ahead through the terminal while simultaneously trying to keep an eye on the remaining hand baggage and Tim, who was quick to disappear into the crowds due to his short stature. We followed as best we could through the sweltering crowds, up a long concrete stairway to cross over the railway tracks towards the main station exit where the logistical nightmare continued. There were no taxis available—only hundreds of auto-rickshaws, all clamouring aggressively for our attention. Col, having quickly paid off the two porters, desperately grabbed a rickshaw and tried to pile all our luggage onto it—a magnificent feat in itself, as these rickshaws are essentially no more than motorised bicycles with a basic canvas cover. A frantic scuffle immediately broke out between the other surrounding rickshaw owners, who tried to physically rip off pieces of our luggage to hoist them onto their own vehicles. They were all shouting, shoving and poking about our things while we desperately tried to hold onto our bags and our collective sanity. Col eventually screamed at the driver of the rickshaw containing our gear to get a move on, yelling that he would pay him well. This seemed to have the desired effect, for suddenly the three of us and our eleven pieces of luggage were mobile. We hung on to as much of the gear as we could, while Col bellowed into the driver's ear that he wanted to be taken to the Pelican Hotel—a place Col had read was positioned closest to the Keoladeo Bird Park. The driver shook his head stubbornly and yelled back through the wind that he would take us to the Falcon Guest House instead. Col, incensed, yelled back, ‘No! I want to go only to the Pelican Guest House!’ The driver insisted it had to be the Falcon Guest House, so the rickshaw continued to putt-putt forward at a slow pace in almost total darkness. We were effectively being hijacked towards a lodging place where the driver obviously received monetary award for delivering tourists. It was a long, tortuous journey and my fingers were completely frozen with the physical effort of trying to hold onto eighty kilograms of stacked luggage with my fingernails. Tim's head lolled sleepily against my back and I hoped he possessed enough strength to hold onto his allocated piece of hand luggage. As this motorised sewing machine chugged its way slowly through the busy, dark streets, loaded to capacity, I vowed to chuck out at least half of our personal belongings the moment we arrived. I would simply wear the same clothes for the entire month we were in India; no one would even notice, I reasoned. After arriving in the near-total darkness of the neighbourhood, there being very few operational streetlights, we carefully unclenched our bodies from the rickshaw frame. ‘This is Falcon Lodge,’ stated the driver unnecessarily. Col followed the direction of the whites of his eyes, silently paid him a hundred rupees and began to unpack our gear. He knew when he was beaten. The owner of Falcon Lodge appeared from the depths of the shadows and Col, after the barest of greetings, asked him how far it was to the Keoladeo sanctuary. It appeared it wasn't actually that far at all, so although it wasn't our chosen accommodation, we figured it would serve the purpose. We were led to our quarters: a large, hot room equipped with a ceiling fan and two doors leading onto a triangular brick verandah. I washed a few travel clothes in the sink and hung them outside to dry and a mattress was brought in for Tim. A large, scratchy brown blanket completed his bedding. 

The Sanctuary of Keoladeo

This morning, I walked into the garden and noticed there were quite a few tourists milling around—maybe they had all read the exact same section of the Lonely Planet guidebook. They were mostly young backpackers, but a few middle-aged travellers were scattered about as well. A vervet monkey was playing around in the foliage; I tried to photograph it, but the moment it saw the camera lens, it promptly scarpered into the trees. 
Later on, I tried my hand at bicycling after a very long absence from the saddle (I had last ridden a bicycle when I was ten years old). Col had hired two bikes for the day. It was a total disaster as I simply wobbled my way erratically around the path while the garden wall kept getting in my way. After I making contact with the brickwork for the second time, I climbed off the seat and announced firmly that I would walk. ‘Don’t be silly,’ retorted Col. ‘It’s miles away! I’ll get us a taxi or something.’ He eventually flagged down a turbaned Sikh to transport Tim and me in his bicycle rickshaw with Col following along in our wake on one of the hired bikes. It was warm but very pleasant pedalling along under the shade of the rickshaw covering. In the park, we located bar-headed geese, starlings and hundreds of vibrant green parakeets. Col spotted a couple of spotted eagle-owls and finally, we saw the magnificent Sarus cranes—tall, elegant grey birds with a distinct patch of crimson skin on their heads. They are quite rare and we watched them calling and displaying with their characteristic bobbing mating dance from the bank of the river. A short while later, Col abandoned his hired bicycle in favour of joining us in the rickshaw; it was difficult trying to hold onto expensive camera gear and ride at the same time.

The Red Stones of Fatehpur Sikri

An Ambassador taxi arrived with our new driver, Babu—he wasn't the same Babu from Southern India, it's just a common name here. He drove us some thirty-five kilometres southwest of Agra to the historic site of Fatehpur Sikri. The Emperor Akbar had constructed the city back in 1569 utilising a distinct red-coloured sandstone. We paid the entry fee for the ‘ghost city’, as it is also historically known, and climbed the massive, 54-metre Buland Darwaza, or Victory Gate. A self-appointed guide joined us and instructed us to remove our shoes. Col was handed a traditional cloth lungi; he awkwardly wrapped it around his chest, not knowing what else to do with the fabric, until the guide came over with a smirk, untied the cloth, and deftly rewrapped it around Col’s waist to hide his shorts. It is considered disrespectful to show your bare legs inside a mosque. We were led through a narrow passage into a high-roofed inner courtyard featuring a stone-tiled floor. Local hawkers immediately clamoured around for money, crying out, ‘I’m Pradesh, remember me!’, ’Sir, Madam, I am Dileep, you promised me I guide you!’ We tried our best to ignore them and followed our appointed guide closely through the arches. He led us down another dim corridor where, in the dusty gloom, we slowly made out the outlines of two men sitting cross-legged on the floor before a massive pile of colourful sari material. One of the men gave us an earnest explanation, with vigorous head-nodding from our guide, regarding the nature of their business. It appeared to be a spiritual concession: this was the tomb of Salim Chishti (Akbar’s saintly advisor) and we were instructed to purchase a sari, throw it over the tomb and make three wishes—all for the price of anywhere from 300 rupees to a more lavish twenty dollars! I was in the middle of telling them that we had absolutely no room left in our suitcases when the guide interjected, ‘No, no, you are not taking the sari! You must be leaving it with the tomb.’ It all became clear: you buy it, you leave it, it gets recycled and the next unsuspecting tourist buys it again. It was a magnificent money-making scam. I chose the absolute cheapest sari available—a boring beige affair since I was never going to wear it anyway—and the guide handed me a wicker bag full of fresh rose petals to scatter over the fabric. We were also provided with three pieces of red and yellow coloured string, to tie inside the tomb of Salim Chishti while making our three wishes. ‘But you must not tell a single soul of these wishes, or they will not come true,’ intoned the guide solemnly. I laid the beige sari over what seemed like a hundred other saris already stacked on the monument (which somewhat dispelled my initial thought that they recycle the cloth the second your back is turned). Tim and the lungi-clad Col scattered the rose petals over the tomb while I videoed the performance for future scrutiny. The guide then led us to a lattice-covered marble window where wishes are made while tying the three bits of string to the screen. I had a bit of difficulty tying intricate knots with my sausage-shaped digits; the visual effect was interesting with hundreds of these little red and yellow pieces of string tied all over the marble latticework. After leaving the sari, petals and string behind, we made our way outside to view the grand mosque. On the way down the colossal stone steps, we were accosted by numerous curio vendors and I found myself exchanging my Saudi open-lid wristwatch for two silver anklets. Their authenticity was assured by one of the hawkers scratching aggressively on a stone step with a bracelet to show its composition; I would have preferred a standard silver hallmark, to be perfectly honest. 

The White Marble of the Taj

We rejoined Babu in the hot, dusty interior of the car and headed straight for the Taj Mahal. This seventh wonder of the world is certainly breathtakingly beautiful, but our visit was severely marred by the constant, aggressive hounding from local touts, beggars, instant guides and general con artists who clamoured insistently for our attention and our money in return for nothing more than a pile of cheap trinkets. Even when the offering was an exquisitely carved miniature marble Taj Mahal, I felt far too hot, tired and irritable by then to appreciate the craftsmanship. Our first shock came with the international entrance price—it was a whopping 980 rupees each. It was this massive financial discrepancy that irritated us most, as we were certainly not on the same wealth level as visiting Americans with their strong dollars or Brits with their pounds. We paid the fee anyway as we had travelled this far, put our heads down and resolutely blocked out the continual pleas for cash: ‘Remember me, I am Ranji!’ or ‘I am Sarjan, buy from me when you come out!’ We climbed more marble steps and then the fantastic, close-up view of the Taj Mahal revealed itself. It was built by Shah Jahan for his beloved Empress Mumtaz Mahal, who tragically died at the age of thirty-nine after delivering his fourteenth child—that would kill anyone, I thought grimly. Personally, I’d have gone for less children and just build me a five bedroomed villa. We were prohibited from taking any video footage from further than the main entrance gateway to the monument and for this privilege, a further twenty-five rupees was demanded. I placed the video camera safely into Col’s camera bag, took out my digital camera instead and took a massive number of still photographs to make up for the lack of film footage. We walked around the perimeter of the white monument, viewing the Taj from all angles. The surrounding gardens were pretty, but the long, rectangular reflecting pool in front was empty of water, which was a great pity as the mirror reflection would have created a complete picture. I was sitting quietly on the stone steps next to the adjacent mosque, taking in the grand surroundings, when suddenly a plump man wearing a white thobe and fez called out to me and ushered me over to the shadows. He whispered conspiratorially that I could take some photos from directly inside the mosque interior, but warned me that I must not let the official guards see what I was doing. ‘Please to take off your shoe and leave outside,’ he whispered, pointing to an area of clean marble floor. I thanked him profusely and walked inside. The arch shape of the grand mosque door framed the white domes of the Taj Mahal beautifully so I took a few digital shots, then nervously extracted the video camera and filmed a quick sequence. The moment someone entered the room at the far end, I shoved the video camera back into its canvas bag and left the building. My beaming imam was waiting for me outside. He asked politely if I had enjoyed the view, immediately followed by a rapid, request for baksheesh! It turned out that his hidden motivation boiled down to cash; so much for the kindness of his religious heart. Col handed him a ten-rupee coin and the man looked down at his palm as though he had been handed a piece of fresh dog turd. Col threw another ten rupees into his calloused hand and we scarpered quickly towards the steps and removed our temporary foot coverings only to discover that we were expected to pay a fee for our shoe retrieval as well! Instead of arguing, we simply gathered them up in our arms and fled towards the exit gates. ‘Any minute now, someone is going to demand we pay for the air we’re breathing,’ I thought uncharitably. We had to remove our shoes yet again to ascend the steps to the top viewing platform. This time, surrendering them to a man operating a busy cloakroom stall. I knew this would cost us yet again. Still, the marble palace looked incredibly pretty in the soft afternoon light and there were clear, panoramic views from the top wall over the dusty sprawl of Agra. We eventually retrieved our footwear, after paying fifty rupees for their ‘safekeeping’. It was all getting to be a bit much. As we made our way back down the path to the car, waving hands and loud cries echoed from the stalls: ‘Remember me, I am Ahmed!’ and ‘Please buy, very cheap for you!’ Tim had a particularly persistent little lad of about six trying to sell him a handful of glass ink pens for a hundred rupees. Tim told him honestly that he didn't have any money, so the price immediately dropped from eighty to sixty, then fifty rupees, until finally, out of sheer desperation, the boy offered them to Tim for free. 

The Express to Jabalpur

The Agra railway station was another classic Indian experience in intense smells, milling crowds, heavy luggage, all combined with the blistering heat and aching limbs. Carrying all our gear was difficult, so we managed to secure a wooden bench positioned in front of some stacked bales of rice. Col held onto his primary camera bag for dear life, his eyes darting all over the platform, looking out for any would-be thieves who might dare to prise it from his clutch. I spotted a mobile fruit cart and went over to purchase three tangerines for ten rupees, mainly because thick-skinned citrus is the safest fruit to eat besides bananas. They were beautiful and sweet, so I went back to purchase a few more. The fruit vendor knew he was onto a good thing and positioned his cart next to our bench and mountain of luggage. The second the last segment of orange disappeared down our throats, he enquired, ‘You want more sweet orange? Banana? Only 25 rupee?’ We politely declined. ‘Okay, some grape?’ I shook my head each time, but he continued relentlessly with his monologue of offerings: ‘Oranges? Bananas? Sweet melon?’ He kept it up until we were both rigid with irritation. A mangy stray dog sat with him and came up to our bench for a quick sniff. It had a really sweet muzzle and we took an immediate liking, giving him a couple of horizontal pats that didn't quite make physical contact. ‘What’s his name?’ I asked the vendor. ‘Orange,’ he replied flatly. I wasn't sure if he was still trying to sell us some fruit or if this was indeed the unfortunate hound’s name, but he officially became ‘Orange’ for the remainder of our wait for the train, receiving a soggy biscuit I dug up from the bottom of my bag. Our train was an express sleeper scheduled to transport us to Jabalpur and onwards to Kanha. We were fortunate to have a full four-berth compartment to ourselves this time, so immediately began moving the luggage onto the top empty bunk to allow for movement around the cabin. Col and I harboured the same thought: that extra bunk would surely be allocated to some random traveller before long. This was India; having an entire compartment with an empty bunk remaining vacant was unheard of. The compartment was closed off with a heavy, thick fabric curtain, which lent an air of intimacy to the area that we had no wish to share with a total stranger all the way to Jabalpur. With this fear in mind, when the ticket collector made his official appearance shortly afterwards, we asked him if the top bunk was booked for anyone tonight. He winked at us conspiratorially and announced that he would personally see to it that no one else disturbed our privacy. His name was Mr Gupta and he promised he would sort the entire matter out for us. We sighed in massive relief until we noticed he wasn't leaving and was instead gazing meaningfully at our luggage. ‘You have a present for me?’ he enquired significantly. I was beginning to understand the Indian way of doing business—nothing is given for nothing. I opened my purse to hand him some cash, but he shook his head; no, he didn't want money only a small present. I searched through our suitcases for something suitable that we could afford to give away before coming across a brand-new leather wallet I’d bought for Tim just before we left Saudi Arabia (at seven years old, he was already an avid collector of foreign banknotes). I clamped my hand over Tim’s opening mouth and handed the wallet to Mr Gupta. He fingered the soft leather for a moment, looked up and declared his pleasure with this small transaction with a gappy, betel-nut-stained smile. The deal was sealed. So, with a series of muffled cries of protest from Tim, Mr Gupta left us to ourselves for the night. I promised Tim under my breath that I would purchase another, ‘much better wallet’ for him later down the line. We settled into our bunks and later the attendant came around with the bed linen—two clean sheets and a rough grey blanket folded up and ready for unrolling. After a quick snack from the depths of my bag we finally settled down to sleep, and arrived in Kanha the following afternoon. 

The Jungles of Kipling Camp 

Kipling Camp is situated about ten kilometres from the main national park gates, established back in 1990 on the edge of the famous Kanha Tiger Reserve in Central India. It’s open to naturalists, conservationists and travellers who wish to relax within beautiful, tropical surroundings. It is also the historic home of Tara, the much-loved Asian elephant who famously starred in Mark Shand's bestselling travel epic, Travels on My Elephant. 
A tall, Nordic-blonde girl met us at the main entrance gates. She showed us to our designated cottages; two in total, including a separate one for Tim. I was feeling feverish; all I wanted to do was lie down somewhere cool and sleep off my exhaustion. Some hot tea was brought to our veranda, and then I did just that, with a huge ceiling fan blowing over me. Col and Tim left me to rest and headed off on the afternoon safari drive. I slept for two hours, having taken an antibiotic as it seemed I’d developed a mild case of ‘Delhi belly’. I felt certain it was due to a moment of temporary amnesia on the express train when I had mistakenly brushed my teeth with a mouthful of tap water. I felt better when I awakened later, sitting outside on the open wooden floored verandah to write up my diary. The clouds overhead gathered and darkened ominously and sure enough, it rained briskly for about twenty minutes, cooling the jungle down beautifully. In the forest next to the camp boundary, I noticed some chital and a troop of langur monkeys crashing around in the high canopy (the monkeys, that is, not the deer). Tim and Col arrived back at the cottage, bursting with excitement. They had seen TWO TIGERS! I was upset. At first, I thought Tim was pulling my leg, but Col confirmed they had spotted them quite close to the dirt road, just over the rise. I was cross and blamed my illness on the whole of India’s seething, virulent masses; if the local bacteria weren't so aggressive, I’d have been well enough to join them on the truck. This was unfair, if true, but the anger helped me process my disappointment. 

The Tiger Census and the Chase 

It really poured with rain the next morning but the park gates were opening late anyway to accommodate the annual tiger census and the weather cleared up by then. The traditional census method involves having park rangers carefully raking a patch of soil about ten feet long across specific demarcated tracks popular for predator crossings. They then count and measure the number of unique tiger pugmarks left in the soft earth the next morning, each one being easily identifiable to a specific animal. It didn't strike me as the most scientific method to monitor a population, but it gave the wardens an idea of the current tiger numbers. After a fairly uneventful few hours of driving, we stopped at a clearing to enjoy our breakfast before setting off for the remainder of the route. Suddenly, a full-scale tiger chase was on. It was incredibly exciting; one of the other safari guides had radioed our driver to report fresh tiger sighting nearby, but we first had to return to the main gate to purchase a special ticket that permitted us to ride an elephant into the thick grass to view the cat. Indian bureaucracy at its absolute best. The truck bounced quickly over the dusty road back to the gate where the ticket was quickly purchased and with all of us holding onto the frame with both hands, we bounced back, dust flying towards the area where the mahouts had located the tiger. Two large elephants stood waiting next to the tall, pale elephant grass. A wooden ladder was hoisted against the side of one of the beasts and we were instructed to climb quickly onto the heavy howdah platform at the top. I noted with interest that our designated elephant was none other than the infamous Shivaji, complete with shorn-off tusks. Local legend had it that he had once killed a forest guard with his tusks. He was a colossal fellow and it seemed a long way down to the ground from his large, concave back. The platform seat was constructed from bamboo and heavy wood, comfortably seating the four of us. So, with me clutching the video camera tightly in both hands, Shivaji climbed the steep embankment through the tall yellow grass and dense reeds to where the tiger lay sleeping—or trying to. It was a youngish tigress and I could just about make out her orange stripes hidden in the reeds. The mahout steered Shivaji as close as he could to where she was lying panting slightly in the heat while we leaned over the rail as she lay seemingly unconcerned in the criss-cross of her dry reed bed. It was thrilling to sit atop the huge, swaying elephant in such close proximity to this lovely predator. The mahout positioned the elephant in such a way that everyone on board received an opportunity to view her. We stayed at the site for a good twenty minutes before slowly ambling back down the slope to the dirt road. I was happy; I had finally seen my first tiger and despite the relatively obscured viewing (we only saw parts of her head through the grass), it had been a worthwhile experience. We bounced back to camp, chattering excitedly. 

Meeting the Guardians of the Wild 

Some prominent VIPs arrived at the camp later that afternoon, sending the lodge girls into a frantic flap of activity. They set the dining tables for lunch, bringing out heavy brass candlestick holders and their finest linen. Several government delegates and executive members of the Tiger Foundation were visiting, including Bob Wright, the owner of Kipling Camp and his daughter, Belinda. They set up a small display table near the lounge with small tiger models, T-shirts, and wildlife cards for sale. Bob Wright was a retired British Indian Army officer and the legendary founder of the Tiger Fund and the Tollygunge Club. His daughter, Belinda Wright, the co-owner of Kipling Camp and the author of the book I had been reading earlier,Through the Tiger’s Eyes. She was one of India’s leading wildlife conservationists, a Founder Trustee of WWF (India) and a key member of the Tiger Task Force that originally set up the famous Project Tiger initiative back in 1973. The Chief Justice—the man responsible for trying anyone caught poaching inside the reserves—and a group of young Indian delegates from various wildlife protective societies were all in attendance. About fourteen delegates in total. After enjoying drinks in the lounge, it was time for supper which had been delayed because we were waiting for Bob to finish his meetings. He gave a warm toast to Belinda and formally welcomed the Mostert family to the camp. He was a real ‘Colonel Hathi’ character, which felt appropriate given that we were in true Kipling territory, and Rudyard had apparently drawn inspiration from the animals in this forest for The Jungle Book. Another ex-Kipling girl arrived at the table with her mother. They were very ‘Sloane Ranger’ in their demeanour—all ‘tally-ho’ and ‘Mummy says’ types—but very pleasant all the same. On our morning game drive the following day, the chase was on again. Thanks to our expert guide, we were able to dispense with bureaucratic paperwork this time and just drive straight out to meet the tracking elephant. Tim was delighted. We climbed directly onto the elephant's back from the seat of our jeep and led into a clearing in the dense elephant grass. There lay a magnificent, male tiger with deep orange and white fur. Although it was wonderful to be able to view the animal at such close proximity, I couldn't help feel a pang of sympathy for him; they must get very little rest being constantly disturbed by strings of gawking tourists. Still, it was another spectacular experience and I wouldn’t have missed it for the world. While elephant-back riding in India was a popular tourist activity this time, the practice has since become controversial due to significant animal welfare concerns. Ethical issues surrounding their confinement and working conditions mean that modern travellers should thoroughly research reputable conservation operations, or choose alternatives such as jeep safaris to avoid supporting cruelty. At least I can say the elephants we rode at Kipling Camp were exceptionally well looked after by their mahouts, but I wouldn't encourage the activity today. We returned to the lodge in excellent spirits and enjoyed a gin and tonic on the verandah to discuss the morning’s wildlife viewing. We were joined by Miranda and her mother. Miranda’s mother was an amusing character; she wore a large straw hat with a small solar panel on the rim that powered a miniature fan, thereby emitting a gentle, continuous breeze across her face. It was an ingenious invention, really. Colonel Hathi—alias Bob Wright—was another character. Kipling Camp had been set up by Bob and his wife, Anne. Bob had been born in Calcutta back in 1924 and both he and Anne were fierce, lifelong conservationists. Bob had been awarded the OBE and Anne the MBE in the 1980s for their services to conservation; they were a fascinating couple, and their passion for wild animals was palpable. 
After a short rest, the camp guests trooped down to the riverbank to watch Tara have her daily afternoon bath. Tara was, of course, the famous elephant that the travel writer Mark Shand—brother to Queen Camilla—had set off with on an epic 1,000-kilometre journey across India to the Sonepur Mela, the world’s oldest elephant market. When he finally reached his destination, he decided he couldn't bear to let Tara be sold into unknown hands, so he brought her to the sanctuary of Kipling Camp in November 1989, leaving her in the permanent care of Bob and Belinda. A wooden ladder was set against her massive side for us to climb up before we walked down to the nearby river channel, some five kilometres away. It was a bit of a lopsided ride for some reason; perhaps the heavy howdah straps weren't balanced straight on her back. At the sandy riverbank, Tara knelt gently in the dirt so we could safely disembark. We spent a very enjoyable hour watching her being washed by her dedicated mahout. First, she frolicked around happily in the deep water, blowing large bubbles and giving herself a magnificent mud bath. Her mahout then got into the river and instructed her to lie flat on her side and he and Tim set to work scrubbing her skin down with firm bristle brushes. She appeared to be enjoying the pampering immensely. Tara eventually climbed out of the water and dried off on a large flat rock then, using a small twig held in her trunk, she began gently picking between her toes. It was amusing to watch such a delicate, precise operation being performed by such a colossal animal. 

Gosh and Golly: The American Arrival 

Returning to camp by noon, we discovered a new family of Americans had arrived at the lodge. They had a young boy of Tim’s age and a little, whey-faced girl who looked as though the climate of India was not agreeing with her gentle constitution. Tim and the boy, Andrew, immediately exchanged Pokémon and Game Boy pleasantries while Col and I chatted with their parents, David and Holly. David was a chartered accountant or something similar; he kept his briefcase open on the table and his laptop was never far from reach. They were a pleasant, typically American couple: open, affable, gregarious and a little naive in matters of international travel. One wondered how they had managed to find their way out of the United States at all. They knew next to nothing regarding India or its local inhabitants, and proceeded to ask Col a million questions, the answers to which invariably elicited a loud round of, ‘Gosh and golly, amazing!’ and ‘Imagine that!’ This enthusiasm came mainly from Holly; David was a little more restrained in advertising either his amazement or his ignorance. They asked us about our life back in Saudi Arabia and thought it incomprehensible that we would willingly choose to live in a place that didn’t possess public cinemas, bars, or any form of Western entertainment. I didn’t enlighten them regarding the vast quantities of excellent homemade wine I had successfully brewed using a hospital chest drain and grape juice. They proceeded to consume two massive helpings of lunch, polished off all the bottled water on the table, ordered extra bread but didn't actually eat it, then requested the staff to wake them up for the afternoon game drive. Col and I sighed in relief when we heard that we would be travelling with Gemma and her mother again for our afternoon route. Bob sipped several more pink gins and got Tim to bang the heavy brass dinner gong, shouting out, ‘Well done, young Tim!’, which tickled him to bits and produced another swing at the gong. 
At the start of the afternoon drive, we didn't see much wildlife. Col focused his lenses on photographing some peacocks displaying their plumage in a clearing; it is always a lovely to witness animals in their natural habitat. We tried our hand at replicating the distinct chital alarm call; Col was remarkably good at the sound, but the deer appeared unimpressed by his performance. Further on, we noticed a group of safari jeeps had stopped a in a meadow, so our driver raced ahead to join what appeared to be a massive meeting of vehicles. The passengers in the lead jeep leaned out of their window and whispered excitedly that they had spotted a tiger crossing the track earlier and that the cat was supposedly still lurking nearby. Suddenly, there he was: a lone male tiger walking slowly through the scrub to our right. It was an exciting sight, if short-lived, because he disappeared very quickly into the tall elephant grass again. Then just six minutes before the official closing time at the park gates, our driver turned the jeep into a small waterhole clearing. There, on the far side of the water, a majestic, dark orange tiger was lying flat on its back with its white paws stuck up in the air like a giant kitty. After witnessing this we had to rush quickly back to the main gate, nearly earning a speeding fine from the park guards in the process. This evening, lodge guests were driven by jeep down to the nearby riverbank where lines of candles lit the pathway to a beautiful picnic area, complete with a roaring bonfire and heavy blankets laid out on the river sand. Tim, Andrew, and the other kids immediately formed an impromptu pyromaniac club and set about throwing anything that would burn into the flames. I removed Tim from the club and settled him firmly on a blanket on the far side of the clearing. Col and I sat down with Holly and Dave. Holly jabbered away for the entire evening, barely pausing for a single breath, chatting about the ‘amaaazing’ animals she had witnessed that day, regularly interrupting herself to introduce anyone who came within half a metre of our blanket. She asked a continuous barrage of questions and the answers were always accompanied by a dramatic gasp of amazement from her, regardless of how mundane the subject matter. 

Mulki and the Grave of Bhagwan 

The next morning, Col returned from the bush having successfully caught a few butterflies and we left for our afternoon drive accompanied by a Scottish couple who had joined the camp this morning. They hopped on board our open jeep and introduced themselves, though I promptly forgot their names; they could have been Bill and Ben for all I know - my memory for names is legendary. I start by asking politely where they had just travelled from and they said Bharatpur. ‘Oh!’ I reply brightly. ‘That’s just where we’ve come from!’ To which Col immediately added dryly, ‘No, it’s not. That’s in the opposite direction!’ This meant that anything I uttered after that topic was subject to confirmation from Col before credibility was restored. As we were photographing gaur—a magnificent, big black bull standing on the side of the track— the Scotsman calmly remarked, ‘There’s a tiger right behind us.’ All of us turned around in our seat and sure enough, crouched in the grass behind our jeep some ten metres away was a  beautiful female specimen. We watched her for a few minutes, all of us filming like mad with our cameras. Within minutes, six more tourists arrived at the spot and she quickly grew fed up with the commotion, rising gracefully to her feet and slinking away into the thick bush. We drove like the clappers to reach the Mulki area before the gates closed, stopping briefly to photograph a few more impressive gaur bulls along the way. We pulled up at the historic grave of Bhagwan—a legendary park guide who had been tragically gored to death by a massive bull years before. Col and our guide, Abhi, stepped out to place a stone atop the grave mound, a local custom that is supposed to bring luck to any potential tiger spotting. We could smell the tiger's kill before we saw the animal—a horrible, overpowering stench of rotting flesh wafting through the trees. Suddenly, Col shouted, ‘Look behind!’ All heads swivelled in our seats and there, crossing the dirt road where our jeep had been just seconds before, was the biggest tiger we had seen during the holiday. He was a massive male known to the rangers as ‘Mulki’, after the specific territory he resided in. We only caught sight of him for a few brief seconds before his heavy frame disappeared into the long elephant grass. On our way back to the lodge, we enjoyed a rare sighting of a pack of wild Indian dogs, or dholes; an exciting viewing. Abhi was pleased with the tiger show and felt our success was due to us placing the stone on the grave earlier. Although I murmured my agreement, I knew it was Col’s acute powers of observation that had secured us the tiger. Later that evening, Holly joined us on our verandah, and the air immediately abounded with a fresh round of ‘gosh’ and ‘golly’ while we recounted our afternoon tiger viewing. She was a real ‘gee whillikers’ sort of person. Tim banged the dinner gong again with an air of great importance. Bob delivered one of his classic dinner speeches, formally welcoming his daughter, Belinda, who had quietly slipped into the room. He expressed his sincere regret at seeing the Hales go— Miranda and her mother were scheduled to leave for the airport at midnight—and then he turned towards our table and announced that it was even worse to be losing the Mostert's. Whilst we were pleased he felt that way, it was a little embarrassing; I am reasonably certain he was on his fifth pink gin by that point, though. He also mentioned that, unfortunately, tiger poaching was on the rise. There were currently fewer than 5,000 tigers remaining according to the latest census figures, which is really very sad. They are truly magnificent creatures and have fast become my favourite big cat. It wasn't easy leaving Kipling Camp; it was such a lovely, friendly, homely sanctuary. The same taxi man collected us for the return journey, playing the identical Bollywood tunes on his car tape deck as we rumbled down the dirt track. I noticed the North of India differs from the South in various ways. The men don’t wear as many lungis, and that characteristic sideways head-nodding isn’t nearly as prominent; furthermore, the local curry is noticeably less potent and far more fragrantly seasoned. The village scenes along the highway were fascinating with the communal water pump serving as the central hub for all social gatherings. The local girls’ long, dark, glossy hair was beautifully bedecked with fresh flowers, and their saris were colourful. They carried heavy earthenware and bright copper pots balanced perfectly on their heads. Many emaciated cattle wandered aimlessly around the tracks and small dukas, chai stalls, and hotelis abounded. I noticed the authorities painted the lower tree trunks white and the villagers coated their walls with a light blue stripe over a white base to keep the termites away. I wondered if it actually worked; most of the surrounding trees seemed intact, so I suppose it must. These Hindu temples were quite small and decorated with fresh flowers and bright red and ochre paint with differing deities situated inside tiny stone alcoves. I asked our taxi driver if he would pull over at the next place with a toilet. He immediately swerved to the side of the road and pulled up next to a basic mud dwelling, instructing me to go inside. So, somewhat unceremoniously, I found myself crouching among local chickens, grass and broken clay tiles. Later Col met up with Joseph, the local who’d been highly recommended to us as a professional wilderness guide. 

Bandhavgarh and the Cubs in the Grass 

Joseph woke us up at 3:30 a.m. after we had managed only a very short nap. Hastening out into the dark towards the waiting Maruti Gypsy—the standard open safari jeep in these parts—I climbed aboard and looked around at the terrain. Bandhavgarh was noticeably prettier than Kanha, dominated by winding silver streams, purple mountainous ridges with rocky outcrops and a great number of colourful orange and red flame-of-the-forest trees. The massive sal trees emitted sweet perfume into the night air. We encountered many nocturnal birds along the way as we bounced across the bumpy dirt road. As we drove deeper into the Bandhavgarh park, we could clearly see the ancient fort positioned high up on the hill; it is over 2,000 years old, originally built in the 10th century. A group of safari jeeps and local trackers had stopped ahead of us; the mahouts had spotted a mother tiger with her two young cubs lying in the tall grass not far from the road. Ernie and Carroll, whom we had met earlier in the trip, were already at the scene. We climbed directly onto the howdah of the waiting elephant and rode straight into the long, yellow grass. The tigress was eating her kill from earlier that morning— it was either a sambar deer or a chital. As I was videoing the crunching noises of bones snapping as she ate, I heard a deep growl and looked straight down at the cubs. They were growling aggressively at the elephant, who was not at all happy with their audacity. It is frightening to realise that all you have between your skin and three tigers is the back of an unstable elephant—little did I know just how close a call I was to have later that day. 
Afterwards, our guide took us to inspect a cavern full of bats. There were several square ventilation holes cut directly into the solid rock to allow natural light in, which had been created centuries ago by ancient builders and hundreds of leaf-nosed bats now resided inside the dank, dark cave. 

The Stand-Off at the Steps 

We were up early the next morning for game viewing. Joseph arrived at the steps with a different Gypsy jeep, having given our regular vehicle to a visiting Spanish couple. It was heavily cloudy and humid, so we collected our rucksacks preparatory to our trek up to the Bandhavgarh Fort. It was then that we discovered Joseph had abandoned our family in favour of the—obviously more financially lucrative—Spaniards. We met the couple earlier; a gaunt Spanish pair, both keen amateur photographers who were completely obsessed with finding tigers. The replacement guide drove to the top of a high ridge in the park, where we were instructed to continue our ascent on foot up the steep mountainside towards the old fort. It was a lovely spot—green, shaded and cool, featuring natural stone steps carved directly into the rock face. A colossal reclining statue of Lord Vishnu, expertly sculpted from a single piece of stone, lay behind a natural pool cut out of the mountain rock and filled to the brim with cool spring water. A small group of tourists from nearby hotels and safari lodges were also waiting to climb to the top of the fort. The sandstone caves surrounding the fortress were over 2,000 years old and massive banyan tree roots snaked like serpents through the cracks of the ancient stone walls, the statues covered in a layer of emerald moss. I waited at the top of the stone stairs alongside an old Indian woman who sat quietly on the low wall and watched with interest as I filmed the grand surroundings with my video camera. Suddenly and with amazing calm she remarked, ‘There’s a tiger over there.’ She pointed a thin, arthritic finger towards the nearby bushes. I thought I had heard her incorrectly until I carefully followed the line of her pointing finger. A medium-sized tiger was indeed crossing the narrow path in front of us, a mere ten metres away. In open-mouthed fascination, I watched the predator move into the thick bushes next to the steps where the rest of our group were sitting. The tiger turned its heavy head and calmly surveyed the huddle of humans. I asked my jellied legs to move as quickly as they could and I tottered unsteadily back towards the others with my heart hammering like a captured bird. I grabbed Tim tightly by the hand and waited with the rest of the group to see what the cat's next move would be. Some of the group were clicking away furiously with their cameras. I remember thinking that I had better video the scene too, as it would likely prove a useful piece of evidence for the local authorities to review after discovering our mutilated bodies in the bush. Someone whispered that we should group tightly together to form a larger human silhouette, thereby acting as a deterrent should the tiger decide he was a bit peckish. Then a second tiger quietly emerged from the undergrowth and joined the first on the path. Col whispered that they might be the two-year-old sub-adult cubs that we had seen from the elephant earlier—but where on earth was the mother? As long as she remained out of the equation, we might survive the encounter, but if she turned up from hunting, one of us was going to be severely upset. I glanced down at Tim—a tender, morsel for any hungry cat—and grabbed his small hand again, walking slowly and deliberately backward down the stone steps. Just then some eejit in our small group decided to whistle in a piercing, shrill manner, instantly alerting the tigers. One of the brothers stepped closer to the edge of the path. I was feeling extremely anxious and planned to shove the stupid whistler directly in front of me should one of the cats charge. 
‘Don’t anyone move,’ Col commanded authoritatively, his voice cutting through the panic. Suddenly the lead tiger gave a soft, guttural growl and dropped its chest, crouching, prior to a pounce. In a flash of panic, everyone in the group lunged simultaneously for the two remaining safari jeeps parked at the base of the steps. The vehicles were already packed full of people so most of us landed on someone else’s lap—a situation that would have been amusing had the situation not been so dangerous. Tigers and humans watched each other warily for a tense minute before suddenly, in a frantic flurry of Hindi, we found ourselves unceremoniously dumped out of the vehicle by the group whose jeep we had been occupying and they shot off down the track in a cloud of dust, leaving our nervous, huddled group stranded in their wake. 

The Fortress of Akbar

Luckily the sudden, loud roar of the jeep unsettled the tigers and they moved off into the thick jungle. We knew they were still lurking in the immediate area so when our guide eventually reappeared carrying the cardboard boxes of lunch and bottled water he had gone to fetch, we clung to his departing shadow like glue as he moved up the mountainside path towards the fort. Tim’s face had turned a bright, glowing crimson so I fed him frequent sips of bottled water. Luckily, there were a few interesting ancient statues to look at along the route that allowed me to pause long enough to view them appreciatively whilst catching my breath, thereby maintaining the illusion I was flitting up the mountain like a mountain goat. Most of the stone sculptures depicted either Lord Vishnu or Lord Rama, with elephants and serpents dominating the designs. I remained nervous of continuing our hike with the possibility of another tiger encounter fresh in my mind. It was a bit of a mushkila trying to keep our scattered group moving together and with wild predators roaming around the path, this lack of discipline was not a good thing. We eventually found our way to the first official gate of the fort—a magnificent, heavily carved iron door cut directly into the solid rock face. A smaller pedestrian door led our group inside the actual fortress grounds, via a winding, grassy pathway lined with more stone carvings. We began another steep ascent towards the main shrine. One specific stone carving depicted a giant tortoise, expertly fashioned out of the rough-hewn rock. At the summit we ate our lunch on a rocky ledge overlooking a sheer cliff face that offered a panoramic view of the park below. Two large vultures' nests clung precariously to the cliff edge by a few spindly-looking branches. I sipped bottled water gratefully before proceeding further past more stone carvings of gods and goddesses in various intricate poses. The guide walked ahead of us pointing out items of historical interest or explaining some of the more obscure mythological carvings. Suddenly, he stopped and pointed to a dark mass lying in the pathway. He turned around and casually informed us that it was fresh tiger scat. We were just internalising this unsettling snippet of information when he gestured towards a pile of bloodied fur and bleached bones and remarked that they were the remains of the tiger's last supper. Some of our group partially regurgitated their recent lunch in fright and we moved closer together for mutual protection, terrified that his next comment was going to be, ‘Ta-da! And there he is!’ He possessed a slightly warped sense of humour, this guide and one could never be certain. We made our way to the main temple, each member of the group warily eyeing the long grass lining the path en route. The building was remarkably well preserved given it was constructed in 10 A.D. A thin man wearing a woollen cap, a woeful expression and a dirty white shirt and lungi met us at the bottom of the long stone steps; he was the local Dvarapala, guarding the temple precinct. He didn't look particularly guard-like to me, but I suppose that didn't mean he wasn't official. He must have been at least eighty-five years old. There was another huge, square pool cut directly out of the surrounding mountain rock. This had served as the swimming pool for the garrison soldiers of bygone years, whose quarters had been constructed next to the water's edge. The ancient barracks housed a hundred separate rooms. Banana trees and the ubiquitous sal trees lined the perimeter, providing the area with some much-needed shade from the blistering sun. We were instructed to remove our footwear and given a brief tour of the crumbling interior walls. The old man showed us a secondary chamber that housed yet another carved deity and taking cue from his hushed, reverent tone we all gazed at the icon for a suitably respectful minute or two. I think it depicted Vishnu, Brahma, and their sister Shiva, but don't quote me. We were shown a small copper pot and asked to deposit a token of our appreciation and Col and I rather furtively chucked a few rupee coins into the container. We understood by now that India appreciates fleecing international tourists as much as possible while they have us in their midst, to be fair, they aren’t the only country afflicted with this particular  condition. In my wanderings around the ancient pool, I discovered a small reefer constructed from rolled-up banana leaves tied with string— clearly the old guard's chosen method for keeping boredom at bay. On our return down the mountain path we found the errant Spanish couple swimming in the rock  pool and making quite a racket with their raucous laughter. It rather spoiled the ancient tranquillity of the area, I thought sourly, still displeased with their having ‘stolen’ Joseph from us with their strong Spanish pesetas. We left them floating in the pool and continued toward the park entrance. The descent was a lot quicker than the climb up, although the knowledge that tigers were roaming free may have given us that extra impetus. After this incredibly exciting afternoon we had a final drive around the park and discovered our replacement guide was remarkably informative, pointing out many items of interest with a brief description as we passed. Two more tiger chases ensued. I felt bad harassing the mother tiger and her cubs as it was the same family we’d viewed from the elephant earlier; this time accompanied by their massive and very protective mother. She ran straight across the dirt road at one stage with the large cubs in tow so we decided not to add to her stress and left. 

Radiators and Railway Regret 

Early the next morning we collected the hired car bound for Katni. The roads were shocking—mostly giant potholes—so that even though the distance to Katni was only a hundred and eighty kilometres the drive took us hours. At one point, we discovered the road ahead had suddenly transformed into a massive pond of muddy water. Col sensibly suggested we drive around the perimeter, but our driver felt confident he could power straight through the middle. Predictably, we got stuck roughly halfway across. Col and I were forced to climb out of the cabin and wade into the sludge, after removing our shoes to help push the car out of the thick mud. The carburettor had become completely wet and the engine refused to start. The driver further complicated matters by continually cranking the ignition, and flooded the engine. With each subsequent setback, we were mentally deducting from his pay. We were also growing anxious about missing our connecting overnight train to Varanasi. At long last, the engine turned over and we slowly moved off out of the pond with sighs of relief. We arrived at the station a few hours later and I purchased a dusty packet of crisps, as it was clearly all we would have to eat for the journey. Thus ‘enriched’ we left for the absolute nightmare that is the Varanasi railway station. I hate these train stations at the best of times and today was no exception. We were surrounded by a pressing mass of people pushing into us, grabbing at our sleeves, begging for money and aggressively trying to sell half-rotten fruit. It was a nightmare made infinitely worse by the sheer volume of heavy luggage we had to carry. The stench was awful; human and animal excrement decorated the tracks. 

The Express to Varanasi 

We discovered we were sharing our sleeper compartment with a massive Indian family, comprising parents, their many offspring and a set of grandparents. We nodded politely to everyone and immediately set about shoving our mountain of luggage onto the top bunk in order to clear some space to move around. Col put down the middle bunk to get some sleep, and Tim squeezed in next to me on the lower mattress. It was a seething, hot mass of eating, drinking, chatting, running, pushing humanity with a deafening noise level to boot. Col, unable to sleep in the commotion, sat in a sort of semi-crouched position on his narrow bunk, looking thoroughly miserable. We rubbed knees and elbows familiarly with strangers and wondered if our sanity was going to last the journey. Our packet of crisps didn’t so I set about creating some makeshift plastic cups of baby porridge utilising our bottled drinking water and milk powder under the interested gaze from our fellow passengers. The resultant gruel looked grey and thoroughly unappetising, but it was a true measure of Col’s hunger that he ate the lot. I felt and looked incredibly grubby; my fingernails were black-rimmed with train muck and my hair had taken on the stiff consistency of wild grass. I sighed and tried to clean my nails surreptitiously with a toothpick. We crossed over the famous Ganges River which seemed a bit low to me. There were many floating islands of silt dotted with basic mud-and-wattle huts—presumably temporary residences until the heavy summer monsoon arrived. I noticed the local people used dried water- buffalo dung as cooking fuel instead of burning precious forest wood; an excellent conservation effort. There was a lovely-looking Indian woman on board travelling with three young children, aged between two and four years old. They seemed to have worn her out completely for she slept heavily for most of the journey while her long-suffering husband ran up and down the train corridor after the kids for the entire trip. 

The Broth of Allahabad

The train stopped for a while at the Allahabad Junction. I whiled away the time watching station hawkers selling their wares on the platform below. Two vendors in particular were doing a roaring trade, especially a fruit seller named Mr Ali, who was selling fresh watermelons. He kept up a continual, rhythmic cry of: ‘Watermelon! Buy cheap!’ Whenever a customer approached the cart, a rapid-fire exchange of Hindi would take place. A quick selection of a ripe watermelon was followed by plonking it onto an ancient set of iron scales; after a quick glance at the weight the melon was wiped off, deftly quartered with a large blade and the juicy innards scooped out with his huge, meaty paw. Any extra bits were casually chucked into an overflowing wooden bucket nearby. A quick wipe of his blade on an indescribably filthy towel followed before melon and money changed hands. The entire process was repeated dozens of times with equal dexterity and speed. Right next to this cart stood two young men of questionable intelligence, slack-mouthed with the effort of cognition. One moved a reed table of peeled cucumbers around the platform, while the other continually flicked dirty water over the vegetables from a bucket to keep them from drying out in the heat—a veritable broth of micro-organisms. After a customer selected a cucumber, the vendor deftly sliced down the middle, ran a knife coated in salt and red chilli powder down the length of the vegetable before completing the process with a quick wrap in old newspaper. I wondered idly how many people fell extremely ill after consuming any of this stuff. The truth is that most local people in India don’t fall ill from consuming food or water contaminated with endemic microbes because their immune systems have developed a long-term tolerance to the local flora. It is the unwary traveller who must avoid such raw offerings at any cost. As we descended from the carriage onto our final platform, staggering under the immense weight of our bags and suitcases, the nightmare unfolded afresh. The platform was a seething mass of humanity in various states of physical decay—a bustling, shoving, milling, watching throng. It was quite frightening and I stood with Tim and the first pile of suitcases in a haze of total exhaustion. Col headed back into the carriage for the second load and then amazingly, a man appeared in front of me holding up a neat cardboard placard that read: Dr Mostert. I nodded happily and could have kissed him with relief. It was our hired limo driver and lodge owner. They were worried we wouldn't find our way to them or locate a hotel in the chaos, so decided to personally fetch us from the station. The driver helped us carry our pile of stuff to a hotel located not too far away. This was officially our last day in India before heading off across the border into Nepal.
