# Chapter 41: Korea 2023

I hate leaving Fish and Chips when I go away; my kitties always know when I am leaving and inevitably I come in to find one of them lying in my suitcase. I manage to spend half an hour gazing into the sunlight (as per the Huberman protocol) with my cup of tea. Fish hangs onto my rucksack strap as I leave so I unclaw him and set him down with a hug.
My Uber driver is Oliver and we chat the entire way to the airport. I have already checked in online, so I don’t have too long to queue. I am pleased to see I have three seats to myself but can’t sleep as it is a day flight to Dubai. On arrival, I find Tim sitting in Starbucks, where he has been for thirty-eight hours. He was in Germany visiting friends and enjoying the Beer Fest, so he flew all night without sleeping. We are on the same plane to Korea but in different seats. We land at Incheon Airport at five, local time and shuffle off the plane into the instantly recognisable airport from the Korean series I have The train takes us into central Seoul. Luckily, Tim has some Won notes from a previous visit, so he buys my ticket as well. The trip takes an hour. 
A very long escalator leads from the station to the road above; long enough for me to have time to chat to a young Korean girl on the way up. She helps me keep my huge case steady and I tell her about watching Korean series at home. Our Airbnb is a self-check-in, but we struggle a bit with the keypad. The room is one of the smallest I have ever seen. It has two single beds on either side with only enough space to move between them if you walk sideways. A shelf sits above each bed, a small bar fridge a foot away from the end of the bed and four hooks on the wall near the door are for clothes. The en-suite shower and toilet may as well be one unit, as you can’t shower without wetting the toilet and basin. Anyway, it will suit our purpose. We change into clean clothes (me in the shower, as there's no room for both of us to stand), then head out into the cool evening for supper.
After a short walk down the street we come across a restaurant called ‘No Beer, No Work’. It’s a strange name for what turns out to be a fried chicken restaurant. It’s small but cosy and we order fried chicken and tteokbokki, which is a dish of spicy, chilli-flavoured, cylindrical rice cake noodles. We share a small jug of the local beer. The meal is delicious but we’re both exhausted, so we pay, and I take pleasure in saying, ‘gamsahabnida’ (thank you), to the waiter. I learnt a few words from watching Korean series at home.
I’m sitting at a small cafe, coffee in hand looking out of the window to the slowly awakening street. Korea looks similar to Japan in its general infrastructure; lots of dangling telephone wires, narrow streets, small apartments and colourful neon signs everywhere, advertising anything from drinks to boy bands. I walk further, exploring the area, until I come to a statue in black marble of a huge woman balancing a ball.
Tim and I walk  over a wide bridge on the Han River. I don’t see a lot of greenery, just cement with occasional squares of grass. Helicopters and drones fly overhead, picnickers are sitting on rugs on the cement pathways, between bushes and even between boat trailers. I ask a small group of youngsters what is going on. They tell us tonight is the annual fireworks competition, which is a popular event in the city. The display starts at eight and it is only six now, so we continue walking around, getting a feel for the place. There’s a good spot for a drink off the pavement walkway so we choose a small table with the idea we can wait here until it is time to find a place to watch the fireworks. Tim says somewhere on the bridge would give us a better view, so we head there after an hour and take up our positions among the already increasing crowd. Parts of the main road have already been closed off to traffic.
The first display shoots off into the sky just after eight and it’s truly spectacular. There are cascades of colour, beautiful shapes and amazing designs. A lot of effort has been put into each display. I don’t know who wins the competition, but they all deserve a medal in my opinion. We watch for over an hour, then, as the displays begin to dwindle, we leave to avoid the crowds and walk back slowly to the train for our Airbnb, called, appropriately, ‘Tiny House’.
After coffee, we walk to a place for ‘breakfastlunchsupper’ as we woke so late, it could be any of them. Tim chooses a Japanese restaurant this time. Our soba noodles are slurped up in the appropriate manner to indicate our enjoyment. Later a train takes us to Seogyo-dong, another part of the city. Large groups of people are milling about in their Sunday best with youngsters walking in groups of two and three wearing the latest fashion. Street dancers have set up a circle with a loudspeaker and some music and take turns ‘in the ring’, showing off their moves while a crowd gathers around them. This is a popular event in Seoul. Some are of the, ‘shrug the jacket off and on’ variety of dance, others involve a lot of leg work.
I book a flight to Jeju Island. I’ve always wanted to go to there after watching a series on Netflix called ‘Hyori’s Bed and Breakfast’. I loved that series, which shows a celebrity couple, Lee Hyori and Lee Sang-soon, opening their home as a bed and breakfast. The show, which has two seasons, features celebrity guests and offers a glimpse into the couple's life and interactions with their guests.
The restaurant we choose for supper goes by the unlikely name of ‘Let’s Get Naked’. Luckily for us, (and possibly others), this is not necessary and we manage to order fried chicken and a (very) pale ale without removing any item of clothing.
Jeju Island
Tim wants to visit Yeouido-dong. The day is cool but clear, with a bright blue sky. This is more of a business district and unlike the place we are staying (which is a bit ‘below the railway line’), or the other more hipster spots, this area is pristine and bright, with huge skyscrapers of business offices. Men in dark suits and ties rush to and from their work and transport, while women wearing smart suits and heels dash around in a similar fashion. We find a great place for work; it is full of books and laptops everywhere. I have a very tasty squid ink roll. Afterwards, back in the ‘petite’ room, we pack our rucksacks for our weekend in Jeju, leaving the rest of our clothes in Tiny House. We have just enough time to take the train to the other airport at Gimpo. Then, having rushed, find there’s still time after check-in to work a bit on our respective laptops. A small plane takes us to Jeju Island by five-thirty. From the airport, we take a taxi to ‘Guest House Brick’, which is well-named because as we pull up, it is indeed a square, red brick building. Our room is also very small but has a better shower than the one in Seoul. After dumping our rucksacks we head to a nearby restaurant with great reviews. It’s busy, but we get a table and order ‘Beef Tartare Hot Stone Pot’ bibimbap with five vegetables.It takes longer to say it than cook it as the meal arrives soon after, sizzling with many side dishes, as is the custom; daikon, kimchi, spinach, asparagus, pumpkin, aubergine, and a rice dish. A miso soup is served afterwards. We share a small bottle of soju—the Korean equivalent of the Japanese ‘sake’. Both are made from fermented rice wine.
Today, the bus takes us to the southern part of the island, called Seogwipo-si. There are a lot more forested areas the higher we climb and it feels quite chilly at the top of the mountain. The bus descends into the village of Seogwipo, the second largest on the island. It’s also built up with sky scrapers so it’s hard to believe you’re on an island. Jeonbang Waterfall is very pretty, with a river, colourful autumnal bushes, and flitting butterflies. A Grey Heron suns itself on the banks of the river. The falls cascade from flat grey rocks to a green river below.
After buying a couple of onigiri (the seaweed-wrapped rice snack that’s favoured in Japan and Korea), we walk to the Jeongbangpokpo Falls, where a notice reads that the waterfall is linked to the tragic ‘Jeju 4.3’ Uprising in 1948, -the chaotic period following Korea’s liberation from Japanese colonial rule in 1945. The peninsula was divided into a Soviet-occupied North and a US-occupied South.During the uprising, civilians caught up in the conflict were executed near the falls, with bodies disposed of over the waterfall. I stare into the water for a few minutes with vivid thoughts of the massacre flitting through my brain.
The Seobok Exhibition Hall is nearby and features artefacts related to the historical figure Seobok, including a bronze carriage and replicas of terracotta soldiers and sculptures from the Mausoleum of the First Qin Emperor. We take a look inside. There are some life-size terracotta soldiers on display. Seobok is said to have come to Jeju Island in search of the miraculous plant "Bulrocho" for the longevity of Emperor Qin of China. It doesn’t say whether he found it or not. 
Back in the apartment Tim says the air conditioner sounds like Shadow licking his balls. Shadow was our old pavement special dog who we loved, even when he was old and smelly.
Today, we walk through another park, find another small museum and pop in for a look. As I’m walking around the building, I find a brown and gold snake lying in the path and almost have a heart attack. It seems quite sluggish, so I just pass it very quickly; one could say I levitate over it. 
After passing over a bridge that has a number of carved, Pinocchio-type wooden creatures carved into the side. I try to find out the location of the house that was used in the series for ‘Hyori’s Bed and Breakfast’ and see it is in Sogil-ri. Unfortunately, we don’t have time to visit it which is quite disappointing for me but then I remember that they don’t live there anymore anyway.
Lunch is eaten in an unassuming fish restaurant where we’re given huge paper bibs and a pair of scissors, for cutting the big fish. There are twenty different fish to choose from and we go for sea bream, which is accompanied by pickled vegetables, loads of rice, seaweed, and daikon. The bream is delicious, if a little bony. After this huge repast, we decide to walk up the highest point in Jeju—Hallasan, a shield volcano. Its summit is 1,947 m (6,388 ft). It’s heavy going on my knees with my heavy rucksack, but the view at the top is worth it. There’s a panoramic view of the city, sea, and a pale yellow sunset. We gaze out at this for some minutes while I catch my breath. 
The light is fading as we make our descent to the Dongmun night market, where vendors sell a variety of food and souvenirs at reasonable prices. The market is huge, busy and full of tiny stalls and open wooden tables selling mostly oranges, dumplings, fish, bean cakes, and biscuits. My feet feel like two tenderised steaks at the end of legs of jelly, but it is nice wandering around and sampling local fare. I come across the galchi, or hairtail fish, that I’ve heard so much about when watching my Korean series. Another popular fish is pollock that is wrapped in thread, which is supposed to bring, ‘good fortune from the spirits to the extent of its length’. Pollock is a relatively large fish that is caught in abundance and for this reason, it symbolises abundance and fertility. There are baskets of fried squid and fried chicken is cooked while the sellers dance to music from a booming loudspeaker. Huge black-shelled crabs (advertised as ‘Pride Crab’) lie glistening on a table.
Today we board the small plane back to Seoul, sitting next to a grumpy old man who complains to the air steward that we are talking too loudly who in turn politely asks us to be quiet. I have never been asked to speak quietly on a plane before as usually I’m the one complaining (albeit in my head).
Our tiny room is really untidy and there are dirty clothes strewn all over the beds, so I do a wash and meet Tim for coffee while he works before taking a break to discuss the intense phone-gazing here. Everyone has their head bent and is scrolling or gazing at their phones, even while walking. I have to move out of the way several times to avoid a collision. We watch in morbid fascination as a young woman crosses a busy road and doesn’t look up once. It can’t be good for their mental health. I realise that they are hardly the only people that do so—back in South Africa, I am constantly skipping out of the way of phone-gazers. 
Today, we visit the National Museum of Modern Art, which houses a collection of around 10,000 artworks. It is quite extensive and takes a few hours to see all the exhibits. Next is a walk to the Jongno-gu district, to an old, traditional village in Seoul that offers a glimpse into traditional Korean life. The village is filled with hanoks, which are traditional Korean houses, many of which date back to the Joseon Dynasty. The wood is light, unlike the darker wood I have seen in similar houses in Japan. We come across what is touted as being the ‘oldest laundry stream in the palace area in Seoul,’ which refers to Cheonggyecheon, a restored stream that was once a part of Seoul's early sewage system. The stream was covered and replaced with an expressway in the mid-20th century but was later restored and reopened again. I gaze at it for a minute, trying to picture apron-draped women washing vigorously at mounds of wet linen, but it just looks like a drain to me. 

We come to two traditionally dressed women wearing short vivid cyclamen skirts over bright lemon yellow jackets, playing a wooden instrument called a gayageum (a plucked zither). An old man comes up to the woman with the gayageum and asks her to play something. She complies and he sings along with her. He has tears in his eyes and mine water in sympathy. He has probably been transported back in time to when he first heard the song, maybe when he first met his wife.
We’re in Itaewon, another suburb of Seoul, drinking good coffee and listening to Wolfmother. The Leeum Museum of Art is next on our agenda. The art itself doesn’t appeal to me, being too modern for my tastes but we both love the Celadon pottery from the 5th century (Joseon period), famed for its jade-green colour and smooth glaze. In our rooms, we have started watching the series ‘Kim’s Convenience’ on Netflix. It seems fitting to watch something like this while we are here, as the series  features a Korean family, albeit living in Australia, and is very amusing.
GANGNAM
The air in Gangham (meaning South of the River,) seems cleaner somehow. There arehuge skyscrapers  and high rise buildings rising from pristine pavements into the sky.  The name Gangnam is perhaps better known from the singer PSY’s song and dance, ‘Gangnam style.’  Next we visit a huge mall called ‘Coex’ that features a huge aquarium and movie house, and walk around sipping our dongcha (bubble tea) until we come to a huge open area that looks like a giant library. Vast walls are covered in shelves of books that seems to go on forever. People sit huddled over small tables reading. 
Changdeokgung Palace is the most well-preserved of the five royal palaces in South Korea. The complex is also known as Donggwol. The palace was built by the kings of the Joseon dynasty, (1392 to 1897- one of the longest ruling dynasties in the world) . The paths leading to the actual palace is lined with tall elms, looking like dancers in a ball waiting for their dance cards to be filled in their bright Autumn colours. We wander around the palace grounds which comprise of a public palace area, a royal family residence building and a large garden at the back. Known as a place of rest for the kings, the rear garden boasts a gigantic tree that is over 300 years old, a small pond and a pavilion. It’s a pleasant way to spend an hour before heading to the National Museum of Contemporary Art. 
Many of the museum works are by Chang Ucchin who is known for his oil paintings depicting Korean sceneries, animals, and children in simple and naive painting styles. A notice invites visitors to utilise the paper and pens provided and I draw a simple bird design on a twig with the word, ‘Bone’ for some pretentious reason then leave it pinned up with other visitor’s works. We wander around the Changdeokgung gardens and the beautiful lily ponds before taking a train to the Gwangjiang market; one of the oldest traditional street markets in Jongno District. 
Many stalls sell clothes but it’s mostly for the food that people visit. Plenty of uncooked seafood; many still alive, swim in large plastic containers of seawater. There’s octopus, crab, sea urchins, clams and many different fish for sale. The night is redolent with the smell and sounds of sizzling food emanating from stalls selling mung bean pancakes, fried potato and various meat and fish dishes. There’s hotteok, a type of thick pancake in an assortment of flavours and Tteokbokki and fried chicken. The owner is very busy so we grab her quickly and order sushi. The sushi is fresh and tasty but we eat quickly to make space for all the other people who are waiting for a table.
‘Ilho’ stall was filmed recently for his success story as a stall owner selling two star Michelin street food. I’d loved to have tried some but the queue is too long. A few stalls are selling Hanbok clothing; copies of traditional costumes. I wish I could afford to buy a dress but it’s too expensive and I probably wouldn’t find many occasions to wear Korean traditional dress from the 5th century at home. I already consider putting on a clean T-shirt as dressing up. On the way back, I find a store that sells ‘Gochugaro’ which is a red pepper powder and buy a kilo of it for Jo who cooks a lot with red pepper. At least I hope she does. She now has a few years supply of it.  
NAKSEONDAE
We’re on the other side of the river today in Nakseondae in the Yeouido Hangang Park which has the one of the best panoramic views of Seoul along the Han river. The park has attractions such as;  the Water Light Square, waterside fountains, a riverside stage and a yacht marina, making it a popular spot in Seoul for tourists and residents alike. 
Back in our tiny room, I’ve lost a pair of panties behind the bed headboard. I try to retrieve them using various kitchen implements from the hostel kitchenette, but lose a pair of kitchen tongs in the process and give up for the time being. I figure the maids will have a field day trying to work that one out!
We’re in Yongsan-gu for a typical Korean BBQ meal. There’s an entire wall inside the busy room made from empty green bottles in The Maple Tree. Each table has a copper grill and chimney. It is rated one of the best in Seoul so we have high expectations. A woman hurries up to us, lights the small grill and takes our order. She brings all the usual accoutrements; rice, pickled vegetables, soy pieces, and a plate full of lettuce leaves. She explains that we are to place morsels of cooked meat into the lettuce leaves and add various veg and pickles and eat it as a ‘lettuce sandwich’. A novel idea I think. She places various pieces of beef and chicken on the grill and returns periodically to turn the meat until its ready. After taking a taxi back to the hostel, we stop briefly in a 7/11 to buy a large biscuit (cookie) the flavour of which I use Google to translate; ‘My neighbour Tongtong’s matcha herb and’  I never did find out what comes after the ‘and’ but it’s tasty non the less. 
This morning we’re taking a walk through Seoul Forest to a coffee shop that has at first glance, a man in a hoodie hanging from the roof, but turns out to be a very lifelike, human-sized model. It’s somewhat disturbing to drink coffee with what appears to be a dead body swinging from the roof. I’m amused to see a large board displaying the following: ‘You are the egg to my bread and the breath to my life’. I may use it should the occasion arise.
When crossing a long bridge in the forest, I look down and see a fallow deer lying in the shade. I love seeing wild animals in unexpected places. There are a few more of them lying under a bush. We exit the verdant confines of the forest and suddenly there’s a range of tall buildings stretching high against the skyline. Over the Han River, the other side of the forest continues with shaded canopies of trees. A spray of wild cosmos decorates the grassy verges and the high IDZ tower stands guard in the distance. It is a glorious day and a pretty walk.
The next day, we visit the National Palace and have breakfast down a side road after which I find a hire shop for renting historical Hanbok clothing.

Hanbok consists of an upper and a lower garment. The basic set for men includes trousers, jacket, vest, and an overcoat. Accessories include belts, ankle bands, socks, and shoes. For women, the set has underpants, underskirts, outer skirts, undershirts, a short jacket, an overcoat, socks, and shoes. Accessories include norigae (pendant-like ornaments), rings, and chignon ornaments. I am excited to dress up. While Tim veers towards the men’s section, I walk downstairs and select my underskirts, outer skirts, and the tight, short jacket that is worn over the skirt and tied with a silk bow. I find some hair pearls that are fastened into the hair. My outfit is mainly pale cream decorated with pink flowers and my overcoat is a cream and pearl silk garment. It’s really pretty, and I twist and twirl in front of a large mirror before going to find Tim. He meets me at the entrance looking the part. He has blue trousers and a cream-and-gold, long-sleeved top, over which a royal-blue overcoat is worn. He has a blue-and-gold belt and a tall hat made of bamboo and horsehair, with a wide brim and high crown and is tied under the chin with a thin black ribbon. A gat was often worn by nobility during the Joseon Dynasty as a symbol of status. Tim looks good and his height enhances the royal effect. We both laugh in delight at our transformation and we saunter over to the Gyeongbokgung Palace, which was built in 1395 as the official palace of the Joseon dynasty.
Everyone else who is similarly dressed up comes to the palace to take a million photos for instagram and Tik Tok. Tim receives a lot of admiring glances from the pretty girls, who also wear long silk ribbons in their beautiful dark hair. The garden is full of colour; yellow, orange, gold, and bright green and red leaves present a palette of autumn hues. A  huge pond in the garden reflects the trees and ancient buildings on its smooth surface. As we finish our wanderings, we return to the vast entrance area where a procession of royalty and soldiers, dressed in all their finery, are gathering to re-enact a few scenes from the Joseon period. Men are dressed in long, bright red jackets and hold replicas of curved blades on long poles called hyeopdo, also known as the ‘eyebrow sword’ because of its curved blade. We watch a faux battle take place, followed by a decree from the king, then everyone disperses.

Nami Island

Today, we are visiting the Naminara Republic, or Nami Island, which is a half-moon-shaped river island in Gangwon Province. It was formed by the rising water of the Bukhan River as a result of the construction of the Cheongpyeong Dam in 1944. I am interested in going as it has appeared in many of the Korean dramas that I watch. The train takes an hour to get to Gangwon.
We arrive in the small village across from the island, which is accessible either by zipline or by a small ferry. In 2006, the Naminara Republic seceded from the rest of South Korea and proclaimed itself a ‘micronation’. As such, it has its own currency, passport, and an 'entry visa', which comes with your ferry ticket to the island. 
Tim and I have lunch in the village, at a small local restaurant before crossing to the island. It’s busy but we’re led to a table with a large frying pan in the centre. The owner; a small framed, dark-haired woman in a flowery apron, takes our order for chicken stir-fry. She brings the raw ingredients, lights the gas under the pan, pours in some cooking oil, and tosses the chicken pieces and cabbage into it then fetches a bowl of kimchi rice, which she adds to the rest. She then briskly fills our plates and leaves us to enjoy a delicious Korean meal of kimchi chicken fried rice. Afterwards we don’t have to wait long for the ferry to arrive and after climbing aboard, climb to the top deck. I watch the forested island slowly come into view. It is just over 1.6 kilometres wide and approximately 4 km in diameter, so not very big. The entrance has a huge wooden post and a sign declaring ‘immigration’.Pine, ginkgo and giant redwood trees line the pathways. 
We circumnavigate the island on a small path, fringed with gorgeous autumnal plumage. Copper and gold leaves, interspersed with red and green, turn the island into a fairytale. The river is calm as we walk around and a gentle breeze keeps us cool. We join the throngs of camera-posing visitors then have coffee and a croissant in the island’s centrally located café before continuing to traverse its breadth. I come across a Siberian chipmunk; a little mammal with brown and beige stripes and a bushy tail, not unlike a squirrel at first glance. Some black and white European rabbits are bounding along in the grass beside us. A viburnum tree with glossy, bright red berries stands over a few cattle in an open field. The Goblin Garden features humorous goblin statues, adding a touch of whimsy and character to the island. There’s a huge bronze statue of a fairly grotesque-looking mother with pendulous breasts, offering one to a child. This breastfeeding sculpture was created by a Chinese sculptor and is supposedly symbolic; the mother’s breasts represent the two main rivers that flow through China and Korea. Another stone statue shows a goblin figure lifting a baby in the air in a moment of suspended animation. A few golden orb spiders have created a large web between two Japanese maple trees. These beautiful red-and magenta-coloured trees are quite prolific now that it is autumn. By the time we’ve walked around the entire island, it has become very busy so we stand in a long line of people for the ferry. This morning we take a bus to where Tim’s new apartment is located; the Yeongdeungpo district. It’s an hour away from here so I’m glad I have a seat. Tim carries my suitcase up the zillion steps into a shopping mall.
Another of our many farewells and I wave as the bus lurches off. Tim is staying another few days.
