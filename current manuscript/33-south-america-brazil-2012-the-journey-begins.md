# Chapter 30: South America — Brazil 2012: The Journey Begins

## The Journey Begins

I was looking forward to seeing the mighty Amazon and hopefully as much Brazilian wildlife as possible. As per the norm, morning saw us stumbling around, bumping into each other like electrons — washing faces, feeding dogs and shoving several chargers into rucksacks. It was pouring with rain in Cape Town and I was trying to keep moisture of any kind from falling on my expensive Brazilian hair treatment, to no avail; a few strands of hair were already curling up in disdain.

I had an aisle seat with two Brazilians in the next seat who laughed uproariously through a Jennifer Aniston movie for almost the entire flight. I had a small glass of wine with my meal and when one of the couple needed the loo, he thoughtfully pried an empty, wine-stained plastic glass from my hair, where it was gently swinging in the breeze from the air-con that was on full blast. The flight seemed endless, so I tried to doze waking to the sound of forty-five crisp (chip) packets being prised open. We arrived in São Paulo at 4 p.m. local time. As we disembarked, warm, moist air slapped my tired face into semi-wakefulness. Customs went with relative ease then the wait in the heat for the hotel bus.

The driver was in a hurry when he arrived. My hand luggage, as mentioned previously weighed a small ton. I had just stepped on board and was in the process of adjusting the strap of my rucksack when he took off and I landed with a whump onto a startled woman’s lap. I swore, then apologised, then staggered towards my seat with Col and Tim looking on impassively. They were used to my misfortunes by now.

We were disgorged onto the hot pavement and stumbled into the hotel lobby where I got onto Wi-Fi immediately as Col had, with his usual efficiency, locked the side door at home. I needed to contact the house sitter urgently. The dogs were locked inside, increasing the urgency. I sent several text messages and he thankfully acknowledged the last one.

Col and I sat outside on the pool balcony and ordered a local beer called ‘Bohemia’. It was a pleasantly balmy evening and we clinked our glasses to the forthcoming holiday.

## Iguazu Falls, Argentina

Morning came early and we took the hotel shuttle back to the airport. We were flying to Iguazu which lies on the border between Brazil and Argentina.

After collecting our luggage we met our local guide, Patricio. He was a small, wiry man with a head of wild, curly dark hair and a ready smile. We shook hands and he led us to a bus that would take us over the border to Paraguay. The border control went really smoothly and we didn’t even have to leave the bus. The bus driver told us he was from Brazil but spoke a mixture of Portuguese and Spanish, ‘Portuñol’ if you will.

On the Argentinian side I took a photo of the Iguazu River. Patricio told us the falls have eroded twenty-five kilometres since they were discovered. A couple of coatis crossed the road; members of the raccoon family with a long snout and a thick, striped tail. A couple of chopi blackbirds were pecking something on the side of the road. After checking into the hotel, I noticed there was a great view of the falls from the balcony. We set off with Patricio and another guide, Martien, to see the upper level of the falls along a narrow path. Each view of the falls was spectacular: horseshoe-shaped with an island in the middle. Caracaras (hawk-like birds) and black vultures flew low over the falls while coatis scampered along the paths. Patricio told us Argentina used to have a huge cattle-farming culture, which had recently changed to farming soybeans. Farmers were slowly returning to cattle but it was proving difficult due to the environmental impact of both industries and a legacy of large-scale, potentially unsustainable agricultural practices.

The falls scattered a fine cooling mist over us as we walked to a restaurant called The Fortune Cataratas. I climbed the polished red cement steps into a large, open-plan area with a long row of buffet food and tried a helping of manioc.
Afterwards Patricio led us to the lower slopes where we were soon drenched despite our plastic raincoats. The views were spectacular. Boiling cascades of rainbow-tinted water. A lookout platform was a good spot to take photos whilst getting sprayed in the face, resulting in some amusing photos. Coatis scattered on the paths as we came across the old Falls hotel now reclaimed by nature and overgrown with weeds. It had only been closed since 2011 but was already derelict. Orange trees were dotted around the garden and kapok fluff lay in patches all over the ground as though emptied from a giant teddy bear. I picked up a handful; it was soft enough to make a sweater. Col called me over to look at the national bird of Argentina, a Rufous hornero. The bird itself is a fairly nondescript russet-brown and white bird but it's their nest that is interesting. A huge, oven-shaped nest made from mud and straw with various 'rooms' for eggs. It's highly resistant to various weather conditions, providing a stable environment for the eggs and chicks.

Another interesting bird we saw was the great dusky swift. They nest in large colonies clawed into the cliffs behind the falls, making nests from moss and mud-cemented pebbles. There must have been at least a hundred of them under one rock.

Tim and I left Col and the guide and returned to the hotel verandah for a local beer; a Quilmes. Col returned around 6 p.m. emerging from the exit door like a lost explorer. A man perched stiffly at the entrance of the dining room playing a harp. I ordered a local catfish called surubí for supper, washed down with some full-bodied Argentinian red wine.

## The Devil's Throat

Patricio met us around 8 a.m. with another guide; Adrian. Our small group headed to the nearby forest. It was a good day for walking being a little cold and cloudy. Col and Patricio were birding so after a while Tim and I broke away with Adrian and carried on towards the waterfall, chatting about local conservation efforts. Adrian showed us numerous exotic trees which had the bark stripped away at the base. 'This will eventually kill the tree without cutting it down', he explained. Felling trees in the park is forbidden. Small groups of capuchin monkeys swung around the trees and an agouti (a large brownish-grey rodent the size of a rabbit) crossed the path. We continued walking to the waterfall; around seven kilometres in total while gazing down to the bottom of the gully. Adrian told me about a drink called yerba maté made from steeping dried maté leaves in hot water, resulting in a light green liquid that is commonly drunk here through a metal straw from gourd-like containers. He swore by its health-giving properties. Actually he just said it was healthy and no profanities were uttered.

Later, we took a jeep down to a large boat. A sweet young Argentinian girl told us about the jungle as we drove to where the boat was parked. She pointed out huge, thin palm trees and told us they cut out the palm heart which is enjoyed as a delicacy worldwide. At the boat we were given life jackets and a blue plastic bag for our valuables. It was a smallish boat with seating for around six people. I'd just moved into a comfortable position when we suddenly plunged forward at full throttle towards the falls as they tumbled over the rock, the spray drenching everyone on board. It was quite exhilarating. The driver manoeuvred the boat carefully so that we passed behind the curtain of water which was even more thrilling.

## The Brazilian Perspective

Later we walked with Adrian towards the Brazilian side of the falls to a small town called Foz do Iguaçu, to await boarding one of the colourful tourist buses. Ours had a colourful painting of a toucan on the side. I bought two raincoats and we took the bus to the start of the falls walk on the Brazilian side. The border crossing from Argentina to Brazil was quick and painless.

As we walked along the path, mist from the falls wet our faces. I enjoyed seeing them from a completely different perspective (as I did with the Zimbabwean/Zambian views of Vic Falls) then continued to the Devil's Throat. This time we were so close and the falls tumbled so powerfully on one side that the heavy mist totally drenched us. An absolute curtain of water fell from the top that was not visible to us from the other side - totally exhilarating. A rainbow slid over the foam as the sun came out and lit up the falls. We continued walking, stopping briefly to buy an aniseed tea from a kiosk; a new and interesting flavour for me. I won't be having it again.

Next morning we were looking for the ‘humming bird’ house. One of the local Argentinian women had turned her garden into a haven for humming birds by placing a significant amount of sugar water around the garden. An old wooden sign on her gate declared ‘Jardin De Los Picaflores’. A short curly-haired woman of around fifty opened the gate and welcomed us in. She and Adrian exchanged a flurry of Argentinian before leading us to the back garden where we could photograph the birds but not disturb them. The garden was small but absolutely swarming with Picaflores (hummingbirds) of all colours. She had plastic-flower based bottles of sugar water dotted all around the garden. They hung from every branch of bush and tree and the result was huge activity of tiny birds. They flew and sipped and seemed to hang in suspended animation. It was truly awesome. Some were iridescent green, blue, yellow and scarlet red, long-tailed, red-beaked, copper-bottomed and every other hue imaginable and the air was electric with flight. Col was in his element snapping away while sunlight came and went like Morse code, striking feathers and turning them into shafts of iridescence. Patricio opened a flask of maté and gave me a sip. It tasted bitter. After leaving reluctantly we thanked the woman for the opportunity and headed back to the border. My passport was filling up alarmingly with all the border crossings. The bus stopped at a place where you could see Brazil, Paraguay and Argentina simultaneously. A small group of Amerindian children sang nearby adding to the general atmosphere.

## São Paulo

Col woke me up at 1 a.m. and said the batteries had fallen out of the alarm clock. I peered groggily at the clock but everything seemed to be intact. I was now wide awake and annoyed especially when I looked over and saw he’d fallen asleep again. I dozed on and off till 3 a.m. when we all got up and dressed in the dark. After introducing ourselves to a new guide, Lucas we set off for the airport by bus. Four people joined our group. Customs at the border went smoothly again, despite there now being five of us. We learned that one couple, John and Steven in their late fifties, were
 both doctors though not practising. 
The other two; Liz and Darren, a young South African couple were joining us for this leg of our tour. 

Cuiabá is the capital and the largest city of the Brazilian state of Mato Grosso. After collecting our luggage we headed for the bus that took us to the hotel. I gazed out of the window and declared Cuiabá ‘not very interesting’. It was dirty, dusty and dry, full of trucks and skeletons of cars with ugly cement buildings. The bus came to a halt outside what looked like a hospital but turned out to be our hotel, the ‘Hits Pantanal’. We checked in and the rather clinical theme was repeated throughout. Our room had a white tiled floor, reminiscent of a giant shower. Aside from a bed, two rickety side tables, there wasn’t much else. We met everyone in the foyer and Lucas said we would meet down here again for supper, so we all trooped back up to our rooms. It was hot, dusty and humid and we were hungry so I tried to order from room service. They were ‘non comprehendo’ so I called down to reception and repeated the order for one omelette and 2 steak sandwiches.
After dropping my iPad and cracking the screen, I bound it with tape and hopped onto the billiard table that was posing as a bed.

## Mato Grosso (West Brazil)

A bus took us back to the airport to board a TAP plane (a Brazilian airline) this time. It looked to be a newish one. We were served more carbs in the form of bread, cheese and banana. I hoped my fitness levels hadn’t deteriorated too much with all the flipping carbs we were eating; there was always cake served at breakfast.

The plane touched down at Alta Floresta airport after a few hours’ flying. It was a small, busy airport where we met Lucas, our new guide for the week. He led us to a small van outside in the shimmering heat and we drove for almost two hours to the Cristalino River Lodge, stopping briefly for a short walk in search of the huge Harpy Eagle. There was a tree where one is often sighted but we weren’t in luck. The van came to a halt along a dusty road and we climbed out and walked a short distance to the Cristalino river where a small boat awaited us. The dark, silky green river had a still, quiet beauty and a gentle breeze cooled our collective foreheads with soft caresses.

The main group were keen birders so there was a lot of bird talk and excited sightings. In a small clearing in the forest we found a tall metal tower whose steel structure stood out against the verdant nature of forest. Up the narrow metal rungs to the top level which flattened out onto a large platform towering over the canopy, providing a fantastic view of the surrounding forest. Monkeys were chattering in the distance, crashing around the tree tops but they were a little too far to identify with the naked eye. John had a good pair of binoculars and said they were black-faced macaques. Marco pointed out a couple of red and yellow macaws and a red-headed parrot. It was already dark when we made our way back.

The sound of the forest was almost deafening, with chirping crickets, whistling birds, grunting animals and the crunch of dead leaves underfoot.

## Alta Florista 

We were travelling by boat to Cristalino and it was refreshingly cool sailing over a rapid where a tree hung over the river, full of small, long-nosed bats. They were so tiny they took the appearance of lichen as they clung in groups to the bark of the tree. One of my favourite sightings occurred when we came across a mother tapir walking on a path next to the river with her striped baby. Tapirs are the largest indigenous land mammal in South America. They’re similar in shape to a pig, with a short, prehensile nose trunk. Later three long, black and red caracaras, a pygmy kingfisher and two spectacled caiman made their appearance to our excited group.

The full moon made these sightings a little easier and cast pale ripples over the still, black river. After leaving the boat on the far side of the river we walked up the short, grassy bank to a bird hide with a screen in army fatigue colours and sat in a row on a long, roughly hewn wooden bench. The air was still and humid with gnats out in swarms. A coati with a reddish coat scurried by. It was completely dark by now, so we walked back to the boat by torchlight.

Back on the boat, we spent some time at the river edges looking at birds. The fish that swam past included parrotfish and piranhas.

We went for a short night walk in the section of forest behind the huts. I love animal spotting at night. There’s something magical about the light, the smells and the fun of coming across some nocturnal wild animal. We hoped to see a night monkey. I couldn’t believe it when, twenty minutes in, we actually found one curled around a branch of a large tree. Lucas identified a golden-brown Azara’s night monkey. It’s mostly grey with a patch of ochre over its small chest.

We carried on walking in single file, following the bobbing torch of Lucas in front. Our next sighting was of a hair-tailed woolly opossum. It has thick, ‘woolly’ hair which is a greyish/reddish colour. Its tail has fur about halfway up and the rest is bare. His eyes were like two green olives.

Tim and I had another sing-song session with Lucas keeping tune on his guitar. Afterwards, we returned to our rooms, taking care not to bump into the tiny jumping spiders. You have to look for a tiny speck of ‘dew’ that is captured by torchlight; these are their eyes. We avoided the huge cicadas that also jump onto your face and hair if you are taken unawares.

I got up at 6 a.m. and began stumbling around the room until I found the candle in the agouti pod and lit it then walked down the short path to breakfast. No surprises there — most of it was cake. I chose coconut cake with copious cups of watered-down coffee. It started to rain harder and a king vulture sat somewhat forlornly in a nearby Meranti tree. It had a cool black hairdo and a red wattle. The bats that were housed under the eaves were still out.
 Lucas told us over lunch that he had two small children, aged 3 and 6. I said it must be difficult being away from them for so long and he smiled a little sadly. Later we boarded the Cristalino bus for the airport. We were joined by an attractive girl and her husband from São Paulo. She said she was a marine biologist (and I had no reason to disbelieve her). En route, the bus ran over a large snake crossing the road and we stopped to have a look. It was a beautiful gold and brown tree boa. It was alive but quite damaged. We continued feeling sad and hoped that it survived, although that’s doubtful. The countryside was quite different to where we had been; mainly cattle farms and some sheep. It used to be a huge soya bean farm, but cattle were slowly taking over again.

Florista Airport was quite small, so getting through Customs wasn’t a problem. I was starting to feel a little off: achey, hot then cold. I hoped it would pass, or I’d ruin an expensive holiday. Considering the number of floating bug capsules we’d been on, it wasn’t surprising I’d catch something. It was very humid again, which didn’t help. We boarded a TRIP aeroplane for a bumpy flight to Pantanal Airport. The Pantanal in Mato Grosso do Sul, south-central Brazil, is one of the largest wetlands in the world. It was extremely hot and humid there, but I felt cold – not a great sign. After collecting our luggage we were taken to a large tour bus which would serve as our animal-viewing bus. Despite air conditioning I remained cold and shivering. darkness descended quickly, but I could see the change in terrain through the uncurtained bit, this being more open, swamp-land. A number of little wooden bridges traversed the swampy areas, although in reality these were nothing more than a few wooden planks lying across a dry riverbed, swamp or river. Some were scary in their state of disrepair so the bus driver negotiated these with extra vigilance and care.

Once it became totally dark, Lucas shone his torch and revealed a red brocket deer, white-lipped peccary, an armadillo scuttling into the high grass and a crab-eating fox.

I was now fully ill, shaking and sweating and by the time we reached the Pousada Alegre, I could barely see. We disembarked at the lodge and I quickly found Lucas and asked him to take me to my room where I had a shower then collapsed into bed, shivering with fever. Lucas brought me a blanket and a mug of maté tea. The next two days were a blur of faces, pills, and vague chatter about dengue fever. Col brought me water, and Tim peered at me with concern. Luckily John and Steven, also doctors, had brought a good supply of medicine with them, and I started a course of antibiotics. I slipped in and out of a fevered sleep for three days. On the afternoon of day three, I heard quite a racket outside en route to the loo. I could only see very dry, dusty red-soiled surroundings with one or two spindly trees. My eyes hurt, so I crawled back into bed.

## Pousada Alegre

That morning at 4 a.m. my fever broke, and I felt so much better, sleeping soundly until 6:30. Col left with the others for a game drive. I got up a bit shakily, showered and dressed and ventured outside. I had no idea where we were or what the place looked like. I had arrived in a fog of pain and fever in the dark of night. I walked towards what I hoped was the dining room for some tea and met the owner who asked how I was feeling. He told me Tim was here and showed me to his room. While waiting for Tim to finish showering, he showed me where the toco toucans were. I was sorry I had missed out on all the action and wanted to make up for it now, so I walked around the huge garden looking at birds; brown capuchin, toucans and aracari. Aracaris are small toucans which are brightly plumaged and have enormous, contrasting patterned bills.

There were some beautiful trees here which I hadn’t seen from my window. Many beautiful birds, too; toucans, red-headed manakins, a black hawk-eagle and a huge iguana with a round tail. Tim and I walked to a beautiful sea of yellow-flowered trees and saw the bus returning. Lucas leaned out of the window when he spotted me, with a huge smile and a thumbs-up sign. He was such a kind man. I met up with everyone in the dining room, and they all said they were happy to see me up and feeling better. I thanked John and Steven for the antibiotics. Col said they saw a tayra that morning; a type of weasel.

My legs were full of bruises and bites as a result of the dengue fever. I had a short walk with Col, and we saw capuchin monkeys and toucans, then a beautiful pair of blue and yellow macaws as well as a large iguana, slowly moving into the nearby bushes.

I felt a bit sad to be already leaving, illness having cheated me out of three days. We boarded the bus again and headed for Porto Jofre. Although it was only 140 km away, it took us the entire afternoon to get there. The husband of the young couple, Darryl, was also starting to feel flu-ey and I was hoping it was just flu.

I had a front seat, having missed so much until now and saw egrets, herons and cormorants and even a few capybaras, submerged in swampy water from the large window. It was very hot when we made stops, once for the toilet and to see an owl and a potoo. Potoos are nocturnal birds native to Central and South America and have exceptional camouflage, large yellow eyes, and mournful calls. By day, they mimic tree stumps or branches, becoming virtually invisible. By night, they hunt flying insects from a perch.

At the lodge, our names were pasted on the doors: ‘Timothy’, ‘Colin Patrick’ and ‘Janette Mary Van’. There were four single beds in each long, narrow room, a bit like a dormitory. Each had air-con, a fridge and, for what it’s worth, a TV. We met the rest of the tour in the dining room, which was quite full as there were two other big tour groups here as well. I walked around the grounds taking photos of birds; blue macaws, jabiru, and black-necked storks.

We were all at lunch except Col. Everyone asked me where he was. I didn’t want to say he was setting traps for butterflies, so I muttered vaguely about looking at the river and chatted to Lucas for a while then Col arrived and whispered to me that he’d caught some nice specimens.

It was nice and cool inside the bus but got progressively hotter and more humid every time we stopped. A few capybaras swam in the nearby river, a giant beehive sitting tumour-like in the fork of a large tree, jacanas, azure gallinules, and some paw prints of a jaguar.

I asked Lucas about the beautiful trees with varying shades of pink flowers, and he told me they were piúva trees. They were really gorgeous. We came across a capybara, a baby with a jacana on its head! After this there wasn’t much else to see, so we returned to the lodge. There was a boat ride on the Cuiabá River that day and the day started off cool and very pleasant, with a slight mist lying low over the water. Lucas told me that catfish create holes in river banks primarily for nesting and breeding, digging burrows in mud or dirt banks to lay eggs. These many nesting holes can destabilise the banks. I looked around at them with renewed interest.

After we spent the morning pointing stuff out to each other, Lucas told us that according to local custom it’s more polite to refer to the position of an item as being at ‘2 o’clock’ or whatever clock position is closest. Our first good sighting after an hour of sailing was at ‘3:15’; a group of giant river otters frolicking around the edges of the river. One had caught a large, round, silver fish and was tearing at it with its sharp teeth. A small troop of black howler monkeys swung noisily in the trees. I used a tin mug to pour river water over my arms and legs to keep cool then thankfully we had a toilet-stop. This entailed mooring the boat, clambering out, scrambling up the embankment and searching for a suitable, private bush. Lucas handed out small 200ml bottles of water before we continued down the river, which was also full of caimans. I photographed a big fellow lying still in the shallow edges with his yellowish jaws open. A small group of capuchins scampered in the treetops. We had another embankment stop; this time Lucas handed out cheese and ham sandwiches, bottled water and boiled eggs which we ate sitting on a large log, munching away, flicking away flies and midges that swirled around our faces in the humidity.

The little capys were adorable. They actually looked more like giant guinea pigs than rats. One of the capybara’s expressions I decided was like a British aristocrat asking for a cucumber sandwich. As we headed home through a beautiful pastel sunset, we came across a small boat on the embankment. As we cut the engines and silently came up alongside them, one of the bearded researchers told us of a jaguar in the nearby foliage, eating. We held our collective breath in excitement and a flurry of camera noises as lenses were attached and we glided up a little closer. As we approached, the stench of what turned out to be jaguar supper assailed our noses. The binoculars came out, and we tried to focus on the jaguar, who was eating the remains of a small caiman. I focused on its face for a few seconds, then its beautiful striped tail twitching in the dappled sunlight. Tim took a look and said he could see the entire cat. Col was photographing furiously. We were all in high spirits as we returned to the lodge. Seeing this beautiful animal had been at the top of all our wish lists. We celebrated with some Argentinian wine that night.

A veil of mist hung over the river like yesterday and the morning started cool and still. Aside from the giant river otter, we saw an otter that morning. Neotropical otters can grow more than three feet long and in some cases almost half of that is their tail. Their sleek shape, waterproof fur, and webbed toes, help the otter swim and hunt in the water.

As the driver negotiated the river we almost got stuck motoring down a very narrow canal carpeted with water plants and lilies – the river was almost choked with them. It was a hot, dank, humid, fly-infested wait while the boatman tried to disentangle river foliage from the motor. I watched a pretty lily trotter while we were waiting. He eventually freed up the engine (the boatman not the lily trotter — he’s useless), whistling all the while, and gently backed out of the canal. The sun gently slipped into the sea, casting red, orange and yellow streaks. We were all sitting quietly, wrapped up in our thoughts, when suddenly the boatman yelled,

‘Jaguar!’

and we all sprang to action and cast our eyes over the riverside, bringing binoculars up to view and preparing the cameras.

‘Where?’

I asked after a few minutes of scanning, then suddenly I saw it: a lone jaguar right on a slip of beach sand. It was drinking from the river. We cut the engine and came closer until I could see the spots on its tail. It was a magnificent specimen. It slowly lapped up water with its huge pink tongue before slowly sloping off into the bushes. The group was overjoyed with the sighting, but as we continued back down the river, the torch picked up not one but two more jaguars. These were two young males lying on their backs, yawning and grinning at each other. We watched in silence until they rose and slowly wandered off out of sight. I took a deep breath. The boatman motored back in silence as everyone digested the majesty of these beautiful cats. We were sailing over the dark, silky water when someone shouted,

‘Snake!’

I turned to find a long, dark snake with a yellow tail undulating past us in the water and up into a tree that was hanging over the river. It was a Caninana, Lucas told us, a type of non-venomous snake often mistaken for a cobra.

It stayed cool that morning, thankfully and as we rounded the edge of the forest near Fisherman’s Corner, we came across a jaguar lying in the shade. There were two other tourist boats in front of us, so we couldn’t see much at first. Eventually, it moved into the bushes and the boats took off. The boat edged closer, the motor cut and we waited. Our patience was rewarded when the jaguar returned, walking slowly past us along the edge of the forest.

The bus rumbled slowly across the Trans Pantaneira highway over the many rickety wooden bridges towards the Mato Grosso Hotel which lies close to the Pixaim River. After alighting, we stretched our legs and checked in. Our room was very basic and hot as the air-con was not working. Lucas got them to fix it while we had supper which was a small mug of piranha soup which tasted quite nice — a bit like… chicken. Supper consisted of manioc, rice, red beans, corn salad, meat and fish. That morning the group set off in the bus towards the airport. En route, a couple of roseate spoonbills dabbed at the shallow water of a large pond which was a seething soup of caimans. A black vulture was eating pieces of fresh caiman from a tree. There was a crab-eating raccoon, pampas deer, and red brocket deer. We left the Trans Pantaneira and stopped for an ice cream, bought by Lucas who handed them out like passports. We were in a typical backwater area with car and lorry carcasses, rusting parts, and tyres lying everywhere. Pistachio-coloured houses were built close to the road. Later we came to flat grasslands dotted with tall, thin palm trees and stopped for petrol. We arrived at the small airport and said goodbye to John and Steve as they were heading back to the States. Fond farewells from the rest of the group as our various flights came up. We were the last to leave. At 5 p.m. we got up and stood in the queue.

The officials announced that the systems were down, so it was absolute chaos. Everything had to be handwritten, a laborious task that took three times as long, but at last we were in and standing at customs where we met another couple who had also been to Cristalino. They said they saw a puma; another cat I’d love to see. The plane was delayed by a further hour and a half before boarding. After landing, and grabbing our luggage off the carousel, we made our way through customs. A stocky man was holding up a sign that read, ‘Indris Tours. Mostert’. We claimed the title and shook hands. He spoke only a little English, resulting in a lot of head-nodding and smiling. Once on board the small bus, I reclined the seat and gave Tim a head massage until he dozed off. The trip from the airport to Campo Grande took a few hours, so we all had a snooze.

It was dark when we woke up and asked for water. The driver nodded towards a cooler box behind the passenger seat. He unnerved me by watching the removal of said water instead of the road in front. At around 11:30 p.m. we turned off onto a dirt track through cattle country towards the Baiazinha Lodge, which is in the Nhecolândia Ecological Reserve.

Just inside the gates, we were surprised and pleased to spot four giant anteaters who walked stiffly past us; a mother, two males and a baby. We watched them waddle into the nearby bush and out of range of the driver’s torch. It was after midnight, we were beyond hungry and I just wanted to curl up in a comfy bed. We were met by a tall, lanky fellow with an earnest expression. It may have been concern – we all looked travel-weary and crumpled.

He thrust his hand out and introduced himself as our guide, Diogo. We stumbled after him as he showed us to our rooms with a glass of juice and a piece of cake. We were to be up early tomorrow but ended up fumbling around in our luggage to prepare for the early start. It was almost 1 a.m. before we collapsed into bed and folded the sheets around us.

As a result, I was freezing for half the night. The air-con was set to ‘polar’ degrees, and I was too tired to get up to look for a blanket.

The three of us were up just after 6 a.m., dressed quickly in the cool morning air and went down to the dining room for a quick breakfast. There was a lovely view of the wetland from our room and a large white hammock beckoned from the balcony. A buffet was laid out in the dining room and we piled up our plates and found a table. There were only two large wooden tables, and we found one with three seats. The other occupants introduced themselves: a middle-aged professor and his attractive wife, and a honeymoon couple. She was a very tall, thin, pale Portuguese girl and was at least a head and shoulders taller than her short, Asian husband, who also appeared to be twenty years older than her. However, they gazed into each other’s eyes during breakfast, whispering and giggling and held hands throughout the duration of the meal. After wandering outside and heading for the large, open-seated truck we climbed aboard for a game drive. Diogo appeared, fresh and beaming. Soon after setting off we came to four capybaras close to the lodge. Two were submerged in a small pond. A few kilometres further, Diogo stopped and pointed out a beautiful hyacinth macaw nesting in a tree at the side of the road. Along the way, we saw a common potoo, a jabiru (a large white stork with a black head), a huge river turtle, peccaries and Tim spotted a yellow armadillo. It stood up and peered at us quizzically before turning around and rustling into the grass.

The driver stopped at the Research Office, where we all piled out and were led to a small room with a number of plastic chairs laid out. A nervous, dark-haired woman gave us a short talk in English on the macaw project. She said we were her first English-speaking audience. I went for a short snooze but ended up sleeping until 3 p.m., waking with a corduroy cheek and stiff hair. So, I only had half an hour to make myself look presentable before we took the open truck on an afternoon drive, stopping at the edge of a huge, leafy pine forest. Diogo said we would have a short walk. Someone spotted a family of howler monkeys sitting in the branches of a large jaboticaba tree. These are the loudest of monkeys, with a distinctive howl that alerts others to their presence and their territory. I was fascinated by the leaf-cutter ants and watched a long line of them carrying away some green foliage. They expertly cut leaves into smaller pieces with their sharp mandibles. These are used to make the mould which feeds their babies. Leaf-cutter ants have very specific roles in taking care of their ‘fungal’ garden and dumping the refuse.

During a short stop in a clearing, Diogo handed us each a small plastic cup attached to a keyring. We were to keep them as souvenirs, he said, as he poured some much-needed water into them. Tim and Col handed me their cups, so I attached them to my belt and carried on, looking like an Irish tinker. Everyone was walking very slowly – especially the honeymoon couple, who had barely untangled themselves from each other – so Tim and I walked on ahead.

That evening we met a South African ranger called Lawrence who was working on the Jaguar Project. We chatted over supper (piranha soup and fish). He said he’d show us around tomorrow night. Col asked Diogo if we could go on a night drive. He agreed, so we donned some warm tops and set off half an hour later in a small, open-seated jeep. It wasn’t long before we came across a small jaguar walking along the pathway. It was very exciting, and more so when a little further on, its mother padded softly out of the bush to join her year-old cub. We watched in silence, save for the sound of clicking cameras and measured breathing then sat back, well satisfied with the evening’s viewing. A little further along, we came across a few peccaries, then a tapir and a crab-eating fox, which are omnivorous despite the name. Next was a sighting of a pygmy owl, its eye caught in the glow of a torch. There were two marsh deer at the edge of an inky-black pool and a caiman. It was a happy group that returned very satisfied with the evening’s viewing after ten-thirty and threw things together for tomorrow’s drive before climbing into bed. I remembered the blanket this time.

After breakfast we met Diogo and waited an extra fifteen minutes for the honeymoon couple to arrive entwined and oblivious to the impatient group waiting for the morning drive. I shot them a murderous glare but they saw no one else but themselves. I wondered if they’d actually seen any animal on this trip aside from each other’s faces in close up then reproached myself for being a grumpy cow.

We found a giant anteater waddling across the road after half an hour of walking which was very exciting, then a brown capuchin in a nearby tree and many caiman off the path, waiting for the sun to get some heat onto their cold bodies. Diogo pointed out a toco toucan flying overhead. It is the largest of the toucans and has a large yellow, red and blue beak made mostly from keratin. Its entire head seems to be mostly curved beak, which despite its size is hollow and lightweight.

Back to the truck where the driver and Diogo extracted some coconut water for us from a few coconuts they’d brought in their backpacks. I had a wee behind a small bush and watched some more leaf cutters line in single file, each carrying a piece of green leaf before heading towards a small tunnel which they use to feed a vast underground network of fungus that is more nutritious for them.

Diego pointed out an ocelot which is a medium-sized spotted wild cat — and another mega tick for me. The honeymoon couple were facing each other nibbling each other’s neck so I wasn’t sure how much they saw.

As we were sharing a Bohemian beer, Lawrence arrived as promised to take us out hunting for jaguars.

It was only the four of us and Diogo so we were both excited and privileged. He told us that his main objective was to habituate the cubs of jaguars to the vehicles not the humans to allow for easier, safer tracking. He had collared five jaguars so far and had created some rough dirt roads in the forest; and took us on one of them now. Lawrence shone his torch on some jaguar tracks in the dirt road.

‘They walk by bringing their back legs to the front and keep their claws intact,’ he explained.

‘They also eat cattle by grabbing at the neck and tearing into the animal from the neck to the shoulder. Lions and tigers, by contrast, attack the flanks and belly of their prey.’

Lawrence said he had already tracked a carcass earlier and said he was sure the jaguar would be there tonight. Col and I turned to each other with excitement; this would be the highlight of our trip.

As we drove further up the dirt track, we saw a nightjar sitting in the middle of the road and continued further into the forest for an hour until we came across a huge bull carcass under a couple of short thorn bushes. The mother jaguar and her cub were lying here. I took a big breath, what a rare and privileged sight. We watched intently, taking numerous photos in between. She seemed oblivious to the torch light and click of cameras and rolled onto her back for a while then onto her side where she appeared to fall asleep. The cub playfully rolled around for a bit next to his mother. Neither seemed affected by the presence of the vehicle which was great for Lawrence, as that was his intention. We watched them for ages. Lawrence said the time we spent here was a third of the time he had watched jaguars in seven months. Col and I were very happy and delighted with the rare opportunity to have seen the jaguar in her own world and returned to the camp ecstatic at having seen both an ocelot and jaguar in one night.

Diogo led us to a nearby shed that housed seven horses. He told us we were going to ride through the pampas on horseback. This sounded amazing and I was all ears as he provided a cursory explanation on how to mount the saddle and ride. I’d had that privilege a few times so carried on with the business of getting my foot in the stirrup and over the saddle. Tim was quite miffed that he’d been given one of the smaller steeds but he was assured that his horse was strong. As Tim put his foot in the stirrup, his horse moved causing him to hop around with one foot caught in the stirrup. He quickly christened his nag,

‘Cheeky Bastard’!

Col looked a bit nervous at first but soon relaxed.

I had a large black and white horse. He looked very sturdy so I kicked gently into his midriff and we set off in single file over the swampy pampas.

My horse liked to eat and stopped frequently to bend his head and nibble on grasses and palm leaves. Unfortunately my stirrups were too short so my feet were at an odd angle which meant they became numb after a while. When we stopped, I called out to Diogo and he adjusted them for me. Clopping along the tall pampas grass in single file and contented silence, we came to a marshy area which the horses naturally handled well. I stopped for some water and removed my pink plastic mug from my belt and Diogo trotted up and poured water from the five litre bottle he had in his backpack into the mug. It was a two-hour ride in all before returning to their stables. I needed some assistance getting off the horse and everyone laughed at my bow-legged walk. All I needed was a stetson, a holster and some pointy boots.

Lunch was a delight on the senses. Baked Pachu fish caught fresh that morning, manioc and corn bread with cheese, a variety of salads and a decadent chocolate mousse with coffee. We all staggered happily away from the table and I decided to go for a walk in the 40°C heat to look for capybaras. They were on the opposite side of the pond, two adults, one adolescent and five babies. I watched them for some minutes. They’re really just giant rats or guinea pigs.

To amuse myself I recalled the different type of fish I’d eaten so far in Brazil; the three ‘P’s namely, red-bellied piranha, Pachu and Pintado; all of them very tasty.

As it was still very hot, Diego drove to the Paraguay river where it was a little cooler. Near the Cayman crossing, the driver suddenly stopped and pointed. It was a large collared male jaguar. His coat was much darker than the ones we’d seen before. I leaned out and snapped away at this magnificent cat then watched it for ten minutes until he disappeared into the forest. Buoyed with excitement, we drove on to the river stopping at one of the cattle farms where the Pantaneros (cowboys) farm cattle and tan hide. A small lean-to shed with a tin roof sat to one side and housed the tanned hides. A number of narrow wooden canoes bobbed gently at the river edge. Diogo hopped off the truck, turned to us with a smile and said we were going canoeing.

I was horrified to hear we were each to take a canoe and row around the river. I may not have mentioned this, but I swim like the proverbial rock, i.e. not at all.

Not my fault, my mum was terrified of water and so was my gran so the fear of drowning gene was successfully passed down, together with red hair, freckles and well-developed calves. I told Col under no circumstances was I going to bob around in what amounts to a narrow plank by myself. He said he was going to row anyway so we chose the biggest canoe and gingerly lowered ourselves and Col’s heavy camera bag into the narrow boat. With Col and his bag already weighing the maximum weight, the canoe submerged deeper than I was comfortable with into the green, glassy depths of the river. I lowered myself in on trembling limbs. Col took up the oars and we wobbled off with Tim in his own canoe and the others in pairs. Col handed me an oar saying he needed a free hand to hold onto his camera. This was a fair request but at one point we were going in circles because I was rowing one way and Col the other. Eventually I relaxed sufficiently to admire the beautiful sunset as it streaked reds and yellows across the darkening sky. We stopped rowing so we could photograph the surroundings. Col accused me of shaking the canoe; he didn’t want his camera bag ending up in the river. I was miffed he hadn’t considered his wife’s body at the bottom of the river in the same circumstances and maintained a tight-lipped silence. We watched a cowboy herd his cattle across a bridge built for this purpose. Diogo later told us that it had soil and grass covering it. We took up the oars again and rowed closer to the bridge. Each time there was a difference in paddle stroke, the narrow canoe wobbled and I froze. We rowed under the bridge and waved at Tim who was rowing smoothly as though he’d been doing it all his life. As we turned and canoed back, closer to the edge of the river a cauldron of fishing bats rose like a dark cloud from the river edge and skimmed their way over the water searching the smooth surface for ripples that indicate the presence of fish. It was a lovely sight against the silver water and setting sun.

We rowed slowly back to shore and I emerged shakily from the canoe, pleased to feel ‘terra firma’ under my sandals. With dusk came thousands of mosquitoes and I was instantly bitten all over, including my bottom which I tried not to scratch. Col showed me some of his photographs on his digital camera. They came out beautifully.

The group ordered a bottle of wine and we toasted each other loudly. Lawrence asked Col to write a review on his experience. Apparently we were the first people to have used the road since he ‘created’ it and he said he may name it after us — I imagined something like Mostert’s Meander. He and Col discussed the prospects of another trip to see snow leopards.

Marcos, our guide from Bonito, arrived in a much smaller vehicle than usual, but thankfully our luggage fitted.

He was a pleasant chatty man with an American-accented English. He pointed out things of interest to us as we drove through the grassy plains to a more bushy vegetation that morphed into open plains with tall trees. We stopped to take a look at a small cachoeria (waterfall); the Saltos del Monday. Marcos insisted on taking us down to get a closer look, stopping on the way down at an old cattle ranch for a cold drink. There was a swimming pool there filled with fish. There were nearly 900 steps down to the waterfall and I quickly computed that ergo, there’d be 900 steps back up under the baking sun. The falls were really nice but there were hundreds of tourists milling around. Col had a quick swim in the pool below the falls with his snorkel. A few small waterfalls spilled gently over the rocks en route where we stopped to catch our breath.

Col asked Marcos if he could find a shop that sold water wings. I was horrified thinking for a moment that these were for me and I protested loudly, but it turned out he wanted to attach his underwater camera to water wings to help keep them afloat. I should know by now that most items Col buys will have something to do with his many hobbies. He once returned from shopping with a beautiful crystal bowl. I was touched and thanked him for a lovely present before he shook his head in surprise and said it was for his butterflies. For those interested; he glued a round piece of polyurethane to the bottom of the bowl, pinned some butterflies to this and placed the bowl over a shallow dish of water. This has the effect of softening the wings, thereby making it easier to set the butterflies. I admit that knowing this didn’t ease my disappointment at not having the bowl for my dining room table. Col is a real character and there’s never a dull moment.

I came across an isolated dress shop where dusty, rumpled clothing hung limply off lifeless mannequins. A dumpy, rather forlorn-looking woman sat in a corner staring out of the window. I gave her a smile and a wave which she returned with some surprise. There were various open-fronted shops selling all manner of mechanical spare parts for presumably bicycles and cars. Hens strutted around the edges of the dusty road with fluffy grey-striped chickens in their wake.

It got hotter and hotter and soon there was enough heat radiating from my flushed face to melt an ice cube. Speaking of ice cubes, I wished I had some, preferably in a tall glass with a slice of lemon. I decided to trudge back to the lodge and order a coffee (mad dogs and Englishmen and all that).

Back at the lodge I asked the waiter for,

‘Leite fervido e água.’

Actually I was trying to say I wanted coffee with milk but it came out in the wrong word order. The waiter looked mystified,

‘Boiled milk and water Señora?’

I realised his English was better than my Portuguese and asked for hot coffee with milk, although I threw in a ‘por favour’ at the end. It was really too hot to think of how to speak Portuguese when I only know the bare minimum, I thought, fanning myself with one of the decorative palm leaves.

We chatted to a small group of Americans who arrived earlier. They rather annoyingly kept referring to the number of ‘crocodiles’ they’d seen. I replied that I also love the ‘Cayman’ and told them that their glowing orange-red eyes at night have been their undoing, showing up in torch light and many are killed that way. They said they hadn’t seen any jaguars but were pleased with the sighting of a crab-eating fox. One woman, a hard-nosed leftist, replied that people need jobs to eat when we mentioned the incredible devastation left by loggers in the Amazon forest. I replied that I saw the vast damage as a result of logging, from the plane and we reached an impasse.

Marcos arrived in a new car with another guide to take us to the Rio de Prato Eco Reserve. He stopped at an old cattle ranch that now served as a meeting point for all visitors who either snorkelled or dived or river-rafted down the river. The garden was large and pretty with flowering trees and colourful plants and bushes. Brightly coloured flowers were dotted around in red soil beds and a wraparound verandah with plenty of benches and tables. Col and Tim headed off to hire wetsuits and snorkels. A few busloads of tourists and small groups of wet-suited people waited to be taken down to the river in an open bed truck. After a long walk I sat at one of the many stools and had numerous little tin cups of thé Citron; a pleasant lemon-flavoured tea.

The boys returned just as I was having a conversation with a small parrot that was hopping around the table. His feathers fluffed up and he said something in Portuguese to which I replied in English. He cocked his head and his little beady eyes twinkled. A delightful conversation followed.

Col joined me for another stroll around the vast garden where we came across a yellow ground woodpecker and watched it for a while as it pecked through dry foliage.

After lunch we left for ‘Buraco das Araras’, the largest sinkhole in South America and one of the biggest in the world, it’s around 100m deep. Made from limestone and sandstone that collapsed from being so fragile. We had come specifically to view the hundreds of red and green macaws (Araras) that flew around this immense sinkhole.

Macaws nest in pairs around the red limestone walls in cracks and fissures in the cliff. Our guide told us of a story of one Silvino Jacques, who let his victims run to escape being shot, they were unwittingly running towards the vast fatal abyss. Unfortunately the macaws had also been used as target practice for shooting and so slowly disappeared. In the mid nineteen eighties, a Mr Modesto Sampaio bought part of the huge farm area and discovered the sinkhole and dirt road were part of the deal. They fished out numerous dead bodies and some cattle and restored the area. After releasing two macaws from captivity to nest there, others followed and today the entire cliffside is studded with these colourful ‘jewels’. I walked up to a lookout ledge and cautiously peered over. The bottom was filled with a huge bright green algae-filled pond.

At breakfast, I secreted some ham and cheese rolls into my bag for the journey later which, from experience, are rather lacking in sustenance. Col went into the nearby forest to retrieve his butterfly trap. Marcos and another driver we hadn’t seen before, arrived to take us to see the Grotto de Azur cave, around 14 kilometres in the opposite direction. The journey took just over an hour from Bonito. On arrival we were given hair nets and hard hats as a safety precaution. Tim and I took silly photos of each other with our hairnets which curled around our hair to look, for all the world, like small furry animals on the top of our heads. There were two hundred and sixty-nine steps down to the beautiful azure cave pool. As we descended, I noticed the many stalactites, stalagmites reaching out towards each other, columns and calcite cascades on the slimy cave walls. The intense blue of the underground lake was best seen through a short window when the sun was directly overhead that turns the colour from a dark blue to an almost iridescent pale blue colour. As I ventured carefully down the cold, moist, stone steps, it started to feel cooler, although humidity was still high. Algae grew along the horizontal planes in its search for light. We walked down to the edge of the water with the group and marvelled at its clarity and colour. I asked if anyone knew how deep it was. The professor said he wasn’t sure of the depth but it was 50 metres in diameter. Apparently many fossils have been found in the cave dating over 12,000 years.

Carlos arrived in a Fiat with a trailer for our luggage and we set off for the airport, some five hours away.

After some time driving, Carlos turned round to tell us that, in the old days, foreigners ‘took over the land’, created false registration papers and stored them in a drawer where a special type of cricket lived.

‘After a week or so, the crickets ate the corners of the paper and the paper turned a yellow colour, leading to a very effective “old paper” effect,’ he continued.

Quite ingenious really.

Campo Grande has a population of over a million, half of them appeared to be gathered around the airport. They sat in large groups, on deck chairs, with plastic tables laden with food. Carlos explained that there wasn’t much to do in the way of entertainment so people gathered at the airport to watch the planes arrive and take off.

At X-rays I had some trouble going through as the machine kept beeping. Turns out Col’s pair of pliers that I’d shoved into the bottom of my bag was the culprit. These were only discovered after emptying the contents of said bag onto the counter where seed pods, small containers of jam, peanuts, a dried snake skin and some river pebbles lay for all to see. Luckily they were more interested in the pliers and I quickly scooped up the rest of the items and hastily shoved them back in my bag. I noticed one of the officials writing my name in a book. I asked him why but he ignored me and just waved his hand to tell me to move on. I moved irritably to the departure lounge thinking I’d probably been branded as a potential terrorist and wouldn’t be allowed back in the country.

Tim sat across from us and was soon asleep. Col also nodded off after tucking into his ‘chickenorbeef’. A baby cried incessantly and a woman prattled non-stop to her mute and probably deaf partner the entire flight. Every time I passed her to go to the loo, I glared at her to no avail. I could cheerfully shove a handful of travel socks down her gullet. My sleep-deprived body didn’t know what to do with itself as I tossed and turned till we landed in Johannesburg the next morning.

Tim and Col were fresh with sleep and couldn’t understand why I was snarling with irritability. Col felt a cup of strong coffee may help. It did to some extent. I sat with my hands curled around the steaming mug and thought how amazing that not a week ago I was gazing at jaguars in the wild and immediately wished I was back there.
