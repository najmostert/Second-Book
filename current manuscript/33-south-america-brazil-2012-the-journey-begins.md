# Chapter 30: SOUTH AMERICA — Brazil 2012: The Journey Begins

I was eagerly looking forward to seeing the mighty Amazon and hopefully, most of its wonderful wildlife. Our morning began with us stumbling around, bumping into each other like so many electrons—washing faces, feeding dogs, and shoving plugs into rucksacks. It was pouring with rain in Cape Town, and I tried in vain to keep any moisture from falling on my expensive Brazilian (natch) hair treatment; already, a few strands of hair were curling up in disdain. I was in the aisle seat with two Brazilians beside me who laughed uproariously through a Jennifer Aniston movie for almost the entire flight. I had a small glass of wine with my meal, and when one of the men wanted to go to the loo, he had to carefully pry an empty, wine-stained plastic glass from my hair, where it had been gently swinging in the breeze from the air-con, which was on full blast. The flight seemed endless, and I eventually tried to doze, only to wake to the sound of forty-five crisp packets being prised open. We arrived in São Paulo at 4 p.m. local time. As we disembarked, the warm, moist air slapped my tired face into semi-wakefulness. Customs went by with relative ease, followed by a wait in the heat for the bus to take us to our hotel. The driver was in a hurry. My hand luggage, as previously mentioned, weighed a small ton. I had just stepped on board and was adjusting the strap of my rucksack when the bus pulled away, and I landed with a whump onto a startled woman’s lap. I swore, then apologised, then staggered towards my seat while Col and Tim looked on impassively; they were quite used to my misfortunes by now. We were disgorged onto the hot pavement and stumbled into the hotel lobby, where I jumped onto the Wi-Fi immediately—Col, with his usual efficiency, had locked the side door at home, and I needed to contact our house-sitter, David, urgently. The dogs were trapped inside, which increased the panic. I sent several text messages, and he thankfully acknowledged the last one. Col and I sat outside on the pool balcony and ordered a local beer called ‘Bohemia’. It was a pleasantly balmy evening, and we clinked our glasses to the forthcoming holiday. 

Iguazu Falls, Argentina 

Morning came early, and we took the hotel shuttle back to the airport. We were flying to Iguazu today, which lies on the border between Brazil and Argentina. After collecting our luggage, we met our local guide, Patricio—a small, wiry man with a head of wild, curly dark hair and a ready smile. We shook hands, and he led us to a bus that would take us over the border. Patricio explained that while there is mainly cattle and sheep farming in Brazil, Argentina focuses more on pine plantations. The border control went smoothly, and we did not even have to leave the bus. The driver told us he was from Brazil but spoke a mixture of Portuguese and Spanish—‘Portuñol’, if you will. On the Argentinian side, I took a photo of the Iguazu River. According to Patricio, the falls have eroded twenty-five kilometres since they were discovered. A couple of coatis—members of the raccoon family with long snouts and thick, striped tails—crossed the road, and a pair of chopi blackbirds pecked at something on the verge. After checking into the hotel, I noticed a marvellous view of the falls from the balcony. Joining Patricio and another guide, Martien, we set off to see the upper level of the falls along a narrow path. Each view was spectacular: horseshoe-shaped cascades with an island in the middle. Caracaras (hawk-like birds) and black vultures flew low over the water, and more coatis scampered around our feet. Patricio noted that Argentina’s once-dominant cattle-farming culture had shifted towards soybeans, though they are slowly returning to cattle. This shift has proven difficult due to the environmental impact of both industries and a legacy of large-scale, potentially unsustainable agricultural practices. The falls scattered a fine mist over us as we walked, cooling us down until we arrived at a restaurant called The Fortune Cataratas. We climbed the polished red-cement steps into a large, open-plan area with a long buffet. I tried some traditional manioc root. Patricio led us to the lower slopes, where we were soon drenched despite our plastic raincoats. The views were spectacular—boiling cascades of rainbow-tinted water. A lookout platform provided a good spot for photos, though being sprayed in the face resulted in some amusing pictures. On our return, we passed the old Falls Hotel, which had been reclaimed by nature and was already overgrown with weeds; it had only closed in 2011, yet it looked derelict. Orange trees were dotted around the garden, and kapok fluff lay in patches on the ground—I picked up a handful; it was soft enough to make a sweater. Col pointed out the national bird of Argentina, a Rufous hornero. The bird itself is a fairly nondescript, russet-brown and white creature, but its nest is fascinating: a huge, oven-shaped structure made from mud and straw, highly resistant to the elements. Another interesting bird we saw was the great dusky swift, which nests in large colonies clawed into the cliffs behind the falls, using moss and mud-cemented pebbles. There must have been at least a hundred under one rock. Tim and I eventually left Col and the guide and returned to the hotel verandah for a Quilmes beer. Col returned around 6 p.m., emerging from the wrong exit like a lost explorer. A man sat at the entrance of the dining room playing a harp. That evening, I ordered a local catfish called surubí for supper, washed down with some full-bodied Argentinian red wine. 

The Devil's Throat 

Patricio met us around 8 a.m. with another guide, Adrian. Today we were walking to the nearby forest. As it was cold and cloudy, it was perfect weather for walking. Col and Patricio went birding, so after a while, Tim and I broke away with Adrian to carry on towards the waterfall, chatting about conservation. Adrian showed us numerous exotic trees with their bark stripped away at the base. This eventually kills the tree without felling it, he explained, as cutting trees in the park is forbidden. Small groups of capuchin monkeys crashed through the trees, and an agouti—a brownish-grey rodent the size of a rabbit—crossed our path. We walked about seven kilometres in total, gazing down into the bottom of the gully. Adrian told me about yerba maté, a drink made from steeping dried leaves in hot water; it is a light green liquid drunk through a metal straw from gourd-like containers. He swore by its health-giving properties. Later, we took a jeep down to a large boat. A sweet young Argentinian girl told us about the jungle as we drove; she pointed out huge, thin palm trees and explained how the palm hearts are harvested as a global delicacy. At the boat, we were given life jackets and blue plastic bags for our valuables. The boat was small, seating only about six people. We suddenly plunged forward at full throttle towards the falls, coming right up to them as they tumbled over the rock. The spray drenched everyone on board—it was quite exhilarating. The driver manoeuvred the boat carefully so that we passed behind the curtain of water, which was even more thrilling. Later, we walked with Adrian towards the Brazilian side of the falls, to a small town called Foz do Iguaçu, to await one of the colourful tourist buses. Ours featured a bright painting of a toucan on the side. I bought two raincoats, and we took the bus to the start of the Brazilian falls walk. The border crossing from Argentina to Brazil was quick and painless. As we walked along the path, mist from the falls wet our faces. I enjoyed seeing them from a completely different perspective—as I had when comparing the Zimbabwean and Zambian views of Victoria Falls—before continuing to the ‘Devil's Throat’. This time we were so close, and the falls tumbled so powerfully on one side, that the heavy mist drenched us completely. An absolute curtain of water fell from the top, visible only from this side. It was totally exhilarating. A rainbow slid over the foam as the sun came out to light up the spray. We continued walking, stopping briefly to buy a tea from a kiosk. I chose an aniseed tea, which was a new and interesting flavour for me; I won’t be having it again! 

The Hummingbird Garden 

The next morning, we headed out to find the ‘Hummingbird House’. One local Argentinian woman had turned her garden into a sanctuary for these birds by placing a significant number of sugar-water feeders throughout her grounds. An old wooden sign on her gate declared, ‘Jardín de los Picaflores’. A short, curly-haired woman of around fifty opened the gate and welcomed us in. She and Adrian exchanged a flurry of Spanish, after which she led us to the back garden, explaining that we were welcome to photograph the birds provided we did not disturb them. The garden was small but absolutely swarming with picaflores (hummingbirds) of every colour. She had bottles of sugar water fitted with artificial flowers dotted all around; they hung from every branch, creating a hub of incredible activity. The birds zipped about, sipping nectar and seeming to hover in suspended animation. It was truly awesome. There were iridescent greens, blues, yellows, and scarlet reds; long-tailed, red-beaked, and copper-bottomed varieties in every hue imaginable. The air felt electric with their flight. Col was in his element, snapping away while the sunlight played like Morse code on their feathers, turning them into shimmering shafts of iridescence. Patricio prepared a flask of maté and offered me a sip; it tasted intensely bitter. Reluctantly, we thanked our hostess and headed back to the border. My passport was filling up alarmingly with all the border crossings. The bus stopped at a point where you can see Brazil, Paraguay, and Argentina simultaneously, where a small group of Amerindian children singing nearby added to the atmosphere. 

São Paulo to Cuiabá 

Col woke me at 1 a.m., claiming the batteries had fallen out of the alarm clock. I peered groggily at the clock, but everything seemed intact. I was wide awake and annoyed—especially when I looked over and saw he had fallen asleep again! I dozed on and off until 3 a.m., when we all got up and dressed in the dark. At reception, we met Lucas, our guide for the next five days. After introducing ourselves, we set off for the airport by bus. Customs went smoothly again, despite there being five of us. We learned that John and Steven, two of our fellow travellers, were both doctors in their late fifties who travelled extensively. We sat in the waiting area for a few hours, so Lucas treated us to coffee and pão—fresh, warm, round cheese rolls that went down easily. We also met a young South African couple, Liz and Darren, who were joining us for this leg of the tour. Cuiabá is the capital and largest city of the Brazilian state of Mato Grosso. After collecting our luggage, we headed for the bus to take us to our hotel. I gazed out of the window and declared Cuiabá ‘not very interesting’—it was dirty, dusty, and dry, full of trucks and the skeletons of cars, all set against ugly cement buildings. The bus halted outside what looked like a hospital, but turned out to be our hotel, the Hits Pantanal. We checked in, and the clinical theme continued throughout; our room featured a white-tiled floor reminiscent of a giant shower, and aside from the bed and two rickety side tables, there was very little else. We met the others in the foyer, where Lucas informed us we would meet for supper later, so we retreated to our rooms. It was hot, dusty, and humid, and we were starving, so I tried to order room service. The staff were ‘non comprehendo’, so I eventually called reception and repeated the order: one omelette and two steak sandwiches. After dropping my iPad and cracking the screen, I bound it with tape and hopped onto the billiard table that was posing as a bed. 

Mato Grosso (West Brazil) 

A bus took us back to the airport to board a TAP flight. The plane looked relatively new, and we were served more carbohydrates— bread, cheese, and bananas. I hoped my fitness levels hadn't deteriorated too much with all the ‘flipping carbs’ we were consuming; there was always cake served at breakfast! We touched down at Alta Floresta Airport after a few hours' flying. It is a small, busy airport where we met Marco, our new guide. He led us to a van in the shimmering heat, and we drove for almost two hours to the Cristalino River Lodge, stopping briefly for a short walk in search of the huge Harpy Eagle. We weren't in luck, so we returned to the van and eventually hiked a short distance to the river, where a small boat awaited us. The dark, silky green river possessed a still, quiet beauty, and a gentle breeze caressed our foreheads. The main group were keen birders, so there was constant discussion of sightings. In a small clearing in the forest, we found a tall metal tower; we climbed the narrow rungs to the top level, which flattened out into a large platform towering over the canopy. The view of the surrounding forest was fantastic. Monkeys chattered in the distance, crashing through the treetops, though they were too far to identify with the naked eye. John, armed with good binoculars, identified them as black-faced macaques. Marco pointed out a pair of red-and-yellow macaws and a red-headed parrot. It was already dark by the time we made our way back. 

Alta Floresta: The Sounds of the Forest 

The sound of the forest was almost deafening, filled with chirping crickets, whistling birds, grunting animals, and the crunch of dead leaves underfoot. We travelled to Cristalino by boat; it was lovely and cool sailing over a rapid where a tree hung over the water, filled with small, long-nosed bats. They were so tiny they looked like lichen as they clung in groups to the bark. One of my favourite sightings occurred when we came across a mother tapir walking on a path next to the river with her striped baby. Tapirs are the largest indigenous land mammals in South America; they are similar in shape to a pig, with a short, prehensile trunk. Later, we saw three black-and-red caracaras, a pygmy kingfisher, and two spectacled caiman. The full moon cast pale ripples over the black river. After parking the boat on the far side, we hiked up a grassy bank to a bird hide with camouflage-patterned screens. We sat in a row on a roughly hewn bench; the air was still, humid, and swarming with gnats. A coati with a reddish coat scurried by. It was completely dark by the time we walked back to the boat by torchlight. This evening, we went for a short night walk behind the huts. I love animal spotting at night—there’s something magical about the light, the smells, and the fun of encountering nocturnal creatures. We hoped to see a night monkey, and I couldn't believe it when, twenty minutes in, we found a golden-brown Azara's night monkey curled around a branch. It was mostly grey with a patch of ochre over its chest. We carried on in single file, following the bobbing torch of Lucas. Our next sighting was a hair-tailed woolly opossum. It had thick, woolly fur of a greyish-reddish colour; its tail was furred halfway up, with the rest being bare. Its eyes glowed like two green olives. Tim and I had another sing-song that night, with Lucas keeping the tune on his guitar. Afterwards, we returned to our rooms, taking care not to bump into the tiny spiders that jumped—you have to look for a tiny speck of ‘dew’ captured by your torchlight; those are their eyes. We also managed to avoid the huge cicadas that can jump onto your face if you are taken unawares. I woke at 6 a.m., stumbled around the room until I found the candle in the agouti-pod holder, lit it, and walked down the path to breakfast. No surprises here: most of it was cake. I chose coconut cake and copious cups of watered-down coffee. It started to rain harder, but we spotted a king vulture sitting forlornly in a Meranti tree; it had a nice black hairdo and a red wattle. Lucas told us over lunch that he had two small children, aged three and six. I mentioned how difficult it must be to be away from them for so long, and he smiled a little sadly. Later, we boarded the Cristalino bus for the airport, joined by an attractive girl and her husband from São Paulo—she claimed to be a marine biologist. En route, the bus ran over a large snake crossing the road, and we stopped to have a look. It was a beautiful gold-and-brown tree boa. It was alive but badly damaged; we felt sad and hoped it would survive, although that seemed doubtful. 

Towards the Pantanal Florista Airport is small, so Customs wasn't a problem. I was starting to feel a little ‘off’: achey, hot, then cold. I hoped it would pass; I didn't want to ruin an expensive holiday! We boarded a TRIP aeroplane for a bumpy flight to Pantanal Airport. The Pantanal is one of the largest wetlands in the world. It was extremely hot and humid, but I felt cold—never a great sign. After collecting our luggage, we were taken to a large tour bus. Despite the air conditioning, I remained cold and shivering. Once it became dark, Lucas shone his torch, revealing a red brocket deer, a white-lipped peccary, an armadillo scuttling into the high grass, and a crab-eating fox. I was now fully ill, shaking and sweating, and by the time we reached the Pousada Alegre, I could barely see. We disembarked at the lodge, and I quickly asked Lucas to take me to my room, where I showered and collapsed into bed, shivering with fever. Lucas brought me a blanket and a mug of maté tea. The next two days were a blur of faces, pills, and vague chatter about Dengue fever. Col brought me water, and Tim peered at me with concern. Luckily, John and Steven, the doctors, had a good supply of medicine, and I started a course of antibiotics. I slipped in and out of a fevered sleep for three days. On the afternoon of the third day, I heard a racket outside, but my eyes hurt, so I crawled back into bed. 

Pousada Alegre 

This morning at 4 a.m. my fever breaks, and I feel so much better, sleeping soundly until 6:30. Col left with the others for a game drive. I get up a bit shakily, shower and dress and venture outside. I have no idea where we are or what the place looks like. I arrived in a fog of pain and fever in the dark of night. I walk towards what I hope is the dining room for some tea and meet the owner who asks how I am feeling. He tells me Tim is here and shows me to his room. While waiting for Tim to finish showering, he shows me where the toco toucans are. I am sorry I have missed out on all the action and want to make up for it now, so I walk around the huge garden looking at birds; brown capuchin, toucans and aracari. Aracari’s are small toucans which are brightly plumaged and have enormous, contrasting patterned bills. There are some beautiful trees here which I hadn't seen from my window. Many beautiful birds, too; toucans, red-headed manakins, a black hawk-eagle and a huge iguana with a round tail. Tim and I walk to a beautiful sea of yellow-flowered trees and see the bus returning. Lucas leans out of the window when he spots me, with a huge smile and a thumbs-up sign. He is such a kind man. I meet up with everyone in the dining room, and they all say they are happy to see me up and feeling better. I thank John and Steven for the antibiotics. Col says they saw a tayra this morning; a type of weasel. My legs are full of bruises and bites as a result of the dengue fever. I have a short walk with Col, and we see capuchin monkeys and toucans, then a beautiful pair of blue and yellow macaws as well as a large iguana, slowly moving into the nearby bushes. I feel a bit sad to be already leaving, illness having cheated me out of three days. We board the bus again and head for Porto Jofre. Although it’s only 140 km away, it takes us the entire afternoon to get there. The husband of the young couple, Darryl, is also starting to feel flu-ey and I’m hoping it is just flu. I have a front seat, having missed so much until now and see egrets, herons and cormorants and even a few capybaras, submerged in swampy water from the large window. It is very hot when we make stops, once for the toilet and to see an owl and a potoo. Potoos are nocturnal birds native to Central and South America and have exceptional camouflage, large yellow eyes, and mournful calls. By day, they mimic tree stumps or branches, becoming virtually invisible. By night, they hunt flying insects from a perch. At the lodge, our names are pasted on the doors: ‘Timothy’, ‘Colin Patrick’ and ‘Janette Mary Van’. There are four single beds in each long, narrow room, a bit like a dormitory. Each has air-con, a fridge and for what it's worth, a TV. We meet the rest of the tour in the dining room, which is quite full as there are two other big tour groups here as well. I walk around the grounds taking photos of birds; blue macaws, jabiru, and black-necked storks. We’re all at lunch except Col. Everyone asks me where he is. I don’t want to say he is setting traps for butterflies, so I mutter vaguely about looking at the river and chat to Lucas for a while then Col arrives and whispers to me that he’s caught some nice specimens. It is nice and cool inside the bus but gets progressively hotter and more humid every time we stop. A few capybaras swim in the nearby river, a giant beehive sitting tumour-like in the fork of a large tree, jacanas, azure gallinules, and some paw prints of a jaguar. I ask Lucas about the beautiful trees with varying shades of pink flowers, and he tells me they are piúva trees. They are really gorgeous. We come across a capybara, a baby with a jacana on its head! After this there isn’t much else to see, so we return to the lodge. There’s a boat ride on the Cuiabá River today and the day starts off cool and very pleasant, with a slight mist lying low over the water. Lucas tells me that Catfish create holes in river banks primarily for nesting and breeding, digging burrows in mud or dirt banks to lay eggs. These many nesting holes can destabilise the banks. I look around at them with renewed interest. After we spend the morning pointing stuff out to each other, Lucas tells us that according to local custom it’s more polite to refer to the position of an item as being at ‘2 o’clock’ or whatever clock position is closest. Our first good sighting after an hour of sailing is at ‘3;15’; a group of giant river otters frolicking around the edges of the river. One has caught a large, round, silver fish and is tearing at it with its sharp teeth. A small troop of black howler monkeys swing noisily in the trees. I use a tin mug to pour river water over my arms and legs to keep cool then thankfully we have a toilet-stop. This entails mooring the boat, clambering out, scrambling up the embankment and searching for a suitable, private bush. Lucas hands out small 200ml bottles of water before we continue down the river, which is also full of caimans. I photograph a big fellow lying still in the shallow edges with his yellowish jaws open. A small group of capuchins scampers in the treetops. We have another embankment stop; this time Lucas hands out cheese and ham sandwiches, bottled water and boiled eggs which we eat sitting on a large log, munching away, flicking away flies and midges that swirl around our faces in the humidity. The little capys are adorable. They actually look more like giant guinea pigs than rats. One of the capybara's expressions I decide is like a British aristocrat asking for a cucumber sandwich. As we head home through a beautiful pastel sunset, we come across a small boat on the embankment. As we cut the engines and silently come up alongside them, one of the bearded researchers tells us of a jaguar in the nearby foliage, eating. We hold our collective breath in excitement and a flurry of camera noises as lenses are attached and we glide up a little closer. As we approach, the stench of what turns out to be Jaguar supper, assails our noses. The binoculars come out, and we try to focus on the jaguar, who is eating the remains of a small caiman. I focus on its face for a few seconds, then its beautiful striped tail twitching in the dappled sunlight. Tim takes a look and says he can see the entire cat. Col is photographing furiously. We are all in high spirits as we return to the Lodge. Seeing this beautiful animal has been at the top of all our wish lists. We celebrate with some Argentinian wine tonight. A veil of mist hangs over the river like yesterday and the morning starts cool and still. Aside from the giant river otter, we see an otter this morning. Neotropical otters can grow more than three feet long and in some cases almost half of that is their tail. Their sleek shape, waterproof fur, and webbed toes, help the otter swim and hunt in the water. As the driver negotiates the river we almost get stuck motoring down a very narrow canal carpeted with water plants and lilies – the river is almost choked with them. It is a hot, dank, humid, fly-infested wait while the boatman tries to disentangle river foliage from the motor. I watch a pretty lily trotter while we are waiting. He eventually frees up the engine ( the boatman not the lily trotter- he’s useless), whistling all the while, and gently backs out of the canal. The sun gently slips into the sea, casting red, orange and yellow streaks. We are all sitting quietly, wrapped up in our thoughts, when suddenly the boatman yells, ‘Jaguar!’ and we all spring to action and cast our eyes over the riverside, bringing binoculars up to view and preparing the cameras. ‘Where?’ I ask after a few minutes of scanning, then suddenly I see it: a lone jaguar right on a slip of beach sand. It is drinking from the river. We cut the engine and come closer until I can see the spots on its tail. It’s a magnificent specimen. It slowly laps up water with its huge pink tongue before slowly sloping off into the bushes. The group is overjoyed with the sighting, but as we continue back down the river, the torch picks up not one but two more jaguars. These are two young males lying on their backs, yawning and grinning at each other. We watch in silence until they rise and slowly wander off out of sight. I take a deep breath. The boatman motors back in silence as everyone digests the majesty of these beautiful cats. We’re sailing over the dark, silky water when someone shouts, ‘Snake!’ I turn to find a long, dark snake with a yellow tail undulating past us in the water and up into a tree that’s hanging over the river. It is a Caninana, Lucas tells us, a type of non-venomous snake often mistaken for a cobra. It stays cool this morning, thankfully and as we round the edge of the forest near Fisherman’s Corner, we come across a jaguar lying in the shade. There are two other tourist boats in front of us, so we can't see much at first. Eventually, it moves into the bushes and the boats take off. The boat edges closer, the motor cut and we wait. Our patience is rewarded when the jaguar returns, walking slowly past us along the edge of the forest. The bus rumbles slowly across the Trans Pantaneira highway over the many rickety wooden bridges towards the Mato Grosso Hotel which lies close to the Pixaim River. After alighting, we stretch our legs and check-in Our room is very basic and hot as the air-con is not working. Lucas gets them to fix it while we have supper which is a small mug of piranha soup which tastes quite nice - a bit like… chicken. Supper consists of manioc, rice, red beans, corn salad, meat and fish. This morning the group sets off in the bus towards the airport. En route, a couple of roseate spoonbills dab at the shallow water of a large pond which is a seething soup of caimans. A black vulture is eating pieces of fresh caiman from a tree. There’s a crab-eating raccoon, pampas deer, and red brocket deer. We leave the Trans Pantaneira and stop for an ice cream, bought by Lucas who hands them out like passports. We’re in a typical backwater area with car and lorry carcasses, rusting parts, and tyres lying everywhere. Pistachio-coloured houses are built close to the road. Later we come to flat grasslands dotted with tall, thin palm trees and stop for petrol. We arrive at the small airport and say goodbye to John and Steve as they’re heading back to the States. Fond farewells from the rest of the group as our various flights come up. We are the last to leave. At 5 p.m. we get up and stand in the queue. The officials announce that the systems are down, so it is absolute chaos. Everything has to be handwritten, a laborious task that takes three times as long, but at last we are in and standing at customs where we meet another couple who have also been to Cristalino. They say they saw a puma; another cat I’d love to see. The plane is delayed by a further hour and a half before boarding. After landing, and grabbing our luggage off the carousel, we make our way through customs. A stocky man is holding up a sign that reads, ‘Indris Tours. Mostert’. We claim the title and shake hands. He speaks only a little English, resulting in a lot of head-nodding and smiling. Once on board the small bus, I recline the seat and give Tim a head massage until he dozes off. The trip from the airport to Campo Grande takes a few hours, so we all have a snooze. It’s dark when we wake up and ask for water. The driver nods towards a cooler box behind the passenger seat. He unnerves me by watching the removal of said water instead of the road in front. At around 11:30 p.m. we turn off onto a dirt track through cattle country towards the Baiazinha Lodge, which is in the Nhecolândia Ecological Reserve. Just inside the gates, we are surprised and pleased to spot four giant anteaters who walk stiffly past us; a mother, two males and a baby. We watch them waddle into the nearby bush and out of range of the driver’s torch. It is after midnight, we’re beyond hungry and I just want to curl up in a comfy bed. We’re met by a tall, lanky fellow with an earnest expression. It may be concern – we all look travel-weary and crumpled. He thrusts his hand out and introduces himself as our guide, Diogo. We stumble after him as he shows us to our rooms with a glass of juice and a piece of cake. We are to be up early tomorrow but end up fumbling around in our luggage to prepare for the early start. It is almost 1 a.m. before we collapse into bed and fold the sheets around us. As a result, I am freezing for half the night. The air-con is set to ‘polar’ degrees, and I am too tired to get up to look for a blanket. The three of us are up just after 6 a.m., dress quickly in the cool morning air and go down to the dining room for a quick breakfast. There’s a lovely view of the wetland from our room and a large white hammock beckons from the balcony. A buffet is laid out in the dining room and we pile up our plates and find a table. There are only two large wooden tables, and we find one with three seats. The other occupants introduce themselves: a middle-aged professor and his attractive wife, and a honeymoon couple. She is a very tall, thin, pale Portuguese girl and is at least a head and shoulders taller than her short, Asian husband who also appears to be twenty years older than her. However, they gaze into each other's eyes during breakfast, whispering and giggling and hold hands throughout the duration of the meal. After wandering outside and heading for the large, open-seated truck we climb aboard for a game drive. Diogo appears, fresh and beaming. Soon after setting off we come to four capybaras close to the lodge. Two are submerged in a small pond. A few kilometres further, Diogo stops and points out a beautiful hyacinth macaw nesting in a tree at the side of the road. Along the way, we see a common potoo, a jabiru ( a large white stork with a black head), a huge river turtle, peccaries and Tim spots a yellow armadillo. It stands up and peers at us quizzically before turning around and rustling into the grass. The driver stops at the Research Office, where we all pile out and are led to a small room with a number of plastic chairs laid out. A nervous, dark-haired woman gives us a short talk in English on the macaw project. She says we are her first English-speaking audience. I go for a short snooze but end up sleeping until 3 p.m., waking with a corduroy cheek and stiff hair. So, I only have half an hour to make myself look presentable before we take the open truck on an afternoon drive, stopping at the edge of a huge, leafy pine forest. Diogo says we will have a short walk. Someone spots a family of howler monkeys sitting in the branches of a large jaboticaba tree. These are the loudest of monkeys, with a distinctive howl that alerts others to their presence and their territory. I am fascinated by the leaf-cutter ants and watch a long line of them carrying away some green foliage. They expertly cut leaves into smaller pieces with their sharp mandibles. These are used to make the mould which feeds their babies. Leaf-cutter ants have very specific roles in taking care of their ‘fungal’ garden and dumping the refuse. During a short stop in a clearing, Diogo hands us each a small plastic cup attached to a keyring. We are to keep them as souvenirs, he says, as he pours some much-needed water into them. Tim and Col hand me their cups, so I attach them to my belt and carry on, looking like an Irish tinker. Everyone is walking very slowly – especially the honeymoon couple, who have barely untangled themselves from each other – so Tim and I walk on ahead. This evening we meet a South African ranger called Lawrence who is working on the Jaguar Project. We chat over supper (piranha soup and fish). He says he’ll show us around tomorrow night. Col asks Diogo if we can go on a night drive. He agrees, so we don some warm tops and set off half an hour later in a small, open-seated jeep. It isn’t long before we come across a small jaguar walking along the pathway. It is very exciting, and more so when a little further on, its mother pads softly out of the bush to join her year old cub. We watch in silence, save for the sound of clicking cameras and measured breathing then sit back, well satisfied with the evening's viewing. A little further along, we come across a few peccaries, then a tapir and a crab-eating fox, which are omnivorous despite the name. Next is a sighting of a pygmy owl, its eye caught in the glow of a torch. There are two marsh deer at the edge of an inky-black pool and a caiman. It’s a happy group that return very satisfied with the evening's viewing after ten-thirty and throw things together for tomorrow's drive before climbing into bed. I remember the blanket this time. After breakfast we meet Diogo and wait an extra fifteen minutes for the honeymoon couple to arrive entwined and oblivious to the impatient group waiting for the morning drive.I shoot them a murderous glare but they see no one else but themselves. I wonder if they’ve actually seen any animal on this trip aside from each others’ faces in close up then reproach myself for being a grumpy cow. We find a Giant Anteater waddling across the road after half an hour of walking which is very exciting, then a Brown Capuchin in a nearby tree and many Caiman off the path, waiting for the sun to get some heat onto their cold bodies. Diogo points out a Toco Toucan flying overhead. It is the largest of the Toucans and has a large yellow, red and blue beak made mostly from keratin. It’s entire head seems to be mostly curved beak, which despite it’s size is hollow and lightweight. 

Pousada Alegre: Recovery and Reunion 

My fever broke at 4 a.m., and I finally slept soundly until 6:30. Col had already left for the morning game drive, so I dressed shakily and ventured outside. Having arrived in the dark, in a haze of pain and fever, I had no idea what the lodge looked like. I walked towards what I hoped was the dining room, where I met the owner, who kindly asked after my health. He told me Tim was nearby and showed me to his room. While waiting for Tim to finish showering, the owner pointed out the Toco toucans in the garden. Eager to make up for the lost time, I spent the morning exploring; I spotted brown capuchins, more toucans, and several aracaris—brightly plumaged small toucans with enormous, patterned bills. The garden was filled with beautiful trees I hadn't seen from my sickbed window, along with red-headed manakins, a black hawk-eagle, and a huge iguana with a thick, round tail. Tim and I later walked to a beautiful stand of yellow-flowered trees and saw the safari bus returning. Lucas, our guide, leaned out the window with a huge smile and a thumbs-up when he spotted me. He is such a kind man. Back in the dining room, the rest of the group seemed genuinely happy to see me up and about, and I thanked John and Steven for the antibiotics that had undoubtedly saved my holiday. Col mentioned they had seen a tayra—a member of the weasel family—that morning. My legs were still covered in bruises and bites from the dengue fever, but I managed a short walk with Col to see capuchin monkeys, toucans, and a beautiful pair of blue-and-yellow macaws. I felt a pang of sadness at leaving so soon, having been cheated out of three days by illness, but we boarded the bus for Porto Jofre. Although it was only 140 km away, the journey took the entire afternoon. Even Darryl, one of the young men in our group, was starting to feel ‘flu-ey’, and I could only hope he had managed to avoid the same fate as me. 

Porto Jofre and the Jaguar Quest 

I claimed the front seat for this leg of the journey, having missed so much already. From my vantage point, I spotted egrets, herons, cormorants, and capybaras submerged in the swampy water. During our stops, we saw an owl and a potoo—nocturnal birds with exceptional camouflage that mimic tree stumps by day and hunt insects by night. At the lodge, our names were pasted on the doors: ‘Timothy’, ‘Colin Patrick’, and ‘Janette Mary Van’. The rooms were long and narrow, dormitory-style, each equipped with air-con, a fridge, and a TV. We joined the rest of our tour group in a crowded dining room, shared with two other large parties. I wandered the grounds taking photos of blue macaws, jabiru, and black-necked storks. At lunch, Col was missing; when the group asked where he was, I muttered vaguely about him ‘looking at the river’ rather than admitting he was off setting butterfly traps. He arrived shortly after, whispering to me that he had caught some nice specimens. As we headed out on the river, the air remained humid. We saw capybaras swimming, a giant beehive sitting like a tumour in the fork of a tree, jacanas, and azure gallinules. We even spotted fresh jaguar paw prints. Lucas explained that the local custom for pinpointing animal locations is to use the ‘clock’ system—referring to positions like ‘3 o’clock’—which made our spotting much more efficient. Our first major sighting after an hour of sailing was a group of giant river otters frolicking near the bank. One had caught a large, round, silver fish and was tearing into it with sharp teeth. A small troop of black howler monkeys swung noisily overhead. After a ‘toilet stop’—which involved scrambling up a muddy embankment to find a private bush—we continued down the river, which was thick with caimans. I photographed a large fellow lying in the shallows with his yellowish jaws agape. Later, Lucas handed out cheese-and-ham sandwiches and boiled eggs; we ate them perched on a log, flicking away the swarms of flies and midges in the humidity. As we headed home through a beautiful pastel sunset, we pulled up alongside a small boat. The bearded researchers inside whispered that there was a jaguar eating nearby. We held our collective breath. The stench of ‘jaguar supper’ hit us before we saw the cat, who was busy devouring the remains of a small caiman. I focused on its face for a few seconds, then its striped tail twitching in the dappled sunlight. We were all in high spirits as we returned to the lodge; seeing this beautiful animal had been at the top of all our wish lists. We celebrated that evening with Argentinian wine. 

The Majesty of the Cats 
The morning started cool and misty again. We spotted a neotropical otter, which can grow over three feet long, its sleek, waterproof fur and webbed toes perfectly adapted for hunting. As we motored down a narrow canal choked with water lilies, we almost got stuck. It was a hot, dank, fly-infested wait while the boatman disentangled foliage from the motor, but eventually, we were free. Suddenly, the boatman yelled, ‘Jaguar!’ We sprang to action, binoculars at the ready. A lone jaguar was drinking from the river on a slip of sand. We cut the engine and drifted closer until I could clearly see the spots on its tail. It lapped water with a huge pink tongue before sloping off into the bushes. To our delight, a little further down, the torch picked up two more jaguars—young males lying on their backs, yawning and grinning at each other. I took a deep breath, barely able to digest the majesty of these beautiful cats. As we motored back, someone shouted, ‘Snake!’ We watched a long, dark Caninana snake with a yellow tail undulate past us in the water and climb into an overhanging tree. On our final morning, we found a jaguar lying in the shade near Fisherman’s Corner. After a patient wait while other boats moved on, the jaguar returned, walking slowly past us along the edge of the forest. It was a magnificent, serene end to our river safari. 

Departure and the Anteater Surprise 

We rumbled across the Trans Pantaneira highway, crossing many rickety wooden bridges towards the Mato Grosso Hotel. Our room was basic and hot, as the air-con was broken, but Lucas managed to get it fixed while we had a supper of piranha soup—which tasted quite nice, a bit like chicken! The next morning, we set off towards the airport. En route, we spotted roseate spoonbills dabbing at a pond seething with caimans, a crab-eating raccoon, pampas deer, and a red brocket deer. We stopped for ice cream in a typical backwater area littered with car carcasses and rusting tyres. We said fond farewells to John and Steven, who were heading back to the States, and were the last of our group to leave. Chaos erupted at the airport when the systems went down, requiring everything to be handwritten. After a long flight and a final customs check, a stocky man holding a sign that read ‘Indris Tours. Mostert’ greeted us. Once on the bus to Campo Grande, I reclined my seat and gave Tim a head massage until he dozed off. It was nearly midnight when we turned onto a dirt track toward the Baiazinha Lodge. Just inside the gates, we were surprised by four giant anteaters—a mother, two males, and a baby—waddling stiffly past us. We were met by our new guide, Diogo, a tall, lanky fellow who looked at our travel-weary faces with sincere concern. After a late-night glass of juice and cake, we collapsed into bed, though I spent the night freezing in a room set to ‘polar’ temperatures. The next morning, we met our fellow guests: a professor and his wife, and a honeymoon couple—the latter so entwined they barely noticed the world around them. We set off on a game drive and were immediately rewarded with hyacinth macaws, jabirus, and a yellow armadillo. Tim and I spent the afternoon at a macaw project talk, followed by a walk through a pine forest where we watched leaf-cutter ants expertly harvesting foliage. That evening, Lawrence, a South African ranger from the Jaguar Project, joined us for supper. We later took a night drive, spotting a jaguar mother and cub, a tapir, a crab-eating fox, and a pygmy owl. It was a deeply satisfied group that finally climbed into bed, and this time, I remembered the blanket! We met Diogo for breakfast, waiting an extra fifteen minutes for the honeymoon couple to arrive, entwined and entirely oblivious to the impatient group waiting for the morning drive. I shot them a murderous glare, but they were so caught up in each other they didn't even notice. I briefly wondered if they had seen a single animal on this entire trip aside from each other’s faces in close-up, then promptly reproached myself for being a ‘grumpy cow’. After a half-hour walk, we found a giant anteater waddling across the road—an exciting sight—followed by a brown capuchin in a nearby tree and many caimans off the path, lying still as they waited for the sun to warm their cold bodies. Diogo pointed out a Toco toucan flying overhead. As the largest of the toucan species, it sports a massive yellow, red, and blue beak made primarily of keratin. Its entire head seems to be mostly curved beak, which, despite its impressive size, is surprisingly hollow and lightweight. Back at the truck, the driver and Diogo extracted fresh coconut water for us. I took the opportunity to relieve myself behind a small bush, where I watched leaf-cutter ants marching in single file, each carrying a piece of green foliage towards a small tunnel. They use this to feed a vast underground fungus network that serves as their primary food source. Later, Diogo pointed out an ocelot—a medium-sized spotted wild cat—a fantastic ‘mega-tick’ for my list. The honeymooners, meanwhile, were back to nibbling each other’s necks, so I have no idea how much of the cat they actually saw. 

The Jaguar Hunt 

That evening, as we shared a Bohemia beer, Lawrence arrived as promised to take us out hunting for jaguars. It was just the four of us and Diogo, which felt like a rare privilege. Lawrence explained that his main objective is to habituate the jaguar cubs to vehicles rather than humans, which allows for safer, more effective tracking. He has collared five jaguars so far and has cut some rough dirt tracks through the forest, one of which we navigated that night. Lawrence shone his torch on some fresh tracks in the dirt road. ‘They walk by bringing their back legs to the front and keep their claws intact,’ he explained. ‘They also hunt cattle by grabbing the neck and tearing into the animal from the neck to the shoulder. Lions and tigers, by contrast, attack the flanks and belly.’ Lawrence mentioned he had tracked a carcass earlier and was confident the jaguar would return tonight. Col and I turned to each other with genuine excitement; this was destined to be the highlight of our trip. As we drove deeper into the forest, we spotted a nightjar sitting in the middle of the road. We continued for an hour until we came upon a huge bull carcass under some short thorn bushes. There lay the mother jaguar and her cub. I took a deep breath—what a rare and privileged sight. We watched intently, snapping numerous photographs. She seemed oblivious to the torchlight and the clicking of cameras, rolling onto her back for a while before turning onto her side to sleep. The cub playfully rolled around next to her. Neither animal seemed bothered by the vehicle, which was exactly what Lawrence hoped for. We watched them for ages; Lawrence noted that the time we spent there represented a third of the total time he had personally watched jaguars over the previous seven months! Col and I were ecstatic to have seen both an ocelot and a jaguar in a single night. 

Saddle and Paddle 

The next day, Diogo led us to a shed housing seven horses. He announced that we were going to ride through the pampas. I’ve had the privilege of riding a few times, so I carried on with the business of getting my foot in the stirrup and swinging my leg over. Tim was miffed to be given one of the smaller steeds, but he was assured it was strong. As Tim put his foot in the stirrup, his horse moved, causing him to hop around frantically; he quickly christened the nag ‘Cheeky Bastard’! I was given a sturdy black-and-white horse. I kicked gently into his midriff, and we set off in single file across the swampy pampas. My horse liked to eat, stopping frequently to nibble on grasses and palm leaves. Unfortunately, my stirrups were too short, leaving my feet at an odd angle that eventually resulted in complete numbness. When we stopped, Diogo adjusted them for me. We clopped along in contented silence through the tall grass and handled the marshy sections with ease. After a two-hour ride, I needed some assistance dismounting; the group had a good laugh at my bow-legged walk. All I needed now was a stetson, a holster, and some pointy boots. Lunch was a delight: baked Pachu fish caught that morning, manioc, corn bread with cheese, a variety of salads, and a decadent chocolate mousse. Later, Diogo drove us to the Paraguay River. Near a caiman crossing, the driver stopped and pointed out a large, collared male jaguar. His coat was much darker than the others we had seen. I leaned out to snap some photos, watching him for ten minutes until he disappeared into the forest. Buoyed by this, we drove on to a cattle farm where the Pantaneros (cowboys) tan hides. A number of narrow wooden canoes bobbed at the river’s edge. Diogo hopped off and announced we were going canoeing. I was horrified. I swim like the proverbial rock—the ‘fear of drowning’ gene was successfully passed down through my mother and grandmother, along with red hair and freckles. I told Col under no circumstances was I bobbing around in a narrow plank by myself. He agreed to row, and we gingerly lowered ourselves and his heavy camera bag into the largest canoe. With the weight, the boat submerged deeper than I was comfortable with. I lowered myself in on trembling limbs. Col handed me an oar, saying he needed a free hand for his camera. This was a fair request, but at one point, we were going in circles because I was rowing one way and Col the other! Eventually, I relaxed enough to admire the sunset as it streaked the sky with reds and yellows. We watched a cowboy herd cattle across a bridge Diogo explained was covered in soil and grass for the animals. As we rowed back, a cauldron of fishing bats rose like a dark cloud from the riverbank, skimming the surface for fish. It was a lovely sight against the silver water. 

Heading for Bonito Marcos, our guide from Bonito, arrived in a vehicle much smaller than expected, but thankfully, our luggage fit. He was a pleasant, chatty man with an American accent. He stopped to show us a small cachoeira (waterfall) called the Saltos del Monday. Marcos insisted on taking us down to get a closer look, stopping at an old cattle ranch on the way for a cold drink, where I was surprised to find a swimming pool filled with fish. There were nearly 900 steps down to the falls, and I quickly computed that there would be 900 steps back up in the baking sun. Col asked Marcos if he could find a shop that sold water wings. I was horrified, thinking for a moment they were for me, but it turned out he wanted to attach them to his underwater camera to keep it afloat. I should have known; Col’s purchases usually revolve around his hobbies. He once returned from shopping with a crystal bowl; I was touched, thinking it was a gift, only to find he intended to pin dead butterflies to a piece of polyurethane stuck to the bottom! Col is a real character; there is never a dull moment. I came across an isolated dress shop where dusty, rumpled clothing hung limply off lifeless mannequins. A dumpy, rather forlorn-looking woman sat in a corner staring out of the window; I gave her a smile and a wave, which she returned with some surprise. Nearby, there were open-fronted shops selling all manner of mechanical spare parts for bicycles and cars, while hens strutted around the dusty road with fluffy, grey-striped chicks in their wake. It grew hotter and hotter, and soon there was enough heat radiating from my flushed face to melt an ice cube. Speaking of ice, I wished I had some—preferably in a tall glass with a slice of lemon. I decided to trudge back to the lodge and order a coffee (mad dogs and Englishmen, and all that). Back at the lodge, I attempted to ask the waiter for ‘Leite fervido e água’. I was trying to ask for coffee with milk, but the word order was all wrong. The waiter looked mystified. ‘Boiled milk and water, Señora?’ I realised his English was better than my Portuguese, so I simply asked for hot coffee with milk, throwing in a ‘por favor’ at the end. It was really too hot to struggle with a language I barely knew, so I just fanned myself with a decorative palm leaf. We chatted to a small group of Americans who had arrived earlier. They annoyingly kept referring to the number of ‘crocodiles’ they’d seen. I replied that I also loved the ‘caimans’ and explained that their glowing orange-red eyes at night are their undoing, as they show up in torchlight and many are killed that way. When we mentioned the incredible devastation left by loggers in the Amazon, one woman—a hard-nosed leftist—retorted that people needed jobs to eat. I replied that I had seen the vast damage from the air, and we reached an impasse. 

The Macaws and the Blue Grotto 

Marcos arrived in a new car with another guide to take us to the Rio de Prata Eco Reserve. He stopped at an old cattle ranch that now serves as a meeting point for visitors who want to snorkel, dive, or river-raft. The garden was large and pretty, filled with flowering trees, colourful bushes, and red-soil flower beds. Col and Tim headed off to hire wetsuits, while I sat at a stool and enjoyed numerous little tin cups of thé citron—a pleasant, lemon-flavoured tea. The boys returned just as I was having a conversation with a small parrot hopping around the table. Its feathers fluffed up, and it said something in Portuguese to which I replied in English; it cocked its head, and its little beady eyes twinkled. A delightful conversation ensued. Col joined me for a stroll, where we watched a yellow-throated woodpecker pecking through dry foliage. After lunch, we left for Buraco das Araras (the ‘Hole of the Macaws’), the largest sinkhole in South America. It is roughly 100 metres deep, formed from limestone and sandstone that collapsed due to its fragility. We had come specifically to view the hundreds of red-and-green macaws (Araras) that live in the sinkhole. Macaws nest in pairs in the cracks and fissures of the red limestone walls. Our guide told us the tragic history of the site: in the past, a man named Silvino Jacques allegedly made his victims run towards the abyss to escape being shot. Later, the macaws themselves were used as target practice. In the mid-1980s, a Mr Modesto Sampaio bought the farm area, discovered the sinkhole, fished out numerous dead bodies and cattle, and restored the area. After releasing two macaws, others followed, and today the entire cliffside is studded with these colourful ‘jewels’. I walked up to a lookout ledge and cautiously peered over; the bottom was filled with a huge, bright green, algae-filled pond. The next day, we visited the Gruta do Lago Azul (Blue Grotto) cave, about 14 kilometres from Bonito. Upon arrival, we were given hairnets and hard hats as a safety precaution. Tim and I took silly photos of each other looking like we had small, furry animals on our heads. There were 269 steps down to the beautiful azure cave pool. As we descended, I noticed many stalactites, stalagmites, and calcite cascades. The intense blue of the underground lake is best seen when the sun is directly overhead, turning the water from dark blue to an iridescent pale blue. Fossils found in the cave date back over 12,000 years. 

The Road to Campo Grande 

Carlos arrived in a Fiat with a trailer for our luggage, and we set off for the airport—a five-hour drive. On the way, Carlos told us that in the old days, foreigners would ‘take over’ land, create false registration papers, and store them in a drawer where a special type of cricket lived. ‘After a week or so, the crickets ate the corners of the paper, turning it yellow, creating a very effective “old paper” effect.’ Quite ingenious, really. Campo Grande has a population of over a million, and half of them seemed to be gathered at the airport. They sat in large groups on deckchairs, with plastic tables laden with food. Carlos explained that there isn't much entertainment in town, so people gather at the airport just to watch the planes arrive and take off. At X-ray security, I had some trouble as the machine kept beeping. It turned out that the pair of pliers Col used for his butterfly specimens, which I’d shoved into the bottom of my bag, were the culprit. The contents of my bag—seed pods, small containers of jam, peanuts, a dried snakeskin, and river pebbles—were emptied onto the counter for all to see. Luckily, the officials were only interested in the pliers, and I hastily shoved everything back in. I noticed an official writing my name in a book. When I asked why, he ignored me and waved me on. I moved irritably to the departure lounge, worried I’d been branded a potential terrorist and wouldn’t be allowed back in the country. The flight was chaotic—a baby cried incessantly, and a woman prattled non-stop to her mute partner. Every time I passed to go to the loo, I glared at her to no avail; I could cheerfully have shoved a handful of travel socks down her gullet. My sleep-deprived body tossed and turned until we landed in Johannesburg the next morning. Tim and Col were fresh, unable to understand my snarling irritability. Col suggested a strong coffee. I sat with my hands curled around the steaming mug, thinking how amazing it was that not a week ago I was gazing at jaguars in the wild—and I immediately wished I was back there.
