# Chapter 40: BALI 2023 the sisters holiday — A Sisters' Holiday

In order to fit in the only person you betray is yourself - quote Brené Brown The Bali trip is to be a sisters' holiday, so the three of us, Jax, Jo and myself, arrive at the airport with one backpack and a suitcase each. Everything goes well until we’re asked for proof of COVID immunisation, which sends each of us into panic mode, as none of us expected to be asked this more than a year after the event. I find a screenshot of mine from two years previously, Jax finds a much-folded piece of paper that proves her immunisation, but Jo is scrabbling frantically in her bag, before sighing dramatically and saying it’s okay, she’ll just hire a car for a month and drive around the coast. I ask her to check her photos and email. Just as we’re all about to change our holiday plans, Jo waves her phone in triumph and shows it to the bored official, who has been sighing his displeasure until now. For some reason, that’s not shared with us by the authorities, there’s a very long queue to the waiting area and it takes nearly an hour to just walk to the departure lounge.
Jo and I jostle for the aisle seat and I win the right to sit there based on the fact she’s dosed to the eyebrows with sedatives and I would have to keep waking her up to go to the loo. We set off almost an hour late, as they needed to remove all the luggage because somebody hasn't shown up. They’re probably propping up the airport bar I think irritably.
During the flight, we each ask for tiny bottles of liqueur and by the time we land, we have accumulated around eight miniature bottles of alcohol that we plan to enjoy sometime during the holiday.
On arriving in Denpasar airport at ten the next evening, we discover, only after collecting our suitcases, that we should have completed an online visa. It takes me nearly forty minutes to complete all the questions.  We’re all beyond tired and it’s nearly midnight and we still have to find our way to our accommodation, Chanteak, in Jimbaran. Jacquie withdraws some cash to pay for a taxi, which drops us at the gate. I don’t see anyone around and I’m trying to work out where to find someone to show us to our rooms. Jo has meanwhile asked the taxi driver to buy her some bottled water which he is (rightly) refusing to do. I walk around the quiet, still place in the near-dark and come across an outside office. There’s a note on the small desk welcoming us and a key to our room. Now we have to tiptoe around and peer at doors to try and work out which room is ours. At last, I find the right room. There’s a large double bed on the ground floor and a loft bed upstairs, which Jo claims. Jax and I share the double. It’s after 2 a.m., so we all fall asleep as soon as our heads hit the comfortable pillows.
I awake around 9 a.m. to find I’m alone. There’s no kettle to make tea, so I’m feeling irritable and grumpy. I venture outside to the breakfast area and find Jax and Jo sitting there looking wide awake and cheerful, in stark contrast to me. They have already enjoyed a swim. I just grunt, and they wisely wait until I’ve consumed a cup of tea and a coffee before engaging me in any further conversation. I meet Gaye, the owner of Cantik ( pronounced ‘chanteek’ and meaning,’pretty’ in Indonesian), who’s from Australia. She is a tall woman with straight grey hair, and a pleasant smile. She’s very welcoming and we relax under her cheerful disposition. Tim arrives on his motorbike around 10 a.m., having left at 7 a.m. He's happy to see his mum and aunties and immediately orders a taxi Uluwatu and follows behind on his motorbike. We are dropped outside my old haunt, Drifters and have lunch there, then take another taxi to Mana, the beautiful open-air restaurant perched on top of the mountain. 
We walk down to Jimbaran beach this morning. It’s quite a hairy experience trying to negotiate our way along the busy roads, with traffic coming from all directions, three abreast and we arrive hot and sweaty at what turns out to be a local beach. Jo wants to buy a sarong, but it’s not going to happen here. 
So we walk to a place called Jimbaran Hub, a popular spot for digital nomads, and order a late lunch before carrying on to the entrance of a huge hotel; hotel Ayana. The grounds are vast, with manicured lawns, huge tropical trees and clean pathways. The hotel grounds are so big that an open-top tram runs up and down all day. Jax and Jo climb up, but the only other empty seat for me lies between the steps, so I have to climb onto the lower step, swing myself over to the middle and grab a metal pole for purchase. Unfortunately, my hands are slippery with sweat and when the tram suddenly takes off, I fall back onto the pavement. I hurt my left shoulder and lie in an undignified heap. Jo says later that she saw my face disappearing but thought I was playing around. In any event, they tell the driver to stop and I stand up carefully and painfully try to climb on again. Everyone is just staring at me, so I feel irritated and angry with myself and the driver. Jax and Jo are trying not to laugh but their shoulders are shaking. I buy a packet of Kopi Luwak coffee (the coffee that is excreted by a civet cat, because everyone needs to have coffee that has passed through an animal’s rectum given to them as a present). 
We all remain cheerful despite a lack of sleep and have breakfast on the small verandah. I have a Bali coffee this morning at breakfast. It is made like filter coffee and although there’s usually a gritty residue, I enjoy it. We set off to visit the Garuda Wisnu Kencana Cultural Park. It’s a good day to visit—no rain and not too humid. The park is dedicated to showcasing the island's art, dance, music, and traditional culture. We have a wide range of attractions, including breathtaking views, monumental statues, and cultural performances.
The Garuda Wisnu Kencana statue in Bali is one of the largest and most impressive cultural landmarks in Indonesia. The statue, which stands at a height of 121 metres, is a representation of the Hindu god Vishnu riding his mount, the eagle-like Garuda. You can see it from miles away. I love the almost sardonic expression on Vishnu’s face. There are numerous carvings and statues within the vast gardens. We walk around, taking in the sights before finding a place where one can dress up in traditional garments. From here, we go to watch a Balinese dancer. He’s dressed up like an ogre (Ogoh-ogoh)—all googly eyes and bushy hair. He prances around the stage to the sound of traditional music. After him there’s Balinese dancing of a different kind. A couple of beautiful Balinese women dance in gorgeous costumes of red and gold. We watch a few dances, then one of the women looks at me from the stage.
‘Oh no!' I think as she approaches with a smile and a glint in her eye. 

She beckons me to join her and my evil sisters push me forward, so I end up joining this beauty and making a complete fool of myself. I try to copy her graceful movements but fear I just look like an escaped convict trying to evade the police. Thankfully, I can leave after fifteen long minutes and I stalk outside to allow the fresh air to cool my sweaty brow. Jo and Jax join me, giggling at my discomfort. Jo says she video’ed my debut Bali dance moves and I plan to delete it later. We find a restaurant that overlooks the valley and decide to stop for supper. It turns out that the view is a lot better than the food. Jax orders tuna, Jo a chicken salad and for some reason, I decide that duck would be lovely —well, it isn’t. 
Jax’s tuna is cooked to a dry crisp, Jo’s chicken salad may have died of old age, and my duck... well, it arrives as a fossilised bone with bits of hardened feathers glued to it’s side. It must have been re-cooked on at least four different occasions. My suspicion is, each order of duck has been rejected, resulting in the bone being kept for another unsuspecting visitor and re-heated. There’s an interesting long pyramid of rice and a single tomato, so I eat that. Being partially British, (hmm will this statement direct me later?) I don’t complain but stick the entire giant fossil in a tissue to give to a passing stray dog—of which there are many. My bag smells of old duck for quite a while afterwards. We end the day in the pool with one of our dinky bottles of liqueur.
We're joining Tim and some of his friends for dinner. Tim gives me and Jax explicit instructions, saying it’s walkable from our villa. We are meeting at an Italian restaurant called Sa’Mesa. The Sa’Mesa dining experience is based on ‘La Nonna Carmela,’ an Italian grandmother who invites everyone for dinner; the neighbour, the neighbour’s girlfriend, three different uncles, her children, grandchildren, and basically everyone else in her village. In keeping with this theme everyone sits around a long wooden table for many courses.
We set off in the dark, armed with my phone and Google Maps. Jax says she thinks Tim said something about turning right at the bottom of the road, so we head in that direction. I’m carefully following the blue arrow on the map on my phone. The traffic is terrible and we find ourselves walking flat against the side of a rice paddy. Traffic is four and five abreast on this very narrow road and I notice everyone is facing us. Car and motorbike headlights are dancing all over the three of us as the traffic bounces over the rocky road. 
‘Shit!’ I exclaim to the other two. ‘We must be on the shortcut, and it’s one-way only!’

We continue hurriedly in single file, almost falling into the rice paddy as the traffic keeps coming in waves towards us. It’s scary and I’m not sure who is more surprised; the drivers or Tim, when I send him a quick voice note. He messages back to say we should get off the road as soon as we can and take a taxi. He says we’re going in the wrong direction anyway.
I glance again at Google Maps and realise that I’ve actually been following myself instead of the route! (yes I’m that bad). By now, we have figured out that we need to turn our torches on and hold our phones aloft to give the drivers some warning of the three mad women walking in the dark on a narrow road next to a damp rice paddy. We make it to the other side more or less intact and find a clear place to stop. I change to another app, but it says we are thirty kilometres away so we carry on walking, but nothing makes sense on the map.
‘I’m just going to order a Grab,’ I inform my sisters and enter the pick-up point on the app. Tim sends another message saying we need to be there by 7:30 at the latest. I glance at my watch. It’s now 7:05.
'Okay, they say they’re on the way,’ I tell Jax and Jo. ‘Look out for a car registration beginning with GH.’ 
It’s only five minutes later that a Grab turns into the road and the driver leans out and asks if I’m ‘Jenna’. They often get my name wrong, but it sounds close enough and a quick glance at the registration shows the first two letters are ‘GH’. We hop in the back and I sigh. We should make it on time.
Someone at Grab offices is phoning me, but I figure I won’t answer because I’m already in the taxi, so I cut the call. After ten minutes of driving, I receive another message from Tim asking where we are. He says we should be there by now, as we were only five minutes away. He sends me a pin drop and I check it against the direction in which we’re heading. It doesn’t look right. 
Grab is phoning again, and irritably I shout, 
‘I’m already in the taxi!.’ and cut the call again before they can say a word. 
'We are going to Sa’Mesa, yes?’ I shout anxiously at the driver. 
He doesn’t seem to understand, so after another five minutes in the opposite direction to Tim’s pin drop, I ask him to pull over so I can show him on my phone. He stops on the verge and turns around to take my phone showing the directions. He looks puzzled. 

‘This is not where Jenna want to go,' he says, shaking his head. 
‘You Jenna?’ He asks, handing my phone back.
‘Um,I’m Jan,’ I answer truthfully. 
There’s a momentary silence as we all digest what’s happening then turn to look at each other as it dawns on us all simultaneously. Grab calls again and this time I answer. They confirm what we have just realised; we have grabbed the wrong Grab. The real Jenna has been standing where we left fifteen minutes ago and is wondering what happened to her ride. In the meantime, our actual Grab has arrived and is looking for Jan. The obvious solution is for Jenna to use our taxi and for this driver to take us to the right place. This plan is shared with all interested parties and he swings the car around.
We arrive five minutes late; everyone has already started on the unlimited supply of limoncello.
I quickly garble to Tim what happened, and he sighs. He already knows I’m a hopeless map reader; this just confirms it. 
‘ Never mind, you’re all here now.' he smiles.
The rest of the evening is a noisy, boisterous one, with plate upon plate of Italian food being brought out at various intervals. The Limoncello flows, and the conversation is loud. I meet some of Tim’s friends, some of whom I know and around five others I hadn’t met before. There are twelve different courses in all. Everyone becomes more raucous as the evening wears on and the drinks slide down. I keep a tight rein on my intake and order a few glasses of water. Towards the end of the evening, the lights are lowered and a waiter brings a huge, towering cake with my name on it and a few candles. 
Everyone launches into ‘Happy Birthday’ while I maintain that fixed rigid smile people wear when they're being sung to.
It is a great ending to the evening, and I thank Tim for arranging it. My actual birthday is in sixteen days, but Tim is leaving for Europe before then.
Tim is working and tells Jax and I of a nice place for lunch that we can walk to from the beach. We come to a wide and fast-flowing river and try to find a place to cross. Jax calls out that it seems less strong nearer the beach and I tramp over and slowly wade in, and halfway across, I have to stop. I’m holding my backpack over my head and feel the current trying to drag me down into the swirling water. 

‘Jax! ‘ I shout. ‘I’m stuck!’
She now has to wade back in and help me and my backpack across without either of us falling in. We’re both wet with salty water and the sun continues to beat down on us mercilessly. I trudge wearily up the beach towards a restaurant for some water. I take a look at the map and see that we’ve missed the turn-off and have to pass numerous places with huge, gargoyle-type statues adorned in the black-and-white or red chequered cloth (to ward off demons). We walk a bit further down a narrow pathway and come to an inviting restaurant with artificial caves and long, winding pools. There are many stone figurines dotted around the place and huge potted plants and many hued flowers fringe the edge of the vast pool- it’s a good place for lunch.
Today Tim takes us all to a place called Sensorium; a modern Australian-style café with an Asian twist, showcasing amazing coffee and mind-blowing food. The decor is simple and clean. It’s already very busy and the air is abuzz with excited conversation. I opt for the kimchi, prawn and edamame beans in a seaweed sauce, which is delightfully fresh and presented artistically. Jo says her ‘avo on toast’ is a work of art and the best she has ever tasted. Jax and Tim have a mushroom dish. We share a ‘lychee granita’ dessert, which tastes sublime. I end up with a charcoal bun with cream, which tastes a lot nicer than it sounds. What an experience in fine dining, with fresh, beautifully presented food at an affordable price.
Sadly, I have to say goodbye to my darling boy today as he’s leaving for Turkey before ordering a cab to take us to the Eco Resort near Ubud. The drive there changes from sea-level vegetation to montane forest. The air is much cooler as we rise up the narrow road past local dwellings until we round a corner and huge, verdant rice paddies come into view. It’s breathtakingly beautiful. This green sanctuary sits among the lush landscapes of the Balinese jungle. With rice fields and terraces stretching endlessly, charming villages steeped in local customs, pristine forests and ancient temples beneath the coconut-covered slopes of Mt. Batukaru.
We exit the roomy confines of the taxi and are greeted warmly and shown to our rooms. I’m speechless, then not so speechless and, ‘Oh my god!’ becomes our only utterance. Our chalet is wooden and overlooks the vast green rice fields with  streams and mountain views that go on forever. The verandah is huge, wide, and open-plan from our two bedrooms. There are beautifully carved wooden tables and comfortable couches and colourful cushions in the front and a long daybed. Another ornately carved sideboard has a jug of water and the makings of coffee. Each open-plan bedroom faces towards the beautiful view, with stone floors and mosquito-netted curtains. Our rooms have a door leading into the most tropical of outdoor showers. Everything is carved from dark stone; the basins, the pillars surrounding a rain shower, with large round pebbles mosaicing the floor. The entire outdoor area is covered in plants. It is truly beautiful and we are still ‘oohing’ and ‘aahing’ an hour later. Everywhere the eye looks, it rests on pure nature - It is paradise personified. As we walk down stony, moss-covered steps to explore the area we come to a river. Jax and Jo immediately climb into the cool water for a swim. A few hours later we walk up to the open dining area with a large bookcase running against one wall.
One of the cooks tells us that everything we eat is grown and harvested on-site using organic, sustainable practices. Even the water comes from a spring. I feel the quiet stillness envelop me like a warm coat, only the rustling of trees sighing in a gentle breeze and the sibilant sounds of a waterfall. We walk back to our rooms and sit on the verandah reading as a sudden downpour cascades below and chat, play cards until supper time and manage to walk up the glistening, rain-soaked steps without getting too wet. Supper consists of a fresh, tasty home grown meal eaten in silence as we listen to the night sounds of the surrounding jungle; croaking frogs, chirping crickets and winking fireflies. The entire place is magical and we're feeling so lucky to be here. 
The morning chirps of birdsong awaken us. I sit up in bed, stretch and look out over the greenery below sighing in delight. After a cup of tea, we wander down to the waterfall at the bottom of one of the huge rice paddies, continuing down the steep stone steps until we reach a clearing and the sound of the waterfall is louder. After turning the corner, the sun’s early morning rays cut through the mist and the water is the colour of turquoise with a deep green pool glistening below a rustling waterfall. A glorious hour is spent here before heading up for breakfast. After a meal of the freshest of exotic fruit and yoghurt we go on what is advertised as a ‘green hike’. It takes us all around the local villages, past bushes of ripening coffee beans and fruit trees with bananas, coconuts, and oranges. There are vegetable gardens with rows of lettuce, cabbage, potatoes, and carrots. Local dogs bark and sniff as we walk past, locals ride by on their scooters, laden with children and chickens as they wind their way up the steep incline. The path is slippery after the rain, so I pick up a long bamboo stick and use this as added support. A gorgeous smell of jasmine infiltrates the warm air. Chickens cluck and scratch around in the dirt or pop their heads out from small bamboo cages and cows tinkle their way through clearings. We pass an area where Durian is being harvested. This fruit, characterised by its spiny exterior and creamy, custard-like flesh and unique, pungent aroma.

Kadek, a young smiling Balinese man wearing a grey T-shirt and grey cargo shorts greets us warmly and motions for us to find a spot on the raffia mat. We’re preparing for some wood carving so sit cross-legged on a square cushion laid over the cement floor while Kadek goes over the carving process. He hands us a flat, square of wood and some tools and we begin chipping away at the  edges to raise the motif. Both Jax and I are pleased with the results of our work. I stand up a little stiffly from the floor with my piece of wood and thank Kadek for his patience and excellent workshop skills and return to our room with the carvings under our arms.
John, the owner of the Eco Stay is visiting and he comes over to chat to us. He lives in Australia but spends a lot of time shuttling between the two countries. He wears a bright green bandana tied at his throat and colourful board shorts. After an enjoyable chat where he tells us some stories of the early days in Bali, Jax and I walk down the mossy stones to the massage ‘bale’—an open, thatch-roofed area outdoors with two narrow wooden beds. Jo has meanwhile gone for her turn at carving. We are greeted by two pretty, smiling Balinese women. They each hold up a piece of cloth while Jax and I strip to our underwear and lie down for a very relaxing massage. There’s birdsong and soft music accompanying the slow, firm movements of the masseuses’ hands. We are totally relaxed at the end of it to the point where I’m almost asleep.
Sadly, we have to leave today and head towards Ubud. We thank all the staff and John and climb into our taxi.
The traffic increases exponentially the closer we get to the centre of Ubud. Soon the streets become congested with scooters, bikes, cars, and pedestrians. We arrive at Nick’s Pension around lunchtime. The property is quite extensive and runs across a few levels. Although we have been thoroughly spoiled by the Eco Stay, this place is also very pretty. It has a lot of charm and also sits above a rice paddy. An open-plan dining area advertises ‘Afternoon Tea,’ which is supposedly complimentary for guests. For some reason, our arrival causes some confusion but eventually we're brought some black tea and a coconut and banana fritter. After this brief repast we explore the area and take a walk behind the dining room to a road that is much quieter traffic-wise than the main road. A fruit seller seems to sell every exotic fruit you can imagine, from mangosteen, durian, jackfruit, salak (snake fruit), rambutan, dragon fruit, boniberry, soursop, and pomelo. Tonight, two pretty Balinese dancers accompany supper. They are dressed in tight pink and orange tops over beautiful red and gold skirts. Each has a gold belt around their tiny waists. Their dark hair is caught up in a bun and decorated with roses and silver bells. They use very expressive eye and hand movements as they dance. After a few dances, they stop and cast their eyes over the diners for volunteers to join them. I become very attached to my phone to avoid embarrassing myself for a second time and give a little sigh of relief when another unsuspecting woman is approached. I have booked a table for us at Zest. Plants sprout from every surface and open spaces with very comfortable seats and interesting decor. Aside from the plants, ornate pillars, small raffia tables, beautiful glass lamps, various deities in 3D and an indoor tree that curls up to the roof in the centre of the room help to draw the diners in.
We’re taken to a table near the open window and order a cocktail each. The meal is delicious, served in large round metal bowls. Just as we’re contemplating if we should have dessert, a young woman approaches us and sits next to me. She’s wearing a light blue shawl and has short dark hair. She speaks with a strong Russian accent and at first, I think she’s going to ask me for money, but instead asks where we’re from, then she tells us that someone, who wants to remain anonymous, would like to pay for our meal. I’m astonished and keep waiting for her to laugh and say, 
“Only kidding! Pay for your own meal!” But instead, she smiles, says not to worry about the bill and leaves.

We amble over to where the gentleman and the Russian woman are sitting and thank him for his kindness and return to Nick’s Pension marvelling at our lucky encounter. A massage is booked for the evening. I’ve rushed my beer and I’m feeling gassy, which doesn’t bode well for my massage where I will have to exercise great sphincter control. Jamu, you’ll remember, is the turmeric and ginger drink that is much favoured by the local Indonesian community. There’s also a small dish with fried banana which I enjoy from the steamy depths of my rose strewn bath. With wet fingers, I carefully pick up my phone and take a few selfies, trying a few expressions from sultry to what I’m hoping is an enigmatic smile but on checking afterwards, I just look as though I’m suppressing a burp.
The Monkey Forest contains over 600 long-tailed macaque monkeys and three 14th-century temples. After paying, we enter the tree-lined forest walkways. As to be expected, monkeys are everywhere; in small groups, climbing the ancient stone walls and temples, scuffling in the trees and on every path. We have been cautioned not to feed them and to hide our mobile phones, as these wily creatures are quite adept at picking pockets and making off with food and technical equipment. Several large water features have huge koi swimming around, with two small stone tunnels overhead. A few tourists are trying to hug a monkey and get bitten on their (luckily) jeans-clad legs. You can’t cure stupid. I take numerous photos of the macaques in every pose including a pair on the stone wall engaged in some serious simian reproduction the resulting photo will prove a huge embarrassment later.
Today we stop at the Saraswati Temple for some cold dragon fruit juice next to a huge lily pond. Jax and I decide to walk along the Campuhan Ridge. It’s an 8-kilometre return walk along the bukit—the Indonesian name for a hill. It starts near a river and winds its way up to the historic side of a small village on the ridge. The first part of the hike is under the cover of the forest but soon becomes open, dry, and more arid. We come to a pair of huge, ornate walls made from volcanic rock and sit in a warung at a low table surrounded by cushions for another refreshing drink of dragon fruit juice. Today would have been our mum’s birthday, so we decide to return to Ibu Rai, meaning 'Mother Rai', for supper. We order three  Bintang beers and toast Mum. ‘We have to go back to the hotel!’  I wave my hand back in the direction from which we have just come.
“Back to same hotel?” he asks, puzzled. “We not go harbour?”
I am too tired to explain. 'No, we go back hoteli, but then I want you to wait and take all of us to another hotel.'
We drive back in silence while he tries to fathom the workings of strange western women. Back at the hotel, I grab all the waiting luggage and he loads them into his car. As soon as I spot Jax and Jo, I flag them down. 
‘There’s been a huge bugger up!’ I exclaim, ‘but this taxi will take us to the next place.’
The driver remains puzzled when he has to head just 500 metres up the road to where our next hotel is situated. We originally intended to just walk there ourselves, wheeling our suitcases. I have already paid him for a trip to the harbour and back, but he has driven less than a kilometre. He shrugs. ‘Mad Bule’ (the local Bali name for Westerners).
We offload our luggage and I stand in the midday sun explaining my eventful morning to my sisters. Their laughter can be heard all the way down the road.
‘Which one am I then?' asks Jo, peering over my shoulder at the two offending photos.

A walk along the beach follows a quick visit to the painter Mayeur’s former Cyclists move up and down the cement pathway. I leave my sisters to swim in the sea and walk back for a foot scrub and massage in a local parlour. The masseuse is very chatty and soon I am hearing all the latest gossip about the local bule.
‘He’s married, but he has nine local girlfriends,’ she says, pointing to an unlikely looking, portly lothario who strolls past.
‘ That one comes from Australia. He is alone and very lonely, but he is also kind,' she says, as a slim man with grey hair and a beard walks past.
As she chats, I smile, nod then fall into a semi-doze. It is a beautiful, balmy day, and her expert kneading of my feet feels good. She finishes with a snap of each oiled toe that startles me to wakefulness.

Kuta is on the cards today for our last few days in Bali. We have a pleasant driver who chats to us the whole way to the Pandawa Resort. I eventually doze off to the sound of ‘Gecko, gecko,’ the distinctive noise of the Tokay Gecko.
After ordering a taxi to Mana in Uluwatu, where we first stayed, we spend the day relaxing by the pool on huge, round, cushioned seats. I book our last night in Bali at ‘Lullaby Bungalows’ because it sounds nice.
We arrive the next day to a small grassy area surrounded by bungalows or thatched rondavels with a massive bed and an outside shower, similar to the Eco Stay. A walk down to Padang Padang beach requires a squeeze through a couple of tight boulders to some steep stone steps. It’s a popular beach, so I go exploring while the other two swim. I find a small, low-hanging cave where it is much cooler inside.
The afternoon is spent at the bungalows in the gorgeous jacuzzi and outdoor poo and our last evening sees us sitting in the pool drinking the last of our miniature liqueurs. It’s back on the plane tomorrow for Cape Town and George, respectively. Jo and Jax agree it’s been a fantastic holiday in paradise.
