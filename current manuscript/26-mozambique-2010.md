# Chapter 23: Mozambique 2010

The typical start to hot weather sees Col and me bumping into each other at intervals after a hideous month of stress at work. In the end, I arbitrarily fling things into a case, hoping some of it will be useful. We’re off to Mozambique on the East coast of Africa, simultaneously avoiding the crowds of tourists who are here for the rugby World Cup. Mozambique is bordered by the Indian Ocean to the east, with neighbouring countries including Tanzania, Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, South Africa, and Eswatini (formerly Swaziland)in 1752, it was brought under direct control from the Portuguese in Lisbon. As a result of this link with India, numerous Indians trading communities settled in the country, and their influence can still be seen today. Independent Arab trading 'states' survived until the end of the 19th century. Portuguese is the official language although English is widely spoken. In terms of my tech equipment, I have several phone, laptop, and camera chargers, one iPod, two cameras, external speakers, and two external hard drives—you would swear we are preparing to set up a music festival of sorts instead of travelling to Mozambique on holiday. Eventually, everyone sits in their respective seats in the Land Cruiser. Everyone includes Jax and Tim’s friend Carl, so it’s a bit of a squash.I think people are being kept upright by the sheer volume of stuff in the car. For some reason, we have packed, aside from two huge dive bags and weight belts, a bag of plants to take to Marrungulo! The car becomes very slow and sluggish as wet enter Ermelo. Turns out we’ve have been travelling on the reserve tank for the last few hours and are now running on petrol fumes. After finding a station to fill up we head to a nearby restaurant for supper. They also have accommodation, so we debate whether to stay tonight or press on, as we are a little behind schedule so I book a couple of rooms from the inebriated owner, whose mother-in-law (‘the old bag', to use his words) owns the accommodation. I find ‘Lurch,’ his brother-in-law, a pale, hunched, irritated-looking chap with only a fringe of hair curtaining the back of his head. He appears to hate his job and sighs dramatically, scowling at me for the audacity of requesting rooms and at the card machine for not working fast enough. He asks for our address and then writes down ‘Cluevellelly’ in the book despite my spelling ‘Clovelly’ for him. He sighs again then shows me the rooms. We order steaks for supper and although they take a while to arrive, they are well worth the wait—huge juicy pieces that melt in your mouth. ( it is to be quite a few more years before I omit red meat from my diet.) I hear the party long before we get to our rooms and sigh in resignation. The world cup braai (BBQ) is in full swing and vuvuzelas are blown at regular intervals with raucous laughter and drunken shouts. The drunken cackles of one woman increases in volume as the night wears on. I doze off to the sounds of, ‘F…you and ‘F… that’ till I fling a few ‘F’ yous in her direction as well in desperation ( it doesn’t help). Accepting defeat, we get up at 3am and hit the road by half past. It is still dark and misty and the road is full of huge trucks. At last we are on Mozambiquen soil. Strange how the vegetation has changed almost at once. It’s drier, warmer and less manicured. Later we're stopped by two men wearing beige uniforms of the sort we take to be police. One of the men says Tim’s feet were up on the dashboard and he fines us Metacals 2500. Jax leans out the window and informs him that the most he should fine us is M1000, according to her notes, (she consults her diary briefly). He seems momentarily non plussed at this, then says he’ll make it M1500. I then chip in with the request for his superiors name and number which brings the ‘fine’ down to M500 which we pay even though it’s now obvious that we are being bribed. We later learn that the police wear a white uniform. They have really strict road rules here. Near one ‘town’ ( read a ‘few mud huts’), a road sign says 80KM speed restriction followed almost immediately afterwards by a 60Km. sign. Go one kilometre over that and you’re fined heavily. Anything to ensure a fine. So we are being very careful. It makes for some frustrating driving with hardly anything else around to be driving at 60km/hour although there are pot holes galore so maybe it’s a good thing. It gets dark as early as 4:45 and fairly rapidly as well. Poor visibility adds to the tortuous drive. We get to the Tofu area then try to find the Cassaberry Resort on the coastal peninsula. It is a long, dusty, potholed nightmare before we get to the place, having asked a local who is either drunk, high or both for directions. Once there though, our cabanas are really nice. Jax and I have a short walk on the beach en route to the market which is a typical tropical-style thatch roofed area with wooden tables on poles. They sell variously; colourful cotton pants and cloth, T-shirts, food, wine and beer. We are immediately inundated by touts selling beads, small round smoky flavoured rolls (called Pão), long loaves and bags of cashew nuts, which are grown locally. Unfortunately Jax makes eye contact and this little bugger sticks to us like a leech. I buy some groceries and a sim card. As ‘sim’ is the word for yes in Portuguese, this makes for some interesting conversation. ‘I’d like to buy a sim card please.’ ‘ Sim?’ ‘ Sim, sim’ I mean I could have omitted my attempt at Portuguese but where’s the fun in that. Jax eventually turns to the little lad who’s been following us and buys a grubby bag of cashews off him. We also buy some still warm pãu rolls and fob off a couple of inebriated guys called Mr Price and President Van De Merwe, who try to sell us a giant wooden hippo. Later we drive to Tofu dive centre, on the other side of the beach. They have a nice set up here - a long thatch roofed verandah overlooking the sea, raffia basket chairs and a restaurant. Col books three dives for tomorrow. On the way back we stop at a small shack promising ‘Fresh rolls, toasted sandwiches and all juices’. As soon as I enter I realise the promising facade stops at the door. It is a tiny cramped room with cement walls and a lop sided wooden table. A small, grimy TV flickers a soccer game intermittently and a blackboard offers far more in the way of food options than it can possibly deliver. We leave with one loaf of Pãu. We walk down to the dive centre via the beach which has very squeaky sand. Col is still diving so we have a drink on the verandah and wait for him to emerge. A flock of pelicans fly silently over the sea. Col eventually joins us but after two dives he’s too tired to go again. One dive was paid for so Tim and Carl kit up and go for a debriefing in the large pool. I‘m anxious as it’s their first scuba dive in the sea without Col. After supper we listen to a very interesting talk on Manta rays given by a fourteen year old doctor from Holland. Only kidding but he really doesn’t look old enough to be a doctor with his round, smooth skin, curly blonde tousled hair, surf shorts and a colourful T-shirt. He gives a very interesting talk, first telling us that he’s standing in for the actual researcher who is busy tagging Mantas somewhere else. There are two types of Manta he informs us solemnly; the Giant and the Coral Manta. Mozambique is one of the few areas that have both. The female manta ray have a 12 month gestation and give birth to live young- usually every two to three years. They live to about 70, 80 years old and are endangered due to the usual suspects - Chinese fisherman as well as Singapore. Historically, manta rays were caught for their gill rakers and sold to China and Singapore for use in traditional medicine. He ends the talk with a little bow. There’s a brief electrical power failure this morning with rough windy weather and rough seas. Not a great day to go diving so off Col goes. It’ll take more than a hurricane and a tsunami to keep him down. Jax and I go for a long walk on the beach to look for shells. It’s warm and windy and we clamber over sharp coral rock but don’t find any shells, however some of the exposed rocks reveals beautiful red coral. We walk up to the Frelimo monument that commemorates the struggle for independence and the ‘fallen heroes’. Speaking of fallen, the monument lies near a cave where victims of the colonial period were thrown into and drowned at high tide. The monument is a simple monolith structure within a clenched fist, a star engraved on one side and an oval shaped rock. The surrounding cliffs are known as the Tofinho Cliffs and another small monument nearby holds some human remains. Jax debates whether to go for a jog along the road ( sandy and uneven) or the beach (sandy and uneven) the beach wins. It’s cloudy this morning with a cool breeze but the sea looks calmer than it did yesterday. Tim, Carl and I join Jax on the beach for an impromptu spot of Yoga. My downward dog is more horizontal mongrel but it’s a start. Carl is surprisingly supple and is twisting himself into admirable pretzel shapes. Jax and I park under a tree, lock up and tread gingerly over damp mangrove roots, trying to avoid stepping on all the fiddler crabs with their distinctive outsized thick, red-coloured arms that wave constantly at non existent traffic. We pick our way via a waning tide to the edge of the sea and ask a few people we meet along the way if they know where we can find pansy shells and find ourselves at another mangrove. A couple of Afrikaans speaking men say we’ll find them on the other side of the island so we dejectedly walk on. I find a miniature starfish and place it carefully in my plastic bag. As we’re turning back I spot the tell tale markings, half buried in the sand and after a quick scrabble, reveal our first and only pansy shell. A good find as it’s complete and quite large. I’m seated on the verandah chatting to Jax, Carl and Tim with a duvet on my knees. Jax has bandaged my septic, broken toe this so it’s gently throbbing in the cool air. Col has gone diving again and probably won’t surface till this afternoon! Bafana lost 3:0 last night. I try to care but lose to indifference. I buy another lurid yellow and red pair of pants with tassels so I look like a gay cowboy with my leather hat. We have a nice supper of pizza, Barracuda and shrimp while we wait for Cols diving buddy to pitch up - Ham from Hamburg! He’s later joined by Frank from Frankfort, I kid you not. It’s quite busy at the Dive spot. They ( the boys and Jax) have to wait for four young girls to arrive before going for a dive. They leave with nine others on a large rubber dinghy to look for Manta rays. Jax is a bit nervous as she hasn’t snorkelled much before. Tim and Carl receive their Deep Diving certificates so feel well qualified to dive. I have a snack and some coffee then go for a walk along the beach. There’s only the odd blue jellyfish visible in the receding tide. Two little urchins ( the human variety) pester me with the ubiquitous beaded bracelets for sale. I return to find the others have come off the boat. Jax is looking very pale- turns out she’d ‘fed the fishes’ after swallowing a lot of seawater. However they saw and and swam with a giant Manta Ray, a Humped back whale and a Whale shark! Col hurt his knees falling from the boat so he’s hobbling a bit, I’m still hobbling with my septic toe so we make a great pair of hobblers. We leave the resort for Morrungulo, a popular beach resort area located on the southern coastline in the Inhambane province, it is known for its beautiful, unspoiled beaches, excellent fishing, and of course diving opportunities- the main reason we’re here. It’s fine to start with, bouncing over the mostly tar road but around five pm the light dims significantly as the sun sinks below the horizon with no sign of the Morrungulo turn off. We ask a variety of locals who all indicate we should carry on which we do. It’s completely dark by now and visibility almost nil. We find the turn off though and drive another kilometre to the Morrungulo Dive resort a little tired and frazzled so the receptionist’s unhelpful manner isn’t well received. Then we’re shown the dining room where we get to bickering with the man behind the bar because he says we should have booked for supper. We point out that we’ve just arrived. He says we have to book in advance for supper at 8pm - it’s now just after 6pm. Our chalet is almost completely open plan except for one enclosed room with a wonky key and two single beds upstairs. I tell him we’ve paid for five beds and demand that they bring another one. They tell us there aren’t any so I ask them to bring a mattress. I drag one down from upstairs but I’m so angry with their attitude, I want to leave. Tim makes me laugh by saying, ‘ Let’ all think of a song about how much we miss Tofu!’ Today Jax and I go to investigate the potential for food shopping. We find a small thatch-roofed lean-to that has a wood oven and buy ten fresh rolls that have have just come out of the oven. It starts to rain so we run to ‘Daniel’s Bakery’ and buy some water and a large tin of milo. We run back through the rain straight into the shower which removes the first layer of skin as there are only two temperatures-boiling hot or freezing cold. With 5 degree burns, we all sit cooling off and playing cards at the table. I tell the boys they’re like termites with their continuous eating. Carl suggests that he’ll eat wood if there’s nothing else and Tim says if he could eat wood, he’d have eaten his Bau board a while ago. I get the message and make them some more rolls. Night descends as it often does in the tropics,- abruptly! I make fried egg and salad sandwiches with the lovely fresh rolls we bought earlier. The boys are sleeping along each side of the open planned verandah, on padded cushions with sleeping bags. Jax and I take off towards Sylvias Shoal with a red Christmas Woolies bag. It’s a warm day with a light breeze. Coconut trees everywhere send fingers of shadows across the hot sand. There are some pretty vibrantly coloured bougainvillea bushes and a few almost deserted fishing villages with palm leaf huts. I suppose the men are all out fishing. A few small children return our greetings of, ‘Bom Dias!’, with a strange response of,’ Sweety!’ We come across a small stall just after the Baobab Camp and as we gaze into its gloomy depths from the dusty wooden counter to its contents beyond, I pick out the happy shape of a tin of sardines, then a small bottle of gin ( for an evening tipple with coconut water), a box of matches and a tin of condensed milk. The owner of the stall is ecstatic with her morning sales and quickly tots up the cost on her cell phone. It’s quiet at Sylvias; only the temporary managers are here. We order a lime and soda and two coffees. The male contingent of management tells us that the owners have just returned to Mozambique but are feeling under the weather so he and his wife will be leaving tomorrow. He’s drinking a huge glass of gin and raspberry juice and has another one while we’re there. The owner of Sylvias, a large, rotund chap with varicose veins, a red nose and a short life span appears later with a breezy greeting ( or he may have been regaining his breath after his short walk to the bar). We place an order for supper and walk to where we’d earlier seen an old man roasting cashew nuts in his clay oven. He’s still sitting on an upturned bucket in his garden under some large trees. I ask him what a cashew tree looks like and he leaves the bucket to show us one nearby. The yellow fruit, also edible, tastes vaguely like apple. The cashews grow in a pod high in the trees. His little garden is beautifully laid out with flowers and ornaments. He has three kittens mewing around his legs. We buy two bags of cashews off him. The boys return from their two dives around 3pm and sit chomping biscuits and cashew nuts then have boiling hot showers. You can always tell by the yelps and screams emanating from the bathroom when someone is having a shower and afterwards we walk up to Sylvias along the bright moonlit path. Our table is positioned close to the edge of the verandah overlooking the sea. We wait a while for our ‘booked’ meal. The old gigolo with the cut-off vest and winking innuendos is working behind the bar and is well into his 4th or 5th local ‘special’ by the looks of things. We order wine by the glass. Eventually the owner’s short, dark-haired wife appears tottering somewhat unsteadily on her kitten heels to lay the table. Her carefully exaggerated placing of condiments is proof she’s also been enjoying her last day savouring the local ‘specials’. I’m nervous, but the meal is good. As I’m washing a pile of clothes, Gemma who works at the dive shop appears at the entrance ( there’s no door) and announces the dive is off, apparently the boat has broken down and needs to be repaired. Col and I exchange glances and decide there and then that we will leave. We’ll drive back to Tofo to find our missing items and to stay the night. We frantically pack to be ready to leave before we’ll be charged an extra day, leaving an awfully blocked loo as a review of sorts and drive towards Masinga and decide to give the Bamboozi Lodge a try. There’s an A-frame available and they bring an extra bed for Jax. It’s already dark by 5pm and we unpack quickly, all feeling fed up of wearing our clothes for the third day running - me in my previously white top with crab stains and the boys in their lurid Bob Marley coloured trousers. It was cold and noisy for most of the night. Tim says he’d found himself entwined in his netting with his face between the bed planks, Col has the squits and the loo has no water. We have a disastrous stop for breakfast, (the omelettes could have been used as defence weapons,) then head towards the border between Swaziland and Mozambique and pass through Massingo’s busy market day towards Gawasa. All the buildings we pass in Mozambique are in a terrible state of repair; peeling paint and crumbling structures with a strange architecture consisting of many arches and intricate brick design - probably the Portuguese influence but a bit out of place in Africa.
