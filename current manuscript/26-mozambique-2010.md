# Chapter 23: Mozambique 2010

The typical start to hot weather saw Col and me bumping into each other at intervals after a hideous month of stress at work. In the end, I arbitrarily flung things into a case, hoping some of it would be useful. We were off to Mozambique on the east coast of Africa, simultaneously avoiding the crowds of tourists who were there for the rugby World Cup.

Mozambique is bordered by the Indian Ocean to the east, with neighbouring countries including Tanzania, Malawi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, South Africa, and Eswatini (formerly Swaziland). In 1752, it was brought under direct control from the Portuguese in Lisbon. Independent Arab trading ‘states’ survived until the end of the 19th century. Portuguese is the official language although English is widely spoken.

In terms of my tech equipment, I had several phone, laptop and camera chargers, one iPod, two cameras, external speakers, and two external hard drives — you would swear we were preparing to set up a music festival of sorts instead of travelling to Mozambique on holiday.

Eventually, everyone sat in their respective seats in the Land Cruiser. Everyone included Jax and Tim’s friend Carl, so it was a bit of a squash. I think people were being kept upright by the sheer volume of stuff in the car. For some reason, we had packed, aside from two huge dive bags and weight belts, a bag of plants to take to Morrungulo!

The car became very slow and sluggish as we entered Ermelo. It turned out we had been travelling on the reserve tank for the last few hours and were now running on petrol fumes. After finding a station to fill up we headed to a nearby restaurant for supper. They also had accommodation, so we debated whether to stay the night or press on, as we were a little behind schedule. I booked a couple of rooms from the inebriated owner, whose mother-in-law (‘the old bag’, to use his words) owned the accommodation.

I found ‘Lurch’, his brother-in-law, a pale, hunched, irritated-looking chap with only a fringe of hair curtaining the back of his head. He appeared to hate his job and sighed dramatically, scowling at me for the audacity of requesting rooms and at the card machine for not working fast enough. He asked for our address and then wrote down ‘Cluevellelly’ in the book despite my spelling ‘Clovelly’ for him. He sighed again then showed me the rooms.

We ordered steaks for supper and although they took a while to arrive, they were well worth the wait — huge juicy pieces that melted in your mouth. (It was to be quite a few more years before I omitted red meat from my diet.)

I heard the party long before we got to our rooms and sighed in resignation. The World Cup braai (BBQ) was in full swing and vuvuzelas were blown at regular intervals with raucous laughter and drunken shouts. The drunken cackles of one woman increased in volume as the night wore on. I dozed off to the sounds of ‘F… you’ and ‘F… that’ till I flung a few ‘F… yous’ in her direction as well, in desperation (it didn’t help). Accepting defeat, we got up at 3 a.m. and hit the road by half past. It was still dark and misty and the road was full of huge trucks.

At last we were on Mozambican soil. Strange how the vegetation had changed almost at once. It was drier, warmer and less manicured. Later we were stopped by two men wearing beige uniforms of the sort we took to be police. One of the men said Tim’s feet were up on the dashboard and he fined us Meticais 2500. Jax leaned out of the window and informed him that the most he should fine us was M1000, according to her notes (she consulted her diary briefly). He seemed momentarily nonplussed at this, then said he’d make it M1500. I then chipped in with a request for his superior’s name and number, which brought the ‘fine’ down to M500, which we paid even though it was now obvious that we were being bribed.

We later learned that the police wear a white uniform. They had really strict road rules there. Near one ‘town’ (read ‘a few mud huts’), a road sign said 80 km speed restriction, followed almost immediately by a 60 km sign. Go one kilometre over that and you’re fined heavily. Anything to ensure a fine. So we were being very careful. It made for some frustrating driving, with hardly anything else around, to be driving at 60 km an hour, although there were potholes galore so maybe it was a good thing. It got dark as early as 4:45 and fairly rapidly as well. Poor visibility added to the tortuous drive. We got to the Tofu area then tried to find the Cassaberry Resort on the coastal peninsula. It was a long, dusty, potholed nightmare before we got to the place, having asked a local who was either drunk, high or both for directions. Once there though, our cabanas were really nice.

Jax and I had a short walk on the beach en route to the market, which was a typical tropical-style thatch-roofed area with wooden tables on poles. They sold, variously, colourful cotton pants and cloth, T-shirts, food, wine and beer. We were immediately inundated by touts selling beads, small round smoky-flavoured rolls (called pão), long loaves and bags of cashew nuts, which are grown locally. Unfortunately Jax made eye contact and this little bugger stuck to us like a leech. I bought some groceries and a sim card. As ‘sim’ is the word for yes in Portuguese, this made for some interesting conversation.

‘I’d like to buy a sim card please.’

‘Sim?’

‘Sim, sim.’

I mean I could have omitted my attempt at Portuguese but where’s the fun in that. Jax eventually turned to the little lad who’d been following us and bought a grubby bag of cashews off him. We also bought some still-warm pão rolls and fobbed off a couple of inebriated guys called Mr Price and President Van De Merwe, who tried to sell us a giant wooden hippo.

Later we drove to Tofu dive centre, on the other side of the beach. They had a nice set-up there — a long thatch-roofed verandah overlooking the sea, raffia basket chairs and a restaurant. Col booked three dives for the next day. On the way back we stopped at a small shack promising ‘Fresh rolls, toasted sandwiches and all juices’. As soon as I entered I realised the promising façade stopped at the door. It was a tiny, cramped room with cement walls and a lopsided wooden table. A small, grimy TV flickered a soccer game intermittently and a blackboard offered far more in the way of food options than it could possibly deliver. We left with one loaf of pão.

We walked down to the dive centre via the beach, which had very squeaky sand. Col was still diving so we had a drink on the verandah and waited for him to emerge. A flock of pelicans flew silently over the sea. Col eventually joined us but after two dives he was too tired to go again. One dive was paid for so Tim and Carl kitted up and went for a debriefing in the large pool. I was anxious as it was their first scuba dive in the sea without Col.

After supper we listened to a very interesting talk on manta rays given by a fourteen-year-old doctor from Holland. Only kidding, but he really didn’t look old enough to be a doctor, with his round, smooth skin, curly blonde tousled hair, surf shorts and a colourful T-shirt. He gave his talk, first telling us that he was standing in for the actual researcher who was busy tagging mantas somewhere else. There are two types of manta, he informed us solemnly: the Giant and the Coral Manta. Mozambique is one of the few areas that have both. The female manta ray has a 12-month gestation and gives birth to live young — usually every two to three years. They live to about 70 or 80 years old and are endangered due to the usual suspects — Chinese fishermen as well as Singapore. Historically, manta rays were caught for their gill rakers and sold to China and Singapore for use in traditional medicine. He ended the talk with a little bow.

There was a brief electrical power failure that morning, with rough windy weather and rough seas. Not a great day to go diving, so off Col went. It’ll take more than a hurricane and a tsunami to keep him down. Jax and I went for a long walk on the beach to look for shells. It was warm and windy and we clambered over sharp coral rock but didn’t find any shells, however some of the exposed rocks revealed beautiful red coral. We walked up to the Frelimo monument that commemorates the struggle for independence and the ‘fallen heroes’.

Speaking of fallen, the monument lies near a cave into which victims of the colonial period were thrown and drowned at high tide. The monument is a simple monolith structure with a clenched fist, a star engraved on one side and an oval-shaped rock. The surrounding cliffs are known as the Tofinho Cliffs and another small monument nearby holds some human remains.

Jax debated whether to go for a jog along the road (sandy and uneven) or the beach (sandy and uneven). The beach won. It was cloudy that morning with a cool breeze but the sea looked calmer than it had the day before. Tim, Carl and I joined Jax on the beach for an impromptu spot of yoga. My downward dog was more horizontal mongrel, but it was a start. Carl was surprisingly supple and twisted himself into admirable pretzel shapes.

Jax and I parked under a tree, locked up and trod gingerly over damp mangrove roots, trying to avoid stepping on all the fiddler crabs with their distinctive outsized thick, red-coloured arms that wave constantly at non-existent traffic. We picked our way via a waning tide to the edge of the sea and asked a few people we met along the way if they knew where we could find pansy shells, and found ourselves at another mangrove. A couple of Afrikaans-speaking men said we’d find them on the other side of the island so we dejectedly walked on. I found a miniature starfish and placed it carefully in my plastic bag. As we were turning back I spotted the tell-tale markings, half buried in the sand, and after a quick scrabble revealed our first and only pansy shell. A good find as it was complete and quite large.

I was seated on the verandah chatting to Jax, Carl and Tim with a duvet on my knees. Jax had bandaged my septic, broken toe so it was gently throbbing in the cool air. Col had gone diving again and probably wouldn’t surface till that afternoon! Bafana lost 3–0 the night before. I tried to care but lost to indifference.

I bought another lurid yellow and red pair of pants with tassels so I looked like a gay cowboy with my leather hat. We had a nice supper of pizza, barracuda and shrimp while we waited for Col’s diving buddy to pitch up — Ham from Hamburg! He was later joined by Frank from Frankfurt, I kid you not.

It was quite busy at the dive spot. They (the boys and Jax) had to wait for four young girls to arrive before going for a dive. They left with nine others on a large rubber dinghy to look for manta rays. Jax was a bit nervous as she hadn’t snorkelled much before. Tim and Carl received their Deep Diving certificates so felt well qualified to dive. I had a snack and some coffee then went for a walk along the beach. There was only the odd blue jellyfish visible in the receding tide. Two little urchins (the human variety) pestered me with the ubiquitous beaded bracelets for sale. I returned to find the others had come off the boat. Jax was looking very pale — it turned out she’d ‘fed the fishes’ after swallowing a lot of seawater. However they saw and swam with a giant manta ray, a humpback whale and a whale shark! Col hurt his knees falling from the boat so he was hobbling a bit. I was still hobbling with my septic toe so we made a great pair of hobblers.

We left the resort for Morrungulo, a popular beach resort area on the southern coastline in the Inhambane province, known for its beautiful, unspoiled beaches, excellent fishing and of course diving opportunities — the main reason we were there. It was fine to start with, bouncing over the mostly tar road, but around 5 p.m. the light dimmed significantly as the sun sank below the horizon with no sign of the Morrungulo turn-off. We asked a variety of locals who all indicated we should carry on, which we did. It was completely dark by now and visibility almost nil.

We found the turn-off though and drove another kilometre to the Morrungulo Dive resort a little tired and frazzled, so the receptionist’s unhelpful manner wasn’t well received. Then we were shown the dining room where we got to bickering with the man behind the bar because he said we should have booked for supper. We pointed out that we’d just arrived. He said we had to book in advance for supper at 8 p.m. — it was now just after 6 p.m. Our chalet was almost completely open plan except for one enclosed room with a wonky key and two single beds upstairs. I told him we’d paid for five beds and demanded that they bring another one. They told us there weren’t any so I asked them to bring a mattress. I dragged one down from upstairs but I was so angry with their attitude, I wanted to leave. Tim made me laugh by saying,

‘Let’s all think of a song about how much we miss Tofu!’

That day Jax and I went to investigate the potential for food shopping. We found a small thatch-roofed lean-to that had a wood oven and bought ten fresh rolls that had just come out of the oven. It started to rain so we ran to ‘Daniel’s Bakery’ and bought some water and a large tin of Milo. We ran back through the rain straight into the shower which removed the first layer of skin as there were only two temperatures — boiling hot or freezing cold. With 5 degree burns, we all sat cooling off and playing cards at the table. I told the boys they were like termites with their continuous eating. Carl suggested that he’d eat wood if there was nothing else and Tim said if he could eat wood, he’d have eaten his Bau board a while ago. I got the message and made them some more rolls. Night descended as it often does in the tropics — abruptly! I made fried egg and salad sandwiches with the lovely fresh rolls we had bought earlier. The boys slept along each side of the open-plan verandah, on padded cushions with sleeping bags.

Jax and I took off towards Sylvias Shoal with a red Christmas Woolies bag. It was a warm day with a light breeze. Coconut trees everywhere sent fingers of shadows across the hot sand. There were some pretty vibrantly coloured bougainvillea bushes and a few almost deserted fishing villages with palm-leaf huts. I supposed the men were all out fishing. A few small children returned our greetings of ‘Bom Dias!’ with a strange response of ‘Sweety!’

We came across a small stall just after the Baobab Camp and as we gazed into its gloomy depths from the dusty wooden counter to its contents beyond, I picked out the happy shape of a tin of sardines, then a small bottle of gin (for an evening tipple with coconut water), a box of matches and a tin of condensed milk. The owner of the stall was ecstatic with her morning sales and quickly totted up the cost on her cellphone.

It was quiet at Sylvias; only the temporary managers were there. We ordered a lime and soda and two coffees. The male contingent of management told us that the owners had just returned to Mozambique but were feeling under the weather so he and his wife would be leaving the next day. He was drinking a huge glass of gin and raspberry juice and had another one while we were there. The owner of Sylvias, a large, rotund chap with varicose veins, a red nose and a short lifespan, appeared later with a breezy greeting (or he may have been regaining his breath after his short walk to the bar).

We placed an order for supper and walked to where we’d earlier seen an old man roasting cashew nuts in his clay oven. He was still sitting on an upturned bucket in his garden under some large trees. I asked him what a cashew tree looked like and he left the bucket to show us one nearby. The yellow fruit, also edible, tastes vaguely like apple. The cashews grow in a pod high in the trees. His little garden was beautifully laid out with flowers and ornaments. He had three kittens mewing around his legs. We bought two bags of cashews off him.

The boys returned from their two dives around 3 p.m. and sat chomping biscuits and cashew nuts then had boiling hot showers. You can always tell by the yelps and screams emanating from the bathroom when someone is having a shower. Afterwards we walked up to Sylvias along the bright moonlit path. Our table was positioned close to the edge of the verandah overlooking the sea. We waited a while for our ‘booked’ meal. The old gigolo with the cut-off vest and winking innuendos was working behind the bar and was well into his fourth or fifth local ‘special’ by the looks of things. We ordered wine by the glass. Eventually the owner’s short, dark-haired wife appeared, tottering somewhat unsteadily on her kitten heels, to lay the table. Her carefully exaggerated placing of condiments was proof she’d also been enjoying her last day savouring the local ‘specials’. I was nervous, but the meal was good.

As I was washing a pile of clothes, Gemma who worked at the dive shop appeared at the entrance (there was no door) and announced the dive was off — apparently the boat had broken down and needed to be repaired. Col and I exchanged glances and decided there and then that we would leave. We would drive back to Tofo to find our missing items and to stay the night. We frantically packed to be ready to leave before we’d be charged an extra day, leaving an awfully blocked loo as a review of sorts, and drove towards Masinga and decided to give the Bamboozi Lodge a try. There was an A-frame available and they brought an extra bed for Jax. It was already dark by 5 p.m. and we unpacked quickly, all feeling fed up with wearing our clothes for the third day running — me in my previously white top with crab stains and the boys in their lurid Bob Marley-coloured trousers.

It was cold and noisy for most of the night. Tim said he’d found himself entwined in his netting with his face between the bed planks, Col had the squits and the loo had no water. We had a disastrous stop for breakfast (the omelettes could have been used as defence weapons), then headed towards the border between Swaziland and Mozambique and passed through Massingo’s busy market day towards Gawasa. All the buildings we passed in Mozambique were in a terrible state of repair; peeling paint and crumbling structures with a strange architecture consisting of many arches and intricate brick design — probably the Portuguese influence but a bit out of place in Africa.
