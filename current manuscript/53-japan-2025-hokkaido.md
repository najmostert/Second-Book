# Chapter 44: JAPAN 2025 — HOKKAIDO

I have a cold shower, taking care not to disturb my newly acquired eyelash extensions. My hairdresser informed me gleefully yesterday that Japan is experiencing the worst flu epidemic in thirty years. Charming! I take a handful of vitamins though, just in case.
I stand waiting for the check-in counters to open, despite a long queue and all check in staff being at their stations, chatting with each other. Eventually, an official gestures to me after staring at me for a moment.

I drag my case over and she holds out her hand for my passport without making eye contact. I hold onto my passport until she turns her head to see why I haven’t placed it immediately into her waiting hand.
“Good morning, “ I say pointedly. She merely raises an eyebrow and gestures again for my passport. I feel like slapping her sullen face. Is it just me or does everyone hate their job? The rest of the check-in takes place in total silence until she barks something like,
‘Case!'
I haul my heavy suitcase onto the scale and I’m surprised to see it’s within the weight limit. I start to feel hot in my jeans, boots and thick jacket and my rucksack seems to have suddenly filled itself with iron bars.
While waiting for the call to board, I try to activate my eSIM, which must be done before leaving. I become increasingly frustrated as my attempts to activate it fail. I take numerous video clips showing my attempts and send these to a very patient person in the helpline, including numerous blurred screenshots of my thumb and phone. I sweat profusely and have to keep wiping my eyes. I’m dressed for a very cold Japan but it’s at least 35°C in Cape Town. I show my boarding pass to the air hostess and she motions for me to go in a different direction from everyone else. This entails walking down fifty steps, crossing the tarmac alone and then being directed by the flagman—or whatever you call him—towards a place somewhere between the plane and the end of the runway.
I don’t understand what’s happening and why I am receiving this ‘specialised' treatment while all the other passengers have probably already entered the plane and are sitting down. I stand there like an idiot near the huge wing feeling hot and fed up, so I shout out to the linesman asking why I have to come this way. He yells back something about safety and jet fuel, which doesn’t enlighten me at all. He keeps gesturing for me to come forward so I eventually figure out that I am supposed to walk behind him and ascend some steps at the back of the plane.
At least I have an entire row of seats to myself for the first leg of the trip to Addis Ababa, but we leave almost an hour late, which worries me since I don't have much time to catch my connecting flight. 
In Ethiopia I have to race through X-rays and board the plane for Japan. Despite having booked an aisle seat, I find myself sandwiched between two passengers. I call the hostess over and show her my booking. She says an aisle seat is available further back. This entails asking some gentleman to vacate his seat halfway through his film but I reason he didn’t book the seat.
I eat my third meal in twelve hours and hear the pilot say the plane will be arriving in Korea in an hour. Korea? I frown, have I heard correctly?  I sit up and look around, as though the answer to this situation lies in the tired faces of my fellow passengers. In a way, it does—they are all Korean.
Wait, am I on the wrong plane? I get up and sway toward the back where a gaggle of stewardesses are congregating. I tell them I’m supposed to be going to Japan but I hear we are landing in Korea. They nod and confirm that is correct.
‘But what about Japan?’ I ask, pulse racing.
‘Oh, there’s another flight, but it leaves twenty minutes after we arrive.’ Smiles the steward.
I walk back to my seat and gather my things. By the time we land, I’m on my feet with my rucksack, clutching my jacket and phone. I try to get past the other passengers, who are now all in the aisle grabbing cases and jackets from the overhead bins.
‘Excuse me,' I mumble, nudging past people and accidentally striking most of them with my rucksack. I manage to get halfway down the aisle and ask a large American man if he will let me pass.
‘Hey lady, we’re all catching other planes,' he snarls at me.
‘Oh, in ten minutes?’ I retort and with a firm thwack from my backpack, I pass him. 
Unfortunately, I only get a third of the way up the aisle at the front and give up. I have to accept that I am going to miss my connecting flight to Japan.
There’s a bus waiting on the tarmac that takes us to the airport where we wait in a long, snaking line at X-rays. One man tells me not to worry; they are all going to the same plane and it will wait. I feel so cross when they ask me to remove my boots as it just wastes precious time and run to the boarding gate with my heavy rucksack, clutching my phone, watch and boots in the other hand.
Despite all the rushing, we have to wait for the plane, so I wander off to look for coffee. I chastise myself for being the worst type of passenger and mentally vow to ‘chill’.
Sudden turbulence means my hovering mouth misses the plastic cup of red wine and finds my jeans instead. I mop the stain ineffectually thereby ensuring it remains permanent. They serve what I suppose might be supper but since I have lost a day and have been eating non-stop since yesterday, it could be anything.
We arrive in Tokyo around 8 p.m., and I can feel the change in temperature just walking from the plane into the airport interior. I walk to the carousel and wait for my suitcase. Several elderly Japanese ladies are hoisting multiple suitcases off the carousel with apparent ease, putting me to shame as I stagger about when mine arrives, almost falling. My new boots are squashing my toes painfully, which means I am hobbling along to passport control and past customs and take the Skyliner to Ueno.
When the train hisses quietly into the station, several cleaners dressed in pink aprons hop on board. When I think they are about done, I hoist my case from the platform onto the train and suddenly, I am met by a very angry cleaner who is shouting a torrent of furious Japanese at me. 
‘Sūtsukēsu o doke te, o machi kudasai’.

The gist is that I must take my case and get back onto the platform until he says I can board! I mutter ‘Sumimasen’ several times and bow out backwards. When it is safe to enter, I find my seat, then Tim FaceTimes me and we have a noisy chat until I remember that talking on your phone is frowned upon. I mutter to Tim that the other passengers will think I am a typical noisy gaijin and the woman across from me smiles.
I get off at Ueno and walk outside the station and find a large silver button on a pillar and press it to magically summons a taxi. I already love this place. My taxi arrives and the back door opens automatically. In no time, we discuss the destination and price and I am on my way to Hotel Koko. I wiggle in my seat and discover that the seat has been warmed for passengers. Can Japan be any more welcoming! I gaze out of the window at all the bright lights of Tokyo. After tapping my credit card to pay for the ride, I stumble up the stairs to reception, wheeling my case behind me.
I then use my room card to tap the lift for my floor and nod at a couple from Jakarta in the lift. I’m only guessing they’re from Jakarta, they could be from Burnham-on-crouch for all I know. My room is small and compact but has everything I need: a double bed, a large-screen TV and all the usual amenities, including the ubiquitous Japanese toilet which has a warm seat and three modes for spraying one’s nether regions. I haul my huge suitcase onto the bed and open it. I fear I may have suffered a minor aneurysm while packing because I can only see two pairs of trousers, one pair of socks, two white T-shirts, three of the same face cream and about twenty miniature tubes of toothpaste!
I dress in my black jeans, T-shirt, boots and my pink puffer jacket as I wait in the lounge for Tim to arrive then wander over to a huge vending machine and after a few taps, I come away with a refreshing sports drink which I sip gratefully.
Tim shows up wearing a green jersey that he says he’s been wearing for a week. We walk down to the nearest coffee place and find one that takes the business of coffee very seriously. We choose the beans from a good selection and luckily for me, they have oat milk. A cute Japanese barista with dimples brings our coffees over on a little silver tray, complete with a tiny glass of water and a card that states the type of bean, the aroma, origin, etc. I turn and give him my thousand megawatt smile and he backs away quickly. A quick glance at my phone shows I have a sprig of parsley nestling between my teeth as though it’s taken root there. It’s probably been there since Korea. I sigh and remove the offending greenery with a pencil. My boots are still hurting me, pinching my toes, which are already red and raw from the pressure, so after coffee we go to look for a shopping mall to find some other shoes. I find a pair of ankle boots for an affordable price then walk through a small park nearby, where the pale skeletal branches of trees claw at the bright blue sky with bleached fingers and the pathway is hard with frost. Apropos of nothing much, Tim comes up with some haikus—some of them are rather good. 
White snow blankets all, 
Dark branches against the white, 
The world holds its breath.

This area, Ginza, is full of name-brand shops and businesses. We find a Tully’s and I order coffee with soy, which tastes like wet cardboard. However, we discover there’s a round table with plugs in the wall nearby so Tim is able to do some work online. I watch in amusement as one middle-aged gentleman in a black suit and tie hears his name called for collection and he does a dainty little run, elbow bent, to fetch his drink. 
After a few hours of work, we walk to Shinjuku, where the famous 3D cat ‘lunges’ out of a ten-storey building. Technology here is really amazing. A  small ramen place in Kabukicho features a long room with chairs down one side and separate cubicles for each person. A hatch opens at intervals to reveal a plate or drink. Each cubicle has a small tap for water. The hatch slides open and my ramen arrives via a disembodied hand, complete with an egg and seaweed ( the ramen, not the hand). It’s very tasty.
As we leave the restaurant and round the corner, we see the giant head of Godzilla atop the Gracery Hotel/Toho Cinema building. It roars every hour, with smoke and lasers around its eyes and mouth for effect. 
Everywhere, colourful posters are plastered all over the city walls showcasing host clubs for women. The most popular male hosts’ photographs and ratings feature below their youthful smiles. There are around six thousand male hosts working in various clubs and they all look very young with quite feminine features, as this is seen as attractive to most young Japanese women. These women spend thousands of yen each month on their chosen host, sometimes becoming deeply in debt as the bills rise. 
Inside the clubs, the host listens attentively, flirts, and pours drinks for the guests, who pay handsomely for the drinks that are sold at exorbitant prices. When a woman becomes attracted to the host—perhaps drawn in by the extra attentiveness of this pseudo-romance, they can take their ‘relationship’ to the next level outside the club. Although this is not officially allowed, there’s not much club owners can do about it. Shinjuku appears to have the majority of these clubs where young men, ranging from eighteen to their mid-twenties can make a tremendous amount of money—especially if they are highly ranked. I am left feeling a bit sad as there must be many lonely women around if they’re prepared to pay so much for male attention and judging by the number of host clubs being advertised, they do.
We continue walking around the busy, colourful streets full of people and I notice the fashion trend among young girls; thigh-high boots and very short dresses topped with large, fluffy jackets. Their thighs are left exposed to the elements and many are blue with cold. We find a small stall selling taiyaki. I can already smell the deliciousness wafting through the air and I can’t wait to try one. Taiyaki is a Japanese fish-shaped pastry commonly sold as street food. It is made in the shape of tai (red sea bream), with a sweet red bean paste, custard, or chocolate filling. Tim buys us one each. Whilst it’s tasty enough, I find it too sweet for my taste. Apparently, they were first sold as street food during the Edo period (1600s–1800s). Today, tai-shaped pastries are often given as thank-you gifts (omiyage) to guests at weddings as a common gesture of celebration and luck.
A shinto shrine; Hanazono Jinja, was founded in the mid-17th century and is considered one of the most historical shrines in Japan. Its history stretches back to before the Edo period when it was revered as a guardian presence in Shinjuku. At the purification water basin, called a temizuya, we rinse our hands and mouths before proceeding to the main worship hall. There, we place some coins in a small pond and ring the long, pulley-type wooden bells to alert the deities of our presence. I enjoy these simplistic rituals regardless of an affiliation to any deity. As we walk down the ancient side streets of Kabukicho, I admire the old wooden houses with paper walls and Zen-style gardens.
There’s a small red coloured bar tucked away on one of the side streets which is open. A young, trendy Japanese woman with short dyed blonde hair serves at the bar, which is filled with bottles, glasses, crystal ornaments, and soft lights. Against the back wall, there are hundreds of foreign banknotes stuck to the wall, left behind by travellers from many different countries and I immediately look for a South African note and find one. After finding a small table Tim orders sake, which arrives in small, square, red lacquered containers called masu. I learn that masu are often made from high-quality wood such as cedar, giving them an elegant appearance and these are a beautiful red colour. The finish enhances the visual appeal and also helps preserve the sake's flavour. The use of masu has both practical and cultural significance: the square shape symbolises abundance and prosperity, as they were traditionally used for measuring rice. Using these containers for serving sake carries a connection to wealth and good fortune. This is apparently the correct way to drink ‘sake’, which, I later find out, simply means ‘alcohol’ and not specifically the fermented rice drink we have ordered. The atmosphere is cosy and inviting. Anyway, ours is hot, as requested. It has a delicious light flavour.
An Englishman enters and orders a drink, and we pass a few pleasantries as we leave the warm confines of the little pub into the icy evening. Our bellies warmed with sake. One store sells all manner of maids' uniforms. Not the sort you don to dust Mrs Ponsonby-Smythe’s antiques, though. No, these are very short dresses with lots of frills, little white aprons, ribbons, bows and knee-high lacy socks. I have a feeling Mr Ponsonby-Smythe would approve.
A window displays a few adorable puppies, so enter the narrow glass door. The puppies are housed in square glass ‘kennels’ with cushions, water, and dainty bowls of food. We watch for a few minutes as they amble unsteadily around, falling over each other like little fluffy acrobats. I am in the process of videoing two pups when the shopkeeper hurries over and flaps her hands at me.
‘No photo! No photo!'  she exclaims. I stop filming.’Sumimasen!’
Today is Tim’s 32nd birthday and walk over to meet him at Blue Bottle Café. I give my boy a birthday hug then head to a soba noodle restaurant for lunch. A huge plate of cold, brown zaru- buckwheat soba noodles arrives accompanied by soy sauce and some large pieces of crispy vegetables. 
At first I'm unsure about eating cold noodles, but I’m pleasantly surprised—it is really delicious. The idea is to select the noodles with chopsticks, dip them into the ginger-infused soy sauce and slurp them down. Slurping your noodles is encouraged and is seen as a polite way to indicate your enjoyment and appreciation for the food. The flavours blend perfectly, and I am thrilled to be sharing this experience with Tim on his special day.
Feeling replete, we pay and walk down the busy, colourful streets of Roppongi, passing the massive Tokyo Tower, an iconic landmark known for its distinctive design and vibrant colours. Completed in 1958, it was inspired by the Eiffel Tower in Paris and stands at 333 metres (1,092 feet), it was the tallest structure in Japan until 2010. The tower is painted white and ‘international orange’ to comply with air safety regulations. Originally built as a broadcasting tower it has served as a communications hub for over 50 years.
Afterwards, I head back to check into my hotel. I ride up a rickety lift to the reception area where I’m greeted by a tired-looking man who speaks very little English. 
‘Irasshaimase. Kochira ga o heya no kagi desu’

He hands me a huge room key instead of the usual card so I figure the hotel must be quite old. When I enter my room, I find it small and compact as I have come to expect in this country where land is at a premium. Despite the size, the room has everything I need and I’m grateful for a cosy place to rest.
A small bar fridge sits under the desk in my hotel room, so I immediately place the small milk containers I brought from Cape Town inside. 
Later, I meet Tim in Minato, and we walk to the restaurant ‘Tenkaipin’ for a fresh sushi experience. The room is cosy, featuring a counter that runs the length of the narrow space; an open-plan kitchen area with two chefs busy preparing a set sushi menu for no more than six couples. The chef explains each beautifully prepared plate to us in English.
We enjoy an array of delicacies; sea cucumber, various seaweeds, slices of incredibly tender raw fish, nigiri-style pieces, caviar and sea urchin. Fresh tuna and salmon are delicately wrapped around rice. There are also finely sliced fish pieces in savoury jellies, grated cabbage with crab and mackerel. to finish off, we receive a tiny square of red bean paste encased in a sweet jelly. Various flavours of sake are on offer with our meal, and they bring a large bottle that corresponds to each drink.

This morning Tim we head to the busy airport and board a small plane for Sapporo. The flight is just over an hour and as we fly in to land at ‘New Chitose Airport’, I see the entire area below is covered in thick snow. I may be alone in this but now shiver with excitement at my first sight of snow in a long while. Sapporo is the birthplace of Miso Ramen, a rich, savoury Japanese noodle soup featuring a broth heavily seasoned with fermented soybean paste (miso).
It’s another hour-long journey from the airport to town. My new shoes are also starting to hurt a lot, putting pressure on my red, sore toes, so I will have to buy yet another pair of shoes, as there’s usually a lot of walking on our trip. The bus drops us outside our hotel, ‘The Monterey Edelhof’, a very un-Japanese-sounding name. We can only check in after 3 p.m., but they allow us to leave our luggage, leaving us time to buy some onigiri  from  a side street vendor. I eat the rice and seaweed-covered delicacy with tuna mayonnaise with relish.
Tim leaves at 6 p.m. to fetch Marina, his girlfriend. They come past my room an hour later and pop in so Tim can introduce her to me. Marina is a tall, pretty Japanese girl with a ready smile and porcelain skin. I apologise for not joining them for supper and say I will chat with them tomorrow, but right now, all I want to do is sleep. I have a quick bath and fall into bed.
I sleep for ten hours, so I’m feeling much better this morning. The air is cold, crisp and quite refreshing. I actually prefer it to really hot weather because my energy levels are higher. En route to breakfast, we pass some huge ice sculptures. The annual winter ice festival has commenced in Sapporo and everywhere you look is full of these sculptures. I walk very carefully, afraid of slipping where it’s icy. The sculptures are mainly designed to resemble Japanese cartoon creatures so there are many I don’t recognise. We find a department store that has good coffee and ride up to the fourth floor to a small corner shop. 
After coffee, Tim and I follow Marina around the department store as she moves briskly from counter to counter, looking at various pastries. She buys a bag full for us to eat later with our coffee. Marina and I leave Tim working and walk to the outdoor fish market as she wants to buy some fish to take back to Hong Kong with her. Mounds and mounds of giant crabs greet us at the market. They have other seafood and fish but hundreds more crabs—huge, glistening, bright orange crabs that vary in size, from hand-sized to the size of a large tray.
Marina buys a few large, raw crab legs for us. These are cooked in a huge pot of boiling water before peeling and presenting us four cooked legs on a paper plate. She also buys a jar of orange caviar and offers me a spoonful. I am not that fond of the salty roe, though. Back to the café to wait for Tim and when he’s done find a huge underground shopping mall for the ‘Tartlet and snow cafe’ that is popular with anime enthusiasts. A local woman is eating cheesecake with a huge blue-haired doll seated at the table; she’s the popular anime character Hatsune Miku. Another man in the corner also has a huge anime doll with him. I have a few photos taken with him and the doll.
Afterwards we walk to Odori Park to see some more ice sculptures. There are two long streets of them; some life-size. Huge anime characters compete with cartoon characters, animals and even one building made entirely of ice, complete with windows and doors. It is dark by now and all the fairy lights are on. Tim bought me some cleats for my shoes last night. I put them on over my shoes this morning and we go in search of coffee. I’m really grateful for them as my walking is much easier walking over slippery paths. The former old Hokkaido government building is next for a quick tour. Our guide reminds me of Sven-Erik, my late father-in-law. He gives us an interesting talk about the history of the place. According to ‘Sven-Erik-san,’ the building was first a school. An American advisor, Horace Capron, planned to construct the building for the Kaitakushi office, topped with an octagonal dome that was a popular architectural style in the US at the time.
Marina’s on standby and needs to get to the airport so she heads off leaving Tim and I to continue exploring the area. The Sapporo Beer Museum looks interesting though it feels less like a museum and more like a place for drinking biiru. They have a small section of wall displaying old beer labels from the 1950s to the present day, while another area showcases huge posters from the same period.
We forgo the beer, as it’s too crowded and neither of us does well with queues. Outside the scenery is beautiful, with colourful lights draped around trees and buildings against the snow, creating a very festive atmosphere. I notice a particularly interesting tree with vivid reddish-orange leaves called 'Sorbus commixta', or Japanese Mountain Ash. The dark green, ash-like leaves turn yellow, then red/orange or purple in autumn. A nearby shopping centre has restaurants that display sampuru outside to coax people in. Sampuru you will remember, are replicas of food made of plastic, wax, or resin that mimic the appearance and sometimes even the texture of real food. The radio tower looks beautiful in the evening light; it seems to glow with an internal light. 

ASAHIKAWA
The journey Asahikawa takes over two and a half hours. I have a window seat and watch the thick, snow-covered landscape rush by. Some houses have snow piled up to the roof. We stop at five stations en route, and each time the doors open, a blast of chilled air enters the otherwise warm compartment. Asahikawa is known for its thriving craft culture, particularly Asahikawa furniture and powder snow which is extremely dry and feels remarkably light and fluffy.
It starts snowing again quite heavily as we walk along the platform dragging our suitcases through the thick, snow-covered pavement to our small hotel. It is quite lovely outside—fresh and pretty, with lights twinkling everywhere. We are forty minutes early for the restaurant we want to eat at, so we spontaneously decide to pop into a karaoke bar. After paying for half an hour and ordering a beer and a small sake, we find our cubicle. Inside, there are two tables, a huge screen and some tablets where you can choose the songs you want to sing. We select some really random songs and are amused to see that although the lyrics are correct, the music tracks appear to be random and Tim posits that it’s probably because they don’t have the rights to play the actual tracks. Anyway, we butcher a few songs, including some by the Arctic Monkeys. Both of us sound like we’re being tortured which leads to more laughter than singing. A street featuring another row of ice sculptures are some of the best we have seen; showcasing sea creatures, forest animals and lots of sparkling, twinkling lights everywhere. We admire all the sculptures before walking back to the hotel.

FURANO
The train pulls into Furano, famous for its rolling purple lavender fields in mid-summer. However It’s snowing heavily so we drag our luggage to the nearest nomad-style coffee place before checking into our accommodation. Our room has a great view of the ski slopes. The hotel has some great amenities such as a private onsen on the 9th floor, two massage chairs, and laundry facilities. We trudge through the thick snow and find a restaurant tucked away in an alleyway. It's small and cosy as sit at the counter to order a selection of side dishes; edamame beans in butter and small bites of fish and chicken. There’s another local couple sitting at the other end of the bar. They listen in on our chatter, smiling and nodding at us. The woman and Tim have a short chat in Japanese and I’m amazed again at how much he actually knows. The meal is tasty, filling, and inexpensive. We dress up in our coats, hats, gloves and scarves and head out into a snowstorm back to our hotel.

Since we have missed breakfast at the hotel, we wander off to find somewhere else to eat. The paths are clear in some places but still thick with snow in others. We find a coffee shop and order two cappuccinos. I am amused to notice that they have ‘earnest’ serviettes and grab a few for my runny nose.
After leaving the warmth of the café, we walk through the thick snow and slippery pathways. I’m sure my cleats have prevented a few broken bones. There are many stalactites of ice clinging precariously from the roofs of buildings, ready to stab an unwary passer-by as they melt.
The bus and then a taxi takes us to Ningle Terrace in Nakagoryo, a tourist spot with picturesque timber cottages and workshops in the woods that sell traditional handicrafts. The snow is even heavier here if that is possible, but the paths are clear. Most of the wooden chalets are closed but we find an open hut and pop in to look at some artwork. The pieces are simply designed and referred to as ‘Giclée prints,’ created using a jet printer. I notice a large wooden café that’s open for business and we enter gratefully. After removing our shoes, we sit around a wide counter to order—tea for me and a coffee for Tim. They have a recessed window filled with colourful homemade pottery cups and plates in various wabi-sabi designs. 
Drinks are followed with ice cream in green tea flavour, served with some mochi.
We take a taxi back to Furano centre and our hotel. It is almost 3 p.m., so I don the garments provided by the hotel for the purpose of wearing to and from the spa and head up to the 9th floor in my baggy waffle-print top and trousers. I later discover I have taken the larger sizes, which means when Tim joins me later, his trousers end mid-calf and his top is too tight.
I am delighted to see I am the first one up here and therefore have a choice of three private onsen to choose from.There’s a small area that includes a hairdryer, body lotion, and shampoo. I carefully undress and head into another section after opening the sliding door. The air is warm and steamy. A large, square pool of natural hot spring water overlooks a breathtaking view of the snow-covered mountain through huge glass windows. I step gingerly into the hot water, then sink into its enveloping warmth. It’s so relaxing sitting in the hot water with such a beautiful view I almost fall asleep. While Tim is taking his well-deserved turn in the steamy room, I pad into the adjoining room with the massage chairs and find one empty. I grab an ice lolly to cool me down and have an amazing massage. That chair does some serious squeezing and kneading. Tim comes in from his soak in the onsen and takes over the chair for his own massage experience. I am feeling so much better; my flu symptoms have also improved.
We wake to another snow blizzard outside, so dress appropriately and head off in search of coffee and find a small café run by two elderly women who call themselves ‘grannies’. 
‘ Ohayō gozaimasu’, says Tim and they return the greeting.
‘ Chōshoku o onegai shimasu’, he asks one of the old ladies.

She beams at him and says, yes we can eat breakfast and points to a small table.
Tim has a traditional Japanese breakfast of rice, pickled vegetables, noodles, fried fish, omelette, and fried leeks. I opt for French toast (sans honey). Both meals are very tasty and we thank the sweet old ladies as we leave.
‘Arigatō gozaimasu!’ I say confidently, this being one of the few Japanese phrases in my rather limited repertoire.
At midday, we head back to reception, pick up our luggage and head out into the blizzard. It is snowing heavily again and we have to drag our suitcases through the snow towards the station.

ASAHIKAWA
The train to Asahikawa speeds by and just the tops of houses are peeking out of mounds of thick, fresh snow. I wonder how they are able to leave their homes as most seem to be blocked in. We arrive in Asahikawa around 3 p.m., so we are able to check into our hotel straight away, which is not far from the station and very central. 
The shopping mall is also close by, so we head there after check in, in search of supper a short while later. There’s a Japanese-Italian restaurant off the main road and I have a delicious cheesy dish. Tim has something more locally flavoured. After wandering around the mall we find a room full of claw machines (known as UFO catchers, ’Yoo-ehf-oh Kya-chah’s in Japan). 
Most of the prizes are boxes of anime figurines. I take the first try to grab a box with the blue-haired ‘Trafalgar Law’ figurine. Naturally, I don’t get it but the box has fallen close to the edge. Tim has a go and manages to get the box to fall into the chute. We are both surprised and pleased as it’s a known fact that getting the claw to actually grab a prize is notoriously difficult. So I’m now the proud owner of a tall blue haired action man to add to my collection. 
OTARU
Snow is coming down hard as we leave the hotel. We’re heading to Otaru today, which is near the coast. Otaru is famous throughout Japan for its exquisite, hand-blown glassware and musical craftsmanship.
Our train carriage has empty seats which is great but we have to change over at Sapporo station and then there’s standing room only. 
Our hostel is a traditional wooden house with tatami mats, low tables, paper walls and simple decor. After checking in we walk far into town past thick ‘hedges’ of snow. A small sushi restaurant has recently opened and we enter hopefully and sit around a wide counter with a number of other patrons and order rice, miso soup, prawns and a bowl of snot-like mush which is tasty if you close your eyes and chew quickly. We stop at a museum to look at stained glass windows which were created in England during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. These pieces were originally part of church window fixtures. In recent years, many of those churches have been demolished for various reasons, yet these stunning works of art have survived. Remarkably, through an unexpected turn of events, they found their way to Japan. There are approximately a hundred of these colourful glass pieces throughout the small museum. 
Afterwards we try to find a place for supper but being Valentine’s day, it’s not easy. The locals have created little heart shaped ‘caves’ in the snow in which they’ve placed lighted candles. Some ice ‘caves’ have flowers ‘floating’ in the ice. Just off the pavement is a fish drying contraption. Hooks are attached to various poles and fish attached to the hooks. A motor turns the rows of fish in the wind, thereby air drying them. Part of the canal has a row of paper lanterns strung across the water. All in all, the air is romantic and pretty.

NONORIBETSU

The Ainu are the indigenous people of Japan and Russia, having inhabited northern Japan, particularly the island of Hokkaido, long before the arrival of the first Japanese immigrants. Today, however, there are very few speakers of the Ainu language left. It took many years for the Ainu to be officially recognised as an indigenous group with their distinct culture and traditions. In fact it wasn't until 2019 that the Ainu of Hokkaido received legal acknowledgment as Japan's indigenous peoples. In the past, they were known as hunter-gatherers, but today, many individuals of Ainu descent live like any other Japanese citizen. In fact, a significant number may not even be aware of their Ainu heritage, largely because they concealed their identities for many years to escape discrimination. Currently, many Japanese prefer to avoid discussing minority groups, opting for simplicity rather than confronting what they see as complex issues. Likewise, Ainu individuals often choose to lead quieter lives, away from the spotlight.
Despite these challenges, there’s been a recent increase in opportunities to engage in Ainu cultural activities. Dedicated individuals are working diligently to keep traditions (which nearly disappeared), alive and are exploring new ways to integrate Ainu culture into contemporary life, which in my book is a great thing. In Meiji Jingu gardens Tim buys a roasted sweet potato from a vendor near the gate before walking under the huge torii gates. Torii gates, typically red in colour are a visual and symbolic marker indicating the entrance to a sacred area where deities (kami) are believed to dwell. Passing through a torii gate is a symbolic act of purification. I notice people are bowing before passing underneath and Marina explains that it is customary to show respect for the sacred space and the kami. The forest is  man-made, created to offer shade for visitors to the shrine, which was built in 1920 to commemorate Japan's first modern emperor and empress. After passing through another torii gate we wash our hands under a bamboo tap as per custom, then fill our cupped hands with the holy water and take a few sips, and wash our hands again. The climb to the shrine is enough for my thighs to protest, but the view is great from the top. There’s a wedding procession taking place and I move closer to watch. The bride is led by two women dressed in short white kimono-style garments over wide red skirts. The bride herself is resplendent in white from head to toe. She is wearing what looks like a huge white pillow over her head but is in fact a tsunokakushi, a silk headdress worn in Shinto weddings, symbolising the bride's modesty and willingness to become a gentle and obedient wife. The word tsunokakushi means ‘horn hider’, signifying that it hides the bride’s ‘jealousy horns’ so she can become more gentle. A man wearing a green jacket and wide white trousers follows her with a huge red parasol, which he is trying to keep over her head. 

We return to the hotel and I note with amusement the growing number of new laundry bags stacking up along the wall. I later join Tim and Marina for a walk into a cold evening in search of supper. We walk up some steps into what we think is a bar but turns out to be a hostess club (kyabakura). As in Korea, these are a type of Japanese bar where female staff provide entertainment and companionship to customers. They sit next to the customers, mostly men, lighting their cigarettes, engaging them in conversation and light flirtation as well as serving drinks- actually everything is to encourage buying more drinks. There is a ‘no-touch’ policy and if customers become a little frisky, the hostess may leave the table. Anyway, it’s obviously not the place for us, though I am curious.
Kamakura
After a poor night’s sleep (I blame the sake we had at supper), I decide to walk to Starbucks for a coffee. I nip off to the loo and as I step in, the toilet lid glides up to greet me. I do love a polite toilet. Then to settle cold buttocks onto the lovely warm seat. Bliss! I will really miss Japanese loos and idly wonder how much it would cost to import one. The washbasin is equally accommodating: the tap sprays warm water over my hands and then blasts warm air over them to dry them. When I get back, Marina has found a boulangerie in the mall and is busy piling a small tray high with delicious pastries. I add a crusty brown bun with cream cheese for my own consumption. We eat these tasty delights standing at Le Pain’s small round table, shedding crumbs everywhere before taking a train to a new place for Tim and me; Kamakura, which is an hour away. Luckily, we have seats so it is quite a comfortable journey. We arrive in a quaint seaside town south of Tokyo. There are dozens of Buddhist Zen temples and Shinto shrines here.
It is very busy and there are people bustling around everywhere: families, couples, and dogs in prams (a big thing here). It’s the Emperor’s birthday today, which explains the crowds. We come to another huge red torii gate and step to the left, bow, and enter the park and take a leisurely amble over old stone bridges before taking a left into Tsurugaoka Hachimangu gardens, a garden with magnificent peonies around the Minamoto pond. After paying a nominal entry fee, we look around. A tonbi (black kite) flies overhead, and I watch it glide onto a tree. The garden is full of carefully cultivated peonies—such delicate petals, almost a translucent pink. There are peonies in small beds ranging from pink to purple. They are huge, the size of a ball. Some grey herons dart at fish around the edges of the pond. 

After walking quite some distance to another temple, Jomyo-ji, which is Zen Buddhist, we head towards a tea ceremony (chadō). The temple is set in a Zen garden and is well worth the walk there. White stones, trimmed trees and sakura buds are everywhere. Originally founded in 1188, many of this temple’s artefacts and designs relate to the Ashikaga clan. Japanese white-eyes (small round birds) dart around the plum trees. The tea ceremony takes place in a low, traditional wooden house. We enter and remove our shoes as is customary. This proves unfortunate, more so for the dignified tea master (chajin) than for me. My feet, having walked for many a mile in the last few weeks encased in both boots and nylon stockings, now emanate a very strong odour. The pong radiates within a good metre of my person. I walk purposefully towards a small wooden table against one wall but notice that both Tim and Marina have sunk gracefully onto a tatami mat and are now sitting cross-legged, looking meaningfully at me. I sigh and wander over towards them. I try to lower myself with equal grace onto the mat, but that ship sailed many years ago. Instead, I land with a bump, my legs splayed unbecomingly outwards.
‘What’s that smell?’ asks Tim, turning towards me accusingly.
I move my legs to the right. ‘Not my fault,’ I mutter. ‘My boots and nylons do not let my feet breathe.’
The tea master (a woman clad in a black kimono) arrives, daintily tip-toeing across to us. She is wisely wearing a mask, but as she approaches, I detect a quivering under the mask. She sinks carefully to the floor, holding a small, square, wooden tray with three round bowls and an equal number of rose mochi (steamed glutinous rice beaten into a chewy dough, filled with sweet red bean paste). She moves in front of Marina, kneels, bows, and offers her a small bowl of matcha tea, after turning it carefully in her delicate hands three times to the most colourful side. As she does this, she follows the traditional etiquette. From Marina, she moves to Tim, who bows in return and accepts the tea. I watch in fascination as he takes three short, sibilant sips and a final slurp. She approaches me and pauses. I twist my body into a pretzel-like pose, my legs so far to the right that I think I am going to topple over. I surreptitiously sprayed my feet earlier with a sample of perfume, but the underlying ‘eau de sweaty feet’ remains as a top note. She gracefully bows, hands me my bowl and exits hastily.
I ask Tim how he knew to sip and slurp, and he says he visited a tea ceremony with his dad a few weeks earlier in Kyoto. Apparently, Col embarrassed everyone by eating the mochi before the tea ceremony began, forgetting to thank the lady or bow, and omitting the delicate sips in favour of a short gulp. I laugh affectionately at his typical behaviour.
Having slurped our tea and chewed our mochi, it is time to leave. Tim and Marina stand up with ease and watch as I provide a short comic interlude by rolling over on all fours, reaching out for purchase on the flimsy paper-walled ledge and rising with a loud groan, just missing tearing a two-hundred-year-old paper wall in the process. We don our shoes—I for one am glad—and venture outside where I find a varied tit in a cherry blossom tree and pause to watch it. 
We have ordered a taxi to Zaimokuza and it arrives at the gate. The restaurant owner asks us to wait in the garden, until we are called in and led to a separate room. The room is sparsely decorated with a small wooden table and four chairs. The owner enters and informs us of two menu options: ‘short’ or ‘varied’. I go for short and the other two have varied. Tim’s meal consists of sardines and beans, pickled tuna and yam, chicken and mushroom soup, beef sukiyaki, a small palate cleanser of pickled something-or-other, cold soba noodles served with dried fish roe and lastly, a dessert of mochi and jelly served with a black bean. These are all served individually.  We are given copious amounts of hōjicha (roasted green tea).
Tim and I walk to Shinjuku tower and Starbucks for coffee. A really long queue snakes out the door today. I spot ‘my’ waitress dressed in civvies in the line and I wonder if she is not sick of the place. We walk back to our hotel with our coffee and later the three of us go out in search of tonkatsu (pork rolled in crumbs and fried). It comes with cabbage, lemon, and rice and is absolutely delicious. The old lady who serves us is very sweet, with a lined face and a twinkle in her eyes. Marina is flying back to Hong Kong today to work so Tim and I take a train to Ueno, managing to sit down halfway. It is a gorgeous day, and from the station we take a slow walk to Ueno Park where a lively spring market and fair is taking place. We stand and watch an open-air stage with samurai dancing with colourful umbrellas and then a sword fight. Their swords look real but it is hard to tell. The sun is setting as we take a taxi to Matsugaya to look for sampuru. I'm looking for a novelty sampuru to take home. After finding the shop, It’s nearly closing time, so I immediately rush around trying to decide what I want. There are so many options, from a bowl of noodles with a pair of chopsticks suspended over the ‘steaming broth’ to a plate of ‘fried chicken and vegetables'. They are not cheap, so I end up with a small note holder with a stack of pancakes and a knob of melting butter, and a key ring holder in the very realistic shape of a chicken drumstick. I also buy a set of ‘edamame beans’, which I plan to use as a necklace.

Today we decide to return to the sweet little old lady that we went to with Marina for my last katsu. Her son is helping her this time. Tim tries to ask for fatty pork for him and non-fatty for me, and she starts giggling so we’re not sure what he orders; I suspect he’s called me fatty but the correct food arrives, for which we are grateful as it’s as delicious as it was before: crispy and tender. 
Sadly it’s my last day in Japan. Tim has booked a room in Ueno for another few days, so we decide to take a train there. He has to carry my suitcase, his case, and two rucksacks up and down the very many flights of stairs in the station because they are too heavy for me. The number of steep stairs in Tokyo’s underground is notorious.
‘I used to come here all the time when I first came to Japan’ Tim tells me looking around wistfully.
It’s a glorious day, cold and fresh with a weak sun. Tim suggests we have tea in a nearby teahouse. It’s a traditional wooden house and the owner smiles as we enter and motions for us to remove our shoes. My boots were cleaned this morning, so they’re reasonably fragrant. We walk up another level to a room that has three tables where the bases are treadle sewing machines, a huge, overstuffed sofa heavy with cushions in one corner and a small toilet off the room. The shelves are decorated with various teapots, cups, and lots of hand-knitted tea cosies. A few chairs have Union Jack cushions. It is all very quaint. We order Earl Grey tea each, and these arrive in huge brown teapots. After putting our shoes back on, we head for the Yanaka Cemetery. From the graveyard, we find a Zen garden with a huge stone Buddha. He wears a smirk on his stony visage. I like him. A fully blooming sakura tree catches my eye with a pink carpet of petals at its base. I tell Tim that I will miss seeing them all in bloom around Tokyo, but he may be luckier as he is staying a few more days. We walk back to his hotel where he checks in and then unfortunately, it’s time to leave and we have just enough time for a big hug before I have to run with all my stuff through the turnstile to my train.

Sure enough it’s half an hour later and we’re taking off and he’s groaning in the seat. I fumble in my rucksack for a couple of Panado and hand them to him.
