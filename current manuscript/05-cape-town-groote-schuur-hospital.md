# Chapter 4: Cape Town — Groote Schuur Hospital

Cape Town felt like a completely different place when I returned. Apartheid was slowly dismantling, there was an air of genuine hope and life felt full of possibilities. I enjoyed nursing and loved the patients. My very first view of the massive white hospital on the hill was of several nurses literally clinging to lamp posts in the typical November wind —which, it must be said, was more of a howling gale.
In many ways, I was incredibly fortunate as I was presented with opportunities during my early career that were rarely given to junior nurses. I worked in the operating theatre with Professor Chris Barnard who had performed the world's first heart transplant (I assisted removal of a piece of glass from a man’s heart though, not a transplant). He gave me a lift in his fancy sports car one day and invited me to one of his holiday homes; he was married to Barbara at the time but was a notorious flirt.
I later flew in a helicopter to drop off emergency medical supplies on Robben Island, which served as a harsh maximum security prison at the time and was where Mandela was incarcerated. I worked as the nurse-in-charge of the hospital's first peritoneal dialysis unit. We had four dialysis machines and four patients who came in daily. After a few years there I was given another opportunity to transfer to the nursing education department, eventually becoming a nursing tutor. I found that I absolutely loved teaching and took to it like the proverbial duck to water. I produced educational videos with a nursing colleague, learned how to operate a video camera, draft scripts, map out camera cards, shoot scenes and I illustrated the video myself. I was subsequently selected to attend a year-long Clinical Educator’s course and found that, given the right encouragement, I was capable of passing with distinction. With this newfound confidence, I passed my final exams as a Registered Nurse with 98% and received four academic awards including the gold badge for obtaining the highest marks in the country. Being fond of art, I found myself painting festive murals on the walls of the children’s ward at Christmastime. This came to the attention of the Vice-Head Matron, who formally commissioned me to complete a farewell painting for the Chief Matron who was retiring. I worked for hours after my shifts on a detailed painting of the famous hospital. The day of her farewell arrived and I was in a tremendous hurry. I woke up late, and as it was the middle of winter it was still pitch dark. I dressed quickly in my uniform of blue trousers and a white top, pinning my epaulettes to my jacket. I carefully wrapped the painting in brown paper, felt around in the dark for my shoes and slipped them on then rushed to work and darted around the ward until it was time to present the gift. I walked quickly towards the massive assembly area, carrying the canvas carefully. Because I was focusing so intently on not dropping it, I didn't notice my uneven gait at first. The hall was huge with a raised stage at one end where the departing Chief Matron was seated alongside the Medical Superintendent and the second-in-charge. Along each wall sat rows of dignitaries and heads of departments. I felt intensely self-conscious walking down the centre of the room with all eyes fixed on me and glanced down at my feet and nearly did a double-take. I was wearing two completely different shoes; one was flat and dark brown, while the other was a navy shoe with a distinct square heel. I suddenly realised why I had been walking with such a strange gait all morning but there was absolutely nothing for it but to carry on. I wished the earth would open up and swallow me whole, especially when I caught a stifled giggle from one of the ward sisters. I arrived at the podium and waited while the Chief Matron was presented with my artwork by the Medical Superintendent. To compensate for my lopsidedness, I assumed an awkward stance, keeping one foot flat on the floor while letting the other hover slightly off the ground so I didn't look completely asymmetrical. I had to endure several excruciating minutes with a hovering foot while the outgoing Matron unravelled the brown paper and exclaimed her joy at the painting, holding it aloft for the entire room to see. She thanked me warmly, remarking that it must have taken ages to paint. ‘The only issue occurred this morning,’ I replied honestly, as the room listened. ‘I was in such a hurry to get to work that I put on two completely odd shoes.’ The Chief Matron laughed appreciatively and I took the embarrassing walk back down the length of the hall with everyone chuckling loudly as I bobbed rhythmically up and down past them. 

Eve and the Midnight Drive: 1980

During those halcyon days of nursing, I had a close circle of friends that met up regularly. One of them, Brenda (not her real name), was a bit of a rough diamond. She was tall, well-built, with short, straight blonde hair; she looked like someone you wouldn't want to mess with. One afternoon she asked if I would like to go to a party that evening. It was my night off so I agreed and asked if I could bring my flatmate and fellow nurse along. Brenda agreed. Eve said she was keen. She was a delicate, petite girl with short dark hair, a round fringe and neat round glasses; we had been sharing a flat in a quiet neighbourhood for over a year. Brenda picked us both up in her old, blue Volkswagen Beetle. It was seven o'clock in the evening and the sky was already turning a deep shade of violet. ‘Where’s the party, Brenda?’ I asked as she zoomed off, one hand casually on the wheel and the other clutching a cigarette. ‘It’s actually a braai in the forest,’ she replied with a grin. Eve and I exchanged worried glances. ‘That’s miles away from here!’ I exclaimed. ‘Yeah, but Robbie’s having some of his mates join us from the Bellville bike gang.’ She took another deep drag of her cigarette and yelled at a passing motorist through her open window. Brenda had a fondness for motorbikes and their owners. Eve and I were uneasy. We drove on in a tense silence. Eventually, some forty-five minutes later, Brenda turned off the tarmac and we found ourselves driving deep into a dense forest of pine trees, navigating a narrow, dusty dirt path barely wide enough for the Beetle. She suddenly swung the car into an open clearing equipped with several brick fireplaces and a small concrete utility building in the corner. There were around twenty people gathered, all clad in black leather and heavy biker boots. The men had long hair tied into ponytails or flowing free and the only other females—three in total—were similarly dressed in tight jeans and heavy denim or leather jackets. Eve and I stepped slowly out of the car and looked around. Loud rock music was blaring from a massive tape recorder and booze was flowing—bottles of whisky and crates of beer stacked high. Everyone appeared to be half-cut already. A tall man with massive shoulders, long, greasy hair and an ill-defined moustache ambled over, waving a beer can. He and Brenda hugged tightly and she gestured vaguely in our direction. ‘Brought you some girls!’ she grinned. ‘Eve and Jan, this is Robbie.’ He smiled, revealing yellowing teeth, one of which featured a prominent gold inlay. ‘Hey. Make yourselves at home. Grab a beer.’ He threw his arm around Brenda and the pair disappeared towards a smoky fire where a rowdy group had gathered. ‘Shit!’ I whispered, turning to Eve. ‘I’m really not feeling good about this. Do you even know where we are?’ ‘No, we seemed to be driving forever. Do you know anyone here?’ I shook my head. ‘Brenda just said it was a party! I thought it would be at someone’s house, not a hundred kilometres away.’ I picked up a can of beer for each of us and we perched nervously on the cold concrete ledge of one of the unused fireplaces. A few leather-clad men ambled over towards us causing Eve and me to shift closer together. ‘Hey, why are you two being so antisocial?’ boomed one greasy-haired, red-eyed individual, slurping noisily from his beer. ‘Why not come over to the fire and be friendly?’ His companion giggled. ‘Um, no thanks,’ I said, offering a trembling smile. ‘We aren’t staying long. We just came for a quick drink.’ I waved my can at him. The friend leered down at us. ‘Come on, join in, we don’t bite!’ he guffawed, taking another heavy swig. ‘We’re fine here,’ Eve stated firmly, her glasses gleaming ominously in the firelight. Deciding we weren't going to be much fun, the pair finally ambled off. We sat side-by-side on the cold brick wall for what felt like hours, watching the crowd of bikers become increasingly rowdy as the night progressed. We couldn't see Brenda anywhere. Suddenly, a loud altercation erupted across the clearing and two bikers began swinging heavy punches at each other, one of them wielding a length of chain rolled tightly into his fist. We had just fended off yet another heavily intoxicated biker with wild eyes when I decided I had reached my limit. It was now close to midnight. I told Eve I was going to find Brenda and tell her we wanted to leave immediately. I walked nervously towards the main fire where meat was sizzling on the grill and people were shouting and dancing to heavy rock music. ‘Hi,’ I ventured hesitantly. Six pairs of wild eyes swivelled towards me. ‘Um, do any of you know where Brenda is?’ Everyone laughed and a man with a long ponytail slung his arm heavily around my shoulders. ‘Brenda is with Big Rob,’ he leered. ‘They’re having themselves some fun. I skeem you and I should go have us some fun too, hmm?’ He took a drag of his cigarette and blew a thick plume of smoke directly into my face. I coughed, waving the smoke away. ‘No thanks, we're leaving. Thanks, everyone,’ I muttered vaguely, breaking away and walking briskly towards the small concrete building shrouded in braai smoke. I noticed a small wooden door and knocked. Receiving no answer, I turned the handle and stepped inside, my eyes slowly adjusting to the gloom. It was a tiny outhouse with old farming implements stacked in the corner. I could dimly make out two figures in the darkness. ‘Brenda? It’s Jan. We need to leave now. Can you take us home?’ I heard a burst of male laughter. ‘Hey Bren, your little friends want to leave!’ There was a sudden shuffle, followed by a metallic clinking sound on the floor. I looked down. Brenda’s car keys were sliding across the dusty cement floor, stopping at my feet. ‘Just take my car,’ mumbled Brenda’s voice from the dark. ‘I’m going home with Robbie.’ She laughed, followed by the wet sound of a kiss. I stood there in disbelief. ‘Um... okay. You can fetch it tomorrow, then,’ I stammered. I grabbed the keys, bolted out of the gloomy room and hurried back to where Eve was waiting anxiously. I told her that Brenda had abandoned us, thrown her keys at me and told us to drive ourselves home. There was only one problem: neither Eve nor I knew how to drive. We were both eighteen, but neither of us had ever had access to a vehicle and we relied entirely on trains to get to and from the hospital. There were, however, no trains departing from the middle of this forest. We quickly assessed our options. Eve looked at me. ‘We can either stay here all night, or one of us has to drive that car back.’ ‘Have you ever driven before?’ I asked hopefully. ‘Only a tractor on a farm when I was twelve years old. Never a car.’ ‘Right, well, you still beat me in experience then. You have officially drawn the short straw.’ Eve was game for anything within reason. It would be considered entirely unreasonable to have someone whose total driving experience consisted of steering a farm implement to navigate a vehicle out of a pitch-black forest, onto a major national highway and back to an apartment forty minutes away without a map. But we figured anything was preferable to remaining in the woods while members of a biker gang tried to bludgeon each other to death. I climbed into the passenger seat while Eve settled behind the wheel, the very epitome of grim determination. She shuffled her seat forward and wiped the condensation off the windscreen with her sleeve.
‘I’ll try to guide you as best I can,’ I told her as she inserted the key into the ignition. ‘I have absolutely no idea how to get it into gear,’ she admitted, grinding the gearbox loudly a few times. I peered down at her feet. She slammed one foot down onto a pedal and turned the key; the Beetle coughed violently, took a wild leap forward and stalled. ‘I think I need to put my foot on this one,’ she muttered, staring intensely at the pedals. For the next twenty minutes, the car bucked and snorted like a spooked horse but at long last she managed to establish forward momentum—albeit at a steady ten kilometres per hour. I sat on the absolute edge of my seat, straining to make out the narrow dirt path in the darkness. Eve fiddled frantically with the various knobs on the dashboard, alternatively setting the windscreen wipers clattering and the indicators flashing. A few minutes later, she successfully located the headlights. They were stuck on high beam but we weren't about to quibble over details; at least we could see. It took a while but eventually the dirt track cleared and we found the main road. Eve bumped the Beetle slowly onto the tarmac which was thankfully deserted and asked me which way we should go. Truthfully, I hadn't the faintest idea but I told her to turn left. We progressed along the empty road in total silence. I strained my eyes against the dark to see if I could recognise a single landmark. ‘Can you go a little faster?’ I pleaded. We were moving so slowly I could have counted individual blades of grass. ‘No, I can’t,’ she muttered through gritted teeth. ‘You’re lucky I even found first gear. I am not risking stopping this car and not being able to start it again.’ This seemed logical. And so we crawled along at a snail's pace until I finally spotted a highway signpost. It indicated that Cape Town lay some forty kilometres ahead. Whilst we didn't live in the city centre, at least we were moving in the right direction. ‘I’ll drive until we get closer to town, then I'll turn right,’ Eve announced. I turned to respond and saw that she was sitting so far forward her face was almost pressing flat against the glass of the windscreen. There was a single, fat bead of sweat trembling on the very tip of her nose, threatening to drop at any second. For some reason, the sheer absurdity of it hit me and I found it hilarious. I started giggling, which quickly spiralled into a fit of uncontrollable laughter. Eve shot me a brief look, her large glasses gleaming in the dashboard light. ‘It’s not funny!’ she cried. ‘I am absolutely petrified right now. What if we encounter traffic?’ She had a point. At that exact moment, two cars zoomed irritably past us from behind, hooting violently as the bright high beams blinded them in their rearview mirrors. Eventually we approached a set of traffic lights. The signals turned amber, then red. Eve had no intention of stopping. With quiet, terrifying determination, she cruised straight through the intersection. ‘That was a solid red light we just drove through,’ I noted, somewhat unnecessarily. ‘What is that smell?’ Eve asked, sniffing the air. We both sniffed. ‘It’s the exhaust, I think,’ I said, turning around in my seat to look out the rear window. A thick, billowing veil of black smoke was trailing behind us like a gothic bridal train. I spotted another signpost through the smog; it indicated that Mowbray lay to the right. It was definitively on our way. After two hours of driving entirely in first gear, chugging through three consecutive red traffic lights, the Volkswagen came to an abrupt, grinding halt on the pavement directly outside our block of flats in a magnificent cloud of black smoke. We turned to each other, the tension breaking instantly and burst into tears of hysterical laughter. I threw my arms around her. ‘Thank you for getting us home alive,’ I gasped, smiling gratefully. ‘I think with a few proper lessons, you’ll be driving like a professional.’ We wended our way wearily up the stairs to bed. 
It is still hard to believe that a mere five years from that night, Eve would be driving massive, long-haul commercial trucks all the way from Cape Town to Namibia, but she did.
