# Chapter 29: Japan 2012: The Journey Begins

Tim has completed high school and is taking a gap year; he is due to undertake a TEFL course in Japan to teach English. I decided to join him, as I have always wanted to visit the country, even if only for a long weekend. Last night, I took a sleeping pill with a glass of red wine, and as a result, I have no memory of going to bed. Tim was annoyed to discover I had shoved a packet of Pronutro cereal into his suitcase —he does not understand my nagging fear that he will starve while in Japan. I am not at all convinced he will appreciate the strange things they might serve for breakfast. Our tickets were printed and the suitcases ‘pupated’ in cling-wrap before we boarded the first of our three flights. After settling into our seats on the huge Emirates Boeing 777, we were handed hot cloths to wipe away the sweat and grime of the terminal. We left late, and I was anxious that this might affect our connection, as we had only two hours in Dubai before flying on to Japan. I placed my earphones firmly on my head to watch Twilight Saga, though I couldn't tell which instalment it was. My foot went to sleep— seemingly refusing to wait for the rest of me to catch up—so I had to get up and stamp around the cabin. We were seated at opposite ends of the plane; Tim was sandwiched between two tiny Japanese girls, both perhaps four feet tall. I, on the other hand was sat next to a huge Nigerian gentleman wearing a voluminous white robe and emitting a heavy pong of eau de B.O. I sat defiantly in the aisle seat and prayed that the middle seat would remain empty—thankfully, it did. I pulled out my blue inflatable pillow and proceeded to blow it up. It took ages, and my cheeks began to ache. The pillow, now fully inflated, was placed behind my head, but in less than a minute, it was flatter than a pancake. I sighed and drifted off anyway, just as the cabin crew began their ‘chicken or beef’ ritual down the aisle. I wiped the dribble from my chin and sat up for supper (or breakfast—given the time difference, it was impossible to tell). I removed the foil wrapping to reveal congealed rice and beef strips. I sipped my red wine, slipped half a sleeping pill under my tongue, and took a large gulp of wine to wash it down. I’m not sure what happened after that, as I must have been unconscious, but I awoke a few hours later with my head leaning against the huge shoulder of my fellow passenger, dribbling in an unseemly manner. I sat bolt upright and returned to the aisle seat, apologising profusely. The man smiled, revealing broken teeth, and said, ‘No problem, you can sleep again,’ as he patted his shoulder invitingly. I gave an involuntary grimace. ‘Er, no thanks, I’m fine now.’ I quickly retrieved my tangled headphones from around my legs and clamped them firmly on my head. Before long, I was snoring again, only to wake to breakfast time. I found myself eating a rubbery omelette accompanied by a bouncy soya sausage. The tea was refreshing, and I sipped it gratefully while trying to locate Tim. I turned around and found him several rows behind; he was sitting very still, with the two Japanese girls' heads nodding towards each of his shoulders. He caught my glance and smiled. Tokyo Arrival After collecting our luggage at Narita Airport, we stood before a passport control officer who took our fingerprints and a digital photo. I spent the time praying that I wouldn't look like the woman in the image—she looked about a hundred years old. The airport officials were incredibly polite and helpful. As Tim wheeled his cling-wrapped ‘pupa’ from the arrivals hall, I suddenly realised I had left my cabin case on the plane. Five minutes later, one of the cabin crew returned, wheeling it towards me with a smile and a slight bow. I mumbled my apologies and thanks. My bladder was full again, so I entered the ladies'. I soon found myself sitting on the warmed-up plastic seat of an interesting toilet—a feature of Japanese culture I hadn't yet encountered. A plastic arm to the left featured various buttons bearing Japanese characters. I pressed one, and there was a sudden whoosh of warm water spurting towards my backside. I rose quickly in alarm, then fell back onto the seat with a mixture of trepidation and excitement. Another button brought a musical rendition of Yankee Doodle to my nether regions. I pressed a third button, which instantly triggered a loud, artificial flushing sound to permeate the air— presumably to drown out the unseemly noise of one’s private business. This was brilliant, except I couldn't find the button to turn off the flushing, singing, whooshing melody. I eventually pressed all the buttons in quick succession, hopped off, and everything finally went silent. Tim took his turn at the ‘buttock-warming ceremony’ after buying us bus tickets to the hotel. The bus station was five minutes away. I changed €100 into Yen and bought a coffee from a vendor—a sweet girl wearing bobby socks, a short skirt, and a black fringed bob. Everything was so clean. A hurrying salaryman stopped long enough to show us the correct bus stop, though we struggled with the mime and gesticulation. We stood somewhere near the signpost, but that was apparently the wrong procedure; I was gently admonished by a woman in a black uniform, white gloves, and a whistle. She indicated the exact spot behind the white line where I was required to stand. Tim grinned in amusement and moved behind me. After the bus arrived and we boarded, the official climbed aboard, bowed to the passengers, and announced in a high-pitched, singsong voice: ‘Yoroshiku onegaishimasu’ (We look forward to serving you), ‘O-ki o tsukete’ (Please take care), ‘Hassha itashimasu’ (The bus is now departing). She repeated this at every stop, making us feel like royalty. At home, by contrast, you usually rush on board while pushing anyone foolish enough to be taking their time; the bus invariably sets off before you have even sat down, often resulting in mild whiplash. It was a two-hour ride to Shinjuku Station, and we eventually found ourselves standing outside the Tokyo Dome Hotel. This was not our hotel, unfortunately, but a kindly concierge directed us to the Hotel Niwa Tokyo, a five-minute walk away. Navigating can be tricky in Japan as streets aren't named; buildings are numbered by block, so you must know the lay of the land as there are few other landmarks. We walked down narrow side streets and across busy roads, dragging our heavy luggage. Tim's suitcase had a short handle, making it difficult to keep upright. It was a chilly 10°C as we crossed the famous Shibuya intersection amidst hundreds of dark-suited commuters. Our room at the Hotel Niwa was small and warm, in a ryokan style with rattan walls and low lighting. It featured the ubiquitous singing toilet, a shower, and two beds. I phoned reception for some milk for my tea, and it arrived five minutes later. The little receptionist knocked gently and called out, ‘Sumimasen, rūmu sābisu desu,’ presented me with a small white jug on a silver tray, bowed, and left with a smile. 

Kyoto Bound 

By 6:30 a.m., we were dressed and ready to explore. Breakfast wasn't included, so we set off for Tokyo Station, breathing in the cold, crisp air. Most shops were still closed, but we found a Starbucks, thanks to a passing salaryman who noticed our lost expressions. Dark-suited commuters rushed past wearing white plastic masks; I discovered later they wear these both to protect themselves from others and to prevent the spread of their own germs, as sick leave is rarely taken in this country. Tim looked for a place to exchange our e-ticket vouchers, but the counters were closed. I suddenly felt lightheaded and exhausted—jet lag was preventing any rational thought. We found a McDonald’s, which was open, and climbed upstairs to a tiny room with small alcoves of tables and stools. We sat down gingerly—it was like sitting on a toadstool. I bought us each a grape juice, which was the artificially flavoured kind, but it did the trick. At the station, we changed our vouchers at the Japanicam booth. The woman explained how the tickets worked, but I hadn't a clue what she was saying. I turned to Tim, ‘Did you understand any of that?’ ‘Not a clue,’ he responded, and we both giggled. We found what was hopefully the track for the Shinkansen Nozomi. A long, round-fronted train arrived noiselessly and glided to a gentle stop. The doors opened, and a horde of people emerged—all strangely quiet given the numbers. Pink-uniformed ladies boarded with little pink cleaning boxes and gloves, and swiftly set about freshening the carriages. The crew—often called the ‘7-Minute Miracle’ team—performed a highly choreographed routine. When they finished, they performed a formal handover ceremony, starting with a deep, synchronised bow towards the passengers, announcing: ‘O-matase itashimashita’ (Thank you for waiting), followed by: ‘Seisou ga ryou shimashita, go-jousha kudasai’ (Cleaning is complete. Please board the train). We settled into comfortable padded seats, and the train set off gently. It soon reached its maximum speed of 320 km/h, yet the ride was so smooth that nothing spilled. We arrived at Kyoto Station two and a half hours later and walked towards ‘The Cube’, located at the apex of the station. Kyoto, which served as the capital from 794 until 1868, is famous for its Buddhist temples, gardens, palaces, and traditional houses. We looked around the immediate area, which entailed walking up a thousand steps with our heavy bags. The view at the top was worth it. We entered a restaurant, and a group of waiters sang out a welcome: ‘Irasshaimaseeeee!’ A waiter ushered us to a wooden table and presented a pot of smoked green tea. We ordered pork and cabbage; the waiter brought a pestle and mortar with roasted sesame seeds, indicating with gestures that we should grind them. We added soya sauce and poured the mixture over our pork. Tim shovelled mounds of rice and pork into his mouth with his chopsticks, whereas I struggled, managing only a grain or two at a time. In the end, I simply held the chopsticks in either hand and shovelled as best I could. It was delicious. We eventually found the New Miyako Hotel. Our room was compact, with a tea tray and a lovely iron teapot. I showered, dressed in the provided black-and-red kimono, and visited the vending machine in the hallway for a tin of cold green tea. We later searched for a room for the following night, but Kyoto was booked solid due to the Sakura (cherry blossom) season. We were lucky to eventually find a room at an inn—the ‘Inn Kyoto’, to be exact. 

Kyoto Exploration 

The following morning, we visited a nearby restaurant. I felt brave and chose a green steamed bun with a ‘mugwort’ flavour. I later learned that mugwort is Artemisia vulgaris, an herb used for its distinctive pale green colour and flavour, sweetened here with azuki (red bean paste). We visited the Yodabashi Camera store to find plug adapters and a new, larger suitcase. The store was massive, and the tiny attendant in her uniform and high heels guided us efficiently. The next morning, the gentle sound of rain awakened us. We had slept for nearly twelve hours. The suitcase was bulging; there must be a law of physics stating that the space available for one's belongings shrinks exponentially the moment one starts travelling. We took the hotel’s plastic umbrellas and headed to the Golden Pavilion (Kinkaku-ji), a Zen temple whose top two floors are completely covered in gold leaf. The surrounding Mirror Pond (Kyoko-chi) reflected the shimmering temple perfectly. That evening, we booked a cultural show in Gion. We watched beautiful kimono-clad dancers, a puppet show, and a comedy routine. There was a tea ceremony, which I found fascinating due to its delicate precision and meaningful gestures. 

Temples and Train Troubles 

The Higashi Honganji temple was a highlight, with its opulent gold-leaf decorations and ornate carvings. Walking the narrow streets, I noticed how many houses used kale cabbages as garden decorations. At Tokyo Station, we located the locker storing Tim’s old brown suitcase. We spent twenty minutes repacking his belongings into the new Kyoto suitcase, then wheeled everything to the giant railway timetable. We didn't actually need the schedule; our plan was to gaze at the board for a few minutes and then walk away, leaving the old brown suitcase behind. There was a small crowd nearby, so we managed to abandon the bag with ease and leave via the ticket gates. However, after walking only a few steps, we turned to each other: ‘We can't leave an empty suitcase at the station.’ ‘They might think there's a bomb in it!’ I said in horror. Back at the ticket office, I tried to explain to the official using gestures and a simple drawing of a suitcase. He looked puzzled, and if truth be told, a little afraid. ‘You want ticket?’ he asked slowly. I shook my head, desperately drawing stick figures to illustrate that we needed to go back to retrieve the bag. He seemed convinced I had escaped from a hospital. Thankfully, Tim remembered the word for suitcase: ‘Chū no sutsukēsu!’ He pointed towards the station. The official wiped his brow and nodded. Tim dashed inside and returned a few minutes later—minus the suitcase. ‘Phew, that was close,’ I muttered. ‘I think he was about to call the police.’ ‘Yeah, I looked, but it wasn't there—so someone has already taken it.’ We looked at each other and started laughing, imagining someone currently inspecting my grandmother's old, empty suitcase. We decided to walk a little faster. We took a train to Kanamachi, where Tim would be staying for his course. We couldn't find the hostel at first, but after phoning the facilitator, we were directed to a narrow brown building. A young man’s head appeared over the balcony—it was Bray, an American student. He showed us to Tim’s room: a small space with a tatami mat, a mattress, and a low table. The walls were made of traditional rice paper. After walking the neighbourhood and picking up some nibbles, I settled down to sleep. At two in the morning, my brain switched on, and I began frantically scrabbling for my phone to verify whether my flight home was at 3 a.m. or 3 p.m.! I was just dozing off when suddenly everything started to shake; the floor, the walls, and the windows shuddered, and items tumbled off the small table. I sat bolt upright, heart pounding, and shook Tim’s shoulder. ‘Tim, Tim, wake up!’ I cried. ‘Everything is shaking!’ ‘Mmmm, don't worry, it's just an earthquake,’ he murmured, before rolling over and going straight back to sleep. I decided to pull my trousers on, just in case we needed to evacuate. I spent the rest of the early morning tossing and turning. By seven, Tim bounded out of bed, washed, and headed down for his first lesson. I dressed and walked down to the first landing to use the toilet. It was a typical Japanese loo with all the modern extras, but I was intrigued to note that instead of a separate basin, there was a small sink built onto the top of the cistern so that the water first came through a tap to wash your hands before filling the tank. It is an excellent, efficient way to recycle water. I carried on down the narrow stairs to the ground floor, where I found Yuki. ‘Konnichiwa! Can you recommend a restaurant nearby for breakfast?’ I asked. She explained that there was a café called ‘Jonathan’s’ down the street and around the corner, so I trotted off and soon found myself eating a typical English breakfast. After my unadventurous meal, I took a stroll in the weak sunlight towards Mizumoto Park, walking alongside a canal and past fishermen casting their rods into the water. The park was huge and blissfully quiet at that hour. I walked for about an hour, crossing a small concrete bridge and passing rows upon rows of spring flowers. I came across some cherry blossoms and stood transfixed; they were breath-taking. It was still a little early for the full bloom, so only a few trees were showing their pink flowers, but I could only imagine how stunning the scene would be when the entire park was in blossom. A tea vendor had a small hut nearby, so I bought a green tea and sat on a bench, enjoying its unique, earthy flavour. 

Parting Ways and the Tsunami Headline 

Back in the room, I managed to squeeze my bulging suitcase closed and hugged my boy goodbye. I was sad to be leaving him, but he seemed genuinely excited; it was his first time travelling alone, and I knew it would be the first of many new adventures. He had written down the details of my train and the station where I needed to get off. I thanked Yuki and began walking to the station. I passed through the village of Takosago, which was truly pretty, and spent my last few Yen on a drink from a vending machine. At the station, I tried to find out which train went to Narita, but I received conflicting information and ended up boarding the wrong one. Once I realised my mistake, I jumped off and waited for the next train, which took me straight to Narita Airport. A copy of The Yomiuri newspaper was available for free at the terminal, and the front page screamed the headline: ‘Huge Tsunami and earthquake forecast for Tokyo!’ I was immediately worried about whether Tim would be okay. He later told me they experienced quite a few tremors and one particularly large earthquake. Tim completed his course with flying colours and taught a class of schoolchildren for a while before travelling across Japan to Fukuoka. There, he worked on a mushroom farm for a sweet, elderly Japanese couple who did not speak a word of English. Nevertheless, they became very fond of one another, and Tim learned how to make soba noodles from scratch, took onsen baths with the old couple, mowed the huge lawn, and mastered the art of cultivating wild mushrooms. By the time he eventually returned home from Japan, he could speak conversational Japanese, had grown into a tall, confident young man, and had gained immense poise in the art of solo travel.
