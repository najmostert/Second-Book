# Chapter 29: Japan 2012: The Journey Begins

Tim had completed high school and was taking a gap year. He would undertake a TEFL course in Japan to teach English. I was joining him because I’ve always wanted to visit Japan even if it’s only for a long weekend.

 Tim was annoyed to discover I’d shoved a packet of Pronutro (cereal) into his suitcase — he doesn’t understand my worry that he’ll starve while in Japan. I’m not sure he’ll like all the strange things they may have to eat for brekkie. News flash - needn't have worried, the food is delicious and he ditched the cereal.

Our tickets had been printed and suitcases pupated in cling-wrap before boarding the first of three planes. Hot cloths were handed out to wipe away the sweat and grime of waiting. We left late and I was hoping this wouldn’t affect the connecting flight as we only had two hours in Dubai before flying on to Japan. My foot had gone to sleep, not waiting for the rest of me to catch up, so I had to get up and stamp around a bit.

We were at opposite ends of the plane; Tim sandwiched between two tiny Japanese girls who were maybe four feet tall. I, on the other hand, had a huge gentleman wearing a voluminous white robe and a heavy pong of eau de ‘B.O’ A seat separated us so I removed my blue inflatable travel pillow and proceeded to blow it up. It took me ages and my cheeks were hurting. The pillow, now fully inflated, was placed behind my head and in less than a minute it was flatter than a pancake. I sighed then nodded off anyway just as they were doing their ‘chicken or beef’ song down the aisle. I wiped the dribble from my chin and sat up for supper (I think it was supper, but given the time difference it’s hard to tell). I sipped my plastic glass of red wine and slipped half a sleeping pill under my tongue. I’m not sure what happened after that as I must have been unconscious but I woke up a few hours later with my head leaning against the huge shoulder of my fellow passenger, dribbling in an unseemly manner. The proximity to his sweaty armpit must have woken me and I sat upright and returned my prone body to the aisle seat apologising profusely. The man smiled revealing broken teeth,

‘No problem, you can sleep again.’ He patted his shoulder invitingly and I gave an involuntary shudder,

‘Er, no thanks, I’m fine now.’

I quickly retrieved my tangled headphones from around my legs and placed them firmly on my head. It wasn’t long before I was snoring again and woke to find it was now breakfast time (supper time in South Africa) and found myself eating rubbery omelette with an accompanying bouncy soya sausage. The tea was very nice and refreshing though and I sipped it gratefully while trying to locate Tim. I turned round in my seat and found him several rows behind. He was sitting very still in the middle seat and the two Japanese girls’ heads were nodding towards each shoulder. He caught my glance and smiled.

## Tokyo Arrival

After collecting our luggage at Narita Airport, we stood in front of a passport control officer who took our fingerprints along with a photo. I’m really hoping I don’t look like the woman in the photo. She looks about a hundred.

The airport officials were very polite and helpful and we were shown where to go. As Tim wheeled his cling-wrapped pupa from the room, I discovered I’d left my cabin bag of clothes on the plane. Five minutes later one of the cabin crew was wheeling it towards me with a smile and a slight bow. I mumbled apologies and thanks. I entered the ladies’ and was soon sitting on the warmed-up plastic seat of an interesting toilet. These toilets are a known feature of Japanese culture but I didn’t know that then. A plastic arm to the left had various buttons bearing Japanese characters. I pressed one and there was a sudden whoosh of warm water spurting towards my backside. I rose quickly in alarm then fell back onto the warm seat with some trepidation and an accompanying frisson of excitement. Another button brought a musical rendition of Yankee Doodle to my nether regions and I hope they appreciated it. I depressed another button which instantly sent a loud flushing sound to permeate the air, presumably to drown out the unseemly sound of someone having a crap. This was all great except I couldn’t find the button to turn off the flushing, singing, whooshing melody that was now torturing my delicate behind. I eventually pressed all the buttons in quick succession and hopped off and everything stopped. Tim took his turn at the buttock-warming ceremony after buying us bus tickets to the hotel.

The Dome Hotel was five minutes away from us. I changed €100 into many yen notes and bought a coffee from one of the vendors, a sweet little girl wearing bobby socks, a short skirt and a black fringed bob. Everything is so clean here. I felt better after my coffee. Tim noted that
'Everyone is so polite and helpful here.' said Tim between sips of coffee, smiling as they gave little bows after each helpful bit of information.

A hurrying salaryman stopped long enough to show us the correct bus stop (accompanied by much miming and gesticulating on our part) and we went to stand somewhere near the bus signpost. This was not correct procedure. I was admonished gently by a small woman wearing a black uniform with white gloves and a whistle around her neck. She told me to stand behind the neatly painted white line by speaking in soft Japanese and pointing to the exact spot where I must stand. Tim grinned in amusement and moved behind me. After the bus arrived, the official ushered us aboard and we took a seat near the front. As the bus filled up, the female official climbed aboard, bowed facing the passengers and in a high-pitched, singsong voice called out,

‘Yoroshiku onegaishimasu’, (We look forward to serving you).

‘O-ki o tsukete’, Please take care (travel safely).

‘Hassha itashimasu’, (The bus is now departing).

She did this every time we set off from a stop and we felt like royalty.

At home, in strong contrast, you rush on board pushing anyone foolish enough to be taking their time, the bus will set off inevitably before you’ve sat down giving you mild whiplash. All of this, mind you, only if the bus service is not on strike.

It was a two-hour ride to Shinjuku Station and we found ourselves standing outside the Tokyo Dome Hotel — this was not our hotel unfortunately, but a kindly concierge directed us to the Hotel Niwa Tokyo, a five-minute walk away. Directions can be tricky in Japan as the streets are not named but rather the buildings themselves are numbered and this with each block, so you have to know the lay of the land as there’s nothing else to identify.

We walked down a few side streets and across busy roads dragging our heavy luggage. Tim’s suitcase only had a short handle so he was finding it very difficult to drag the case and keep it upright. It was a chilly 10°C as we walked over one arm of the famous Shibuya crossing with hundreds of dark-suited people then found the Hotel Niwa down another small side street. Our room was a small, warm ryokan style with rattan walls and low lighting. We had the ubiquitous singing toilet with warm seats, a shower and two beds. The bathroom had all manner of creams and gels, toothpaste and combs etc. I phoned reception for some milk for my tea and it arrived five minutes later. The little waitress knocked gently and called out,

‘Soo-mee-mah-sen, roo-mu sah-bee-su dess.’

Actually she said, ‘Sumimasen, rūmu sābisu desu,’ and presented me with a small white jug on a silver tray, bowed and left the room with a smile.

## Kyoto Bound

By 6:30 a.m. we were up and dressed, ready to explore. Breakfast was not included with the room so we set off for Tokyo Station breathing in the cold, crisp morning air and bought tickets to central Tokyo. We were both feeling good, invigorated by sleep and the fresh air and excited to see what Japan had in store. Most of the shops were still closed at this early hour but we found a Starbucks, thanks to a passing salaryman in his black suit and briefcase. He had noticed our lost expressions and asked what we needed. Some minutes later we were sipping a giant café latte. Tim ordered a cinnamon sausage roll and a white chocolate frappé which he declared delicious.

Dark-suited men and women rushed to work wearing white plastic masks. I found out later they wear the mask both to protect themselves from others as well as to prevent the spread of any flu bug they may be carrying. Sick leave is rarely taken in this country. Tim looked for a place to exchange our e-ticket vouchers but the counters were still closed. We wandered around looking into shop windows but I suddenly felt lightheaded and very tired. Tim said he was too. In fact jet lag was preventing any rational thought so we looked around for a place to eat. Another American icon appeared ahead. McDonald’s was open and we trooped upstairs. A small room held alcoves of tables and high stools around which several people sat hunched over their food. No one spoke but music blared in the background. Our tiny round table had small chairs so I sat down gingerly — it was like sitting on a toadstool. Tim’s legs splayed out from his mushroom. Japanese girls were occupied with cellphones, painting nails, rolling their hair and listening to their own music through earphones. We both heard the sound of cats and were wondering if this was an affliction brought on by feeling very tired in a strange country or actual strays- never did find out.

At the train station we changed our e-tickets at Japanicam booth. The woman behind the low counter explained how the tickets worked. I nodded my head but hadn’t a clue what she was saying and was hoping Tim did. We thanked her and left the cubicle wherein I turned quickly to Tim,

‘Did you understand any of that?’ I asked hopefully,

‘Not a clue,’ he responded and we both giggled.

The Japanese are quiet people and don’t talk to each other much in public, preferring to listen to music through earphones or their cellphones. There weren’t many westerners around (this being 2012 — it’s a different story now).

We found what was hopefully the right track for the bullet train or ‘Shinkansen Nozomi’. A long round-fronted train suddenly arrived noiselessly into the station and glided to a gentle stop. The doors opened and a horde of people emerged, still strangely quiet for the numbers that were emerging.

Several pink-uniformed ladies boarded the train with their little pink cleaning boxes and pink gloves and swiftly set about freshening the carriages with much bowing and nodding as they passed each other. The crew — often called the ‘7-Minute Miracle’ team — performed a highly choreographed routine. When they finished and were ready for you to board, they don’t just step aside; they perform a formal ‘handover’ ceremony starting with a deep, synchronised bow towards the passengers,

‘O-matase itashimashita,’ (Thank you for waiting), followed by,

‘Seisou ga ryou shimashita, go-jousha kudasai.’ (Cleaning is complete. Please board the train.)

Wide aisles and comfortable padded seats with folding trays awaited us so we settled in and set off gently. The window revealed a blur of countryside which was a pity really as I would have liked to see some of rural Japan. A tiny doll-like Japanese girl in a red checked uniform and pigtails arrived with the food trolley. I ordered a pack of sandwich fingers and a can of green tea. The train was now travelling at its maximum speed of 320 km/h but was so smooth, nothing spilled.

What we could see of the view changed from built-up high-rises against a grey backdrop to a more open area and patchy blue sky. The trip took two and a half hours with a few stops in between. We arrived at Kyoto Station and walked towards the Cube, located at the apex of the huge train station.

Kyoto served as Japan’s capital and the emperor’s residence from 794 until 1868. A quick Google search stated that it’s famous for the many classical Buddhist temples, gardens, imperial palaces, Shinto shrines and traditional wooden houses. It’s also known for formal traditions such as kaiseki dining, consisting of multiple courses and geisha, female entertainers in the nearby Gion district. We decided to look around the immediate area. This entailed walking up a thousand steps with our heavy backpacks and suitcases. The view at the top was worth it though — the city of Kyoto was spread out below. A few small families and teens sat chatting on the stairs or fiddling with their cellphones. Tim and I entered a restaurant at the top of the Cube and immediately a group of waiters looked up from whatever they were doing and sang out a quick welcome in Japanese,

‘Irasshaimase’, (sounds like, Irasshaimaseeeee! as the word is drawn out).

A waitron ushered us to a small wooden table near the window and presented me with a pot of smoked green tea almost immediately. Tim and I ordered the pork and cabbage meal. The waiter brought us a small white pestle and mortar with a glass container of roasted sesame seeds. He indicated by way of a few concise gestures what we were to do with it. Tim took his pestle and poured the sesame seeds into a small bowl. He set about grinding the seeds with enthusiasm and I followed suit. We added a little soya sauce to the mixture from a small jug and ladle on the table.

‘You have to pour this onto your pork,’ said Tim knowledgeably as though he’d done this numerous times.

 Tim picked up his chopsticks and began shovelling mounds of rice, pork and cabbage smoothly into his mouth. I, on the other hand, picked up one ‘riceicle’ at a time and managed to add fragrant sauce to my clothing rather than my mouth. Tim showed me how to hold my chopsticks but I succeeded only in grabbing two grains a try. In the end I found holding the chopsticks separately in either hand and lifting as much as I could into my mouth while hoverering over the plate like a Hoover, I was able to eat at a faster pace. It was delicious. Likewise the smoky flavoured green tea which is drunk black (or should that be, green). The waiter appeared at regular intervals to smile and bow in our direction. I smiled my appreciation through mouthfuls of rice and pork with a few, ‘Arigatou’s’. We ended our first authentic Japanese cuisine with coffee and thus sated headed to the hotel.

Tim retraced our steps and we ended up on a side street not far from the Cube where the New Miyako Hotel is situated. I left him to sort out the booking of the room (his knowledge of Japanese being 100% more than my repertoire of ‘Hai’-yes and ‘Arigatou’- thank you).

A small lift took us up to our small, compact room with a tea tray for the makings of green tea — including a lovely iron teapot. I showered and dressed in a black kimono gown and padded over to a vending machine down the hallway (always one nearby no matter where you are in Japan).  Later we took a walk to another hotel. I got as far as handing over my credit card and signing for our room when the concierge bowed and said,

‘Wercome to hoterr.’ With a little bow.

I realised I'd just booked for tonight not tomorrow. After explaining this in short English sentences he reversed the process but added that he did not have a room for tomorrow. We set off again and the next two hotels were also booked up. Despondently we continued our search. The reason for the booked rooms was that sakura, cherry blossom season had started and many tourists were in Kyoto. We were lucky to eventually find a room at the inn — the Inn Kyoto to be exact.

## Kyoto Exploration

Menu offerings were on display through a narrow glass counter at a nearby restaurant. Tim chose a milkshake  and I had coffee and feeling brave, I chose a green steam bun with a ‘mugwort’ flavour, I found the name fascinating and bit into it tentatively. It had an interesting flavour which I couldn’t define. I later found out that the mugwort is the common name for *Artemisia vulgaris*, an herb commonly used to flavour food in Japan. Sweetened with azuki or red bean paste, my bun was also flavoured with green tea which gave it its distinctive pale green colour. The bun was moist and fairly tasty. Tim and I walked down to the shops at the back of the Cube. We looked for a shop that sold computer stuff and plug adapters. The Yodabashi Camera store is huge; many storeys high and filled with all manner of electrical equipment. A store attendant in a red and white uniform, long socks and high-heeled shoes showed us where to locate the adapters. 

## Golden Pavilion and Tea Ceremony

The gentle sound of rain wakened us the following morning. I padded over to the window in my flowery kimono robe and peered outside. There were puddles everywhere and the city wore a shiny grey sheen of rain. I glanced at my watch, it was 10 a.m. — we had slept for nearly twelve hours. I had a bit of a headache but it was time to move on, so we dressed and packed — shoving everything into any available space in the new suitcase we'd bought. There must be some law about the amount of space being exponentially smaller according to the amount of accumulated stuff when travelling! Or something like that because the suitcase was almost bulging.

It was still drizzling when we left the hotel so they gave us two plastic umbrellas. Tim located the new hotel and we left most of the cases at reception and headed off for a bite to eat. Café Mozart was busy and quite smoky so as we were about to turn and leave, a waitress approached with a small bow and a smile and led us to a non-smoking table — right next to three ‘smoking’ tables thereby rendering the sign redundant.

The Japanese show what’s on their menu by means of realistic plates of ‘sampuru’ (samples) which are displayed in windows.

Before colour photography and laminated menus were common, restaurants needed a way to show patrons exactly what they would be getting. In the early 1920s, artisans created models out of paraffin wax which melted under the hot sun in shop windows. In the 1970s, the industry switched to polyvinyl chloride (PVC), which is durable, heat-resistant, and holds paint much better, allowing for the hyper-realistic detail of present day Japan. Thus a typically Japanese fare of noodles and pork is displayed in full colour in a bowl with chopsticks hanging over them in suspended animation.

I later learned that in Japan, regulations require that images on food packaging and sampuru must be a highly accurate representation of the actual product. The goal is to prevent consumer deception and build trust. I love this about the Japanese.

Our day passes took us to the Golden Pavilion which is a Zen palace in northern Kyoto whose top two floors are completely covered in gold leaf. Formally known as Rokuonji, the temple was the retirement villa of the shogun Ashikaga Yoshimitsu and according to his will it became a Zen temple after his death in 1408. Each floor of this beautiful structure represents a different style of architecture. Tim and I gazed at it in awe and the surrounding Mirror Pond (Kyoko-chi) that reflects the shimmering temple in the still water. The rest of the grounds were full of manicured lawns and little waterfalls and Zen structures. I popped in to a teahouse offering a bowl of matcha tea and a mochi. I left my shoes outside and was ushered to a space at a small table. At one of the small stalls near the gate, I succumbed to a tasting of rose petal and green tea chocolate which is as delicious as it sounds.

A traditional wooden café down a side street was close to the geisha area. I was excited to find two geisha girls exiting a small wooden-framed door and climbing into a limousine. I’ve heard they are usually shy of being seen.

Our brazier was lit and a small tray arrived with raw rice cake slices which we placed on the brazier tray to cook. Another silver tray arrived with small dollops of sauces; wasabi, ginger, soy and a vegetable root. It was an interesting meal but not very filling so we ordered a cake each. Mine was fig, date and walnut with fig ice cream. That evening we booked a cultural show in the Gion area of Kyoto and we watched some beautiful kimono-clad dancers, a puppet show, a short comedy routine (we laughed despite it being in Japanese because it was so over the top and the actor playing a ‘drunk’ man was very good). There was a flower arranging section and lastly a tea ceremony which I found fascinating with its delicate precision and meaningful gestures.

## Temples and Train Troubles

The Higashi Honganji temple is one of the popular centres of Buddhism in Kyoto. It has a huge green dragon water feature. We copied other tourists and took the long-handled copper ladle to scoop up some water to drink. Others we saw poured the water over themselves. The most common religions in Japan are Shinto and Buddhism. Many Japanese people practise both, seeing them as complementary rather than conflicting. Shinto is the indigenous faith of Japan, focusing on nature and spirits (kami), while Buddhism is concerned with the soul and the afterlife. Inside, the temple was sparsely furnished with wooden or rush matting floors and opulent hanging lights in gold leaf. The altar area was also full of gold artefacts and lights. The Karamon gate, Nishi Hongan-ji temple is much smaller. It has the oldest Noh stage in Japan but I was transfixed by the opulent and ornate beauty of the huge dark wooden gate, beautifully carved and set with gold and blue.

We walked along clean, narrow streets past tall, narrow houses with tiny, neat garden patches. Many gardens had kale cabbages as decoration. Most people use bicycles in the rural areas and there’s a bicycle lane on all the roads.

As we entered Starbucks, all waitrons looked up and said,

‘Irasshaimase!’ as a welcome and the pretty Japanese waitresses sang, ‘Arrigaaatogazaimasuuu!’ (thank you) as you collected your coffee.

At Tokyo Station, we located the locker that had been storing Tim’s old brown suitcase and spent twenty minutes repacking his stuff from the old into the new one we bought in Kyoto before wheeling it with all the rest of the luggage to the huge railway timetable. We weren’t actually interested in the train schedule; our plan was to gaze at the board for a few minutes then walk away and leave the old brown suitcase behind. There was a small crowd of people gazing up at the board so we managed to walk away with ease and leave the station via the ticket gates.

Without walking more than a few steps we both turned to each other and said the same thing,

‘We can’t leave an empty suitcase at the station.’

‘They might think there’s a bomb in it,’ I said in horror.

Back at the ticket office window,

‘Konnichiwa. We need go back station to collect bag', I tried to explain to the official using numerous gestures and mime, while looking up at a non-existent board and using my fingers to show us ‘walking away’. He remained puzzled and if truth be known, a little afraid.

‘You want ticket?’ he asked slowly.

I shook my head and grabbed a piece of paper out of my bag and drew stick figures and a very simple diagram of a suitcase to illustrate what we did and hopefully what we need to do now. The official was convinced I  required urgent medication; and backed away from the window. Thankfully Tim had meanwhile been racking his brain trying to remember the word for suitcase. He exclaimed behind me and came forward,

‘Chū no sutsukēsu!’ He pointed towards the station.

‘Aaah, so so so,’ replied the official, wiping his brow hurriedly and he nodded.

Tim dashed inside and a few minutes later he exited minus a suitcase and dashed past the perplexed official.

‘Phew, that was close,’ I muttered, ‘I think he was about to call the police on me.’

‘Yeah, I looked but it wasn’t there so someone has already taken it.’

We looked at each other and started laughing imagining my gran’s old suitcase was right now being surrounded by men with guns. Nevertheless, we walked a little faster.

With a shared desire to visit the Emperor’s Garden that day we took a train to Kanamachi. Tim would be staying here for the TEFL course.

The taxi driver deposited us outside a narrow brown building with a brass nameplate on the gate. Just as we were wondering if we had the right place, a young man’s head appeared over the balcony and enquired if this was Tim. A few minutes later he was opening the door and said his name was Bray. I tried not to think of a donkey. He was one of the American TEFL students and said he'd arrived yesterday and that Larry and his wife were out right now but he’d show us to Tim’s room. He was surprised to learn that Tim was only nineteen and said he thought he was older. He was explaining that shoes are left at the door and slippers are available for use indoors, when Larry and his wife appeared. Larry is a tall dark haired American and his wife Yuki a petite Japanese. I explained my presence and said I was just dropping Tim off before looking for a nearby hotel. Larry immediately suggested I stay in Tim’s room for the night and led us up some narrow wooden stairs to a room which was small and sparsely furnished. A  tatami mat rested on the floor next to a mattress and a low brown table. The walls were made of traditional rice paper and the windows had wooden shutters. A wooden built-in cupboard sat against one wall which Tim was filling with clothes. We decided to walk around the area a bit as it was to be Tim’s home for a few months. There were small, tightly positioned houses, narrow streets, window boxes and flower pots bursting with colour. As the sky darkened, we popped in for some nibbles from one of the popular supermarkets before making our way back to the house to settle down to sleep. I switched off the light and crawled onto my mat. My brain remained switched on and at two in the morning, I was scrabbling for my phone to see if my flight home was three a.m. or three p.m.

I was just dozing off when suddenly everything started to shake; the floor, the walls and windows and items fell off the small table. I sat up, heart pounding and woke Tim up,

‘Tim, Tim, wake up!’ I shook his shoulder, ‘Everything is shaking!’

‘Mmmm, don’t worry, it’s just an earthquake.’ he mumbled
and rolled over and went back to sleep. I decided to dress in case we had to evacuate and spent the rest of the early morning tossing and turning. At seven, Tim bounded out of bed, washed and went down for his first lesson. I walked down to the first landing to use the toilet. It was a typical Japanese loo with all the extras but I was intrigued to note that instead of a separate basin, a small hand basin had been built onto the top of the toilet so that the water first comes through a tap to wash hands before filling the cistern. Excellent way to save water and recycle. I carried on down the narrow stairs to the bottom where I found Yuki.

‘Konichiwa! Can you recommend a restaurant nearby for breakfast?’ I asked.

She said there was a café called ‘Jonathan’s’ down the street so I trotted off and soon found myself eating a typical English breakfast. After my unadventurous meal I took a stroll in the weak sunlight to Mizumoto Park,  walking alongside the canal past fishermen casting their rods into the canal water. The park is huge and seemed quiet at that time of the day. I walked for about an hour across a small co before coming across  cherry trees and stood transfixed. They are so beautiful. It was still too early for the full bloom so only a few trees had pink flowers but I can only imagine how pretty it is when they're blossoming. A  vendor sold green tea from a small hut nearby. I sat on a bench enjoying its unique flavour.

Back in the room, I squeezed my bulging suitcase closed and hugged Tim goodbye. I was sad to be leaving him but he seemed excited; it was his first time travelling alone and was the first of many new adventures. He’d written down the details of my train and where to get off. I came to the pretty village of Takosago and spent my last few yen on a drink from a street vending machine. At the station I tried to find out which train travelled to Narita but received conflicting information and ended up on the wrong train. I jumped off at the next station and waited for the next train which took me straight to Narita Airport. A copy of *The Yomiuri* newspaper was available for free and the front page screamed,

‘Huge Tsunami and earthquake forecast for Tokyo!’

I was immediately anxious that Tim would be okay. He later told me they experienced quite a few tremors and one big earthquake.

He completed his course with flying colours and taught a few school children for a while before travelling across Japan to Fukuoka where he worked on a mushroom farm for a sweet old Japanese couple who didn’t speak  English. Nevertheless they became very fond of each other and Tim learned how to make soba noodles from scratch, take onsen baths with the old couple, mow the huge lawn and cultivated wild mushrooms. When he eventually returned home from Japan, he could speak conversational Japanese, and had grown into a tall, confident young man.
