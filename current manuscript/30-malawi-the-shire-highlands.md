# Chapter 27: Malawi: The Shire Highlands

We arrived in Lilongwe, the capital of Malawi, at 4:30 p.m. and found Col’s contact, John, had sent his friend to fetch us. An hour and a half in the car and we arrived at Zomba Plateau; a mountain of the Shire Highlands in southern Malawi. The driver turned into the long, steep driveway to John and Fumiyo’s place (she is his Japanese wife). A huge, rambling stone house sat at the top as if in wait, built in a garden that was wild and untamed. John greeted us, an affable, white-haired man of some sixty-five years or so. His slender wife, Fumiyo, smiled warmly in greeting. John told us that this was the oldest house in Malawi, built in 1879. He led us outside to a large balcony where the forest grew wild and a stream meandered at the bottom of the garden. Although humid, a small breeze wafted over our sweaty faces at intervals. Fumiyo showed us to our room, then offered strong Malawian tea and homemade tart. Afterwards, we followed John down the dusty road while he pointed out various birds and butterflies. He is an avid butterfly collector, so he and Col chatted away about swallowtail and nymphalid varieties that are endemic to Malawi, while I photographed the surroundings. The air was filled with Latin names and birdsong in equal measure.

John said he used to live in Kenya and travelled many times to Uganda. I was amazed to hear that he also knew Temple Perkins, the author of *The Kingdom of the Elephant*, and Dennis Burkitt, both of whom were friends of my parents when I lived in Uganda as a child.

I woke at 4:30 and heard John and Fumiyo going for their walk. They did warn us that they wake early when it’s cool. I turned over and slept fitfully until around 6:30.

Col and I took a short walk in the forest redolent with birdsong.

## Arrival at Namizimu

Namizimu, in Mangochi, lies on the eastern side of the mountain. It was a two-hour drive, so we stopped for petrol. Luckily we got some — very often the tanks are dry around here. After crossing the Shire River, which flows into Mozambique and is the largest river in Malawi, John stopped to buy some mangoes from a roadside stall. There were five stalls, all selling the same thing, with rows and rows of mangoes in various stages of ripeness, in colours ranging from green, yellow, and orange to purple.

We arrived at Namizimu just after midday after driving the last ten kilometres up a long, winding road through the forest. A petite, dark, curly-haired Iranian girl greeted us with a warm smile and said she was Tara. She was bubbly and welcoming and I liked her immediately. She introduced us to her partner, Dave; a tall, thin man with a long, dark ponytail that was greying in parts. Tara bounced away and returned with glasses of freshly squeezed lemon water and melon slices, which were delightfully cool on the palate. As we sipped our drinks, I turned to look down towards Lake Malawi, which was a glittering silver line below us.

After a quick wash, we met everyone at the large wooden table situated in the middle of a huge verandah and lunched on toast with garlic-flavoured hummus, tomato, avo and cucumber. The company was very pleasant, and we were soon chatting away like old friends. I made a cup of tea from the small tray outside the kitchen and chatted to Tara, who told me she met Dave ten years before, and they decided to run the house which belonged to his parents, as a B&B. John regaled us with his humorous tales of adventure. He looked a bit like the late Ian Fleming, I decided. He told us of an old University of Cambridge friend who dabbled with something illegal using fraudulent cheques. He was imprisoned, then faked a broken arm. He escaped from hospital and was on the run for years but was eventually caught when he tried to buy a house with a million dollars in cash. I jokingly said it sounded like a good script for a movie. John smiled and said it was made into a movie called *Julia*.

Another story was about the man who lived in the house we were staying at, called Derek something, who died and bequeathed the entire area to John. According to John, Derek’s mother disliked him but came to stay with him in the house. She ended up dying there. Her wish was to be cremated. John told us there’s only one place where you can be cremated in Lilongwe, so Derek rolled his mother in an old curtain, put her in the back of his car and set off looking for this place. En route, he took a break to visit some old friends and they invited him in for lunch. An hour later, he casually mentioned that his mum was still in the car. Startled, they insisted he bring her in. He explained that may not be wise, as she was deceased. He left his friends and continued his search for the cremator. It was getting late and he had driven another 100 kilometres but still couldn’t find the place, so he stopped to see other friends to ask their advice. Their ‘houseboy’ informed them that they were away for the weekend. He didn’t want to drive all the way back with his mum still in the back of his car, so he decided to leave her in his friends’ garage with a note explaining the situation. He asked if they would do him a huge favour and contact the Indian who does cremations. It is not known what their reaction was when they returned from their weekend away to discover their friend’s mother wrapped up in a curtain in the garage, but the story goes that they were not well pleased. We laughed uproariously at John’s quiet, measured way of popping out one incredible story after another.

I followed Col and John down the road for a few hours. Their guide was carrying the important stuff — like lunch. I returned to the house glowing with sweat and heat and Tara handed me a jug of lemon water, which I gulped down gratefully. I sat at the big wooden table and chatted to her. Lucy, her sister-in-law, called me to her bedroom to show me a butterfly that had settled in the shower.

‘That’s an owl butterfly,’ I told them confidently, secretly vowing to confirm with Col later.

## Mother-in-Law Stories

I sipped my brew gratefully and gazed over the sunrise-streaked mountains. The latest arrivals joined us for breakfast, and the talk was lively. John retold the mother-in-law story, which prompted Lucy to tell her MIL story. Not to be left out, Col and I told them of the time I inadvertently put my MIL up in a house of ill repute. I’d answered a newspaper advert and booked a room for Anne-Sofie and Sven-Erik. When visiting the place a day before they arrived, we discovered its true nature and that most of their guests paid by the hour. It was too late to find another place so for two nights they slept in a room with a thick pink shaggy-pile carpet and mirrors on the ceiling. The bathroom was carpeted everywhere and two showers sat at each corner.

Col and John left for a walk up to the plateau and I went with Tara and the nurses to the lapa area. This is where activities like dancing, pottery, and sewing take place, Tara told us. It was a large, round clay-floored area with a thatch roof and no walls, held up by four sturdy wooden beams. The dirt floor had been hardened over time to a light colour. There were three old local women waiting for us. They were sitting cross-ankled on the floor with an array of clay pots around them. One woman had short grey hair and one tooth. She beamed at us happily and gestured for us to sit then showed us how to pound the clay to remove impurities, then how to form the pots, working from the inside out and smoothing the clay dextrously with worn, arthritic fingers using a piece of bamboo and a smooth stone. It was fascinating to watch. Col sat at the table and set the two *aliciae* he caught earlier, watched by an interested audience. He folded paper into a small envelope, then placed the butterfly carefully between the paper envelope before placing it into a small, plastic-lidded container with a few mothballs to preserve them. On our return, Tara excitedly announced that her sister would be arriving soon from Cape Town. We settled down at the table with a cup of tea when suddenly Tara let out an excited squeal. From the taxi that had just pulled in, a replica of Tara emerged. She was a little taller than her big sister; otherwise, they were very similar. Wafa (for that is her name) told me she lived in Observatory. We had a lively supper with lots of chatter and stories while eating roast duck and vegetables.

Col and John decided to go for a walk despite the rain and left with the bearer and chief butterfly spotter. Col looked like an intrepid explorer from another time with his big hat, many-pocketed khaki jacket, and butterfly pole.

## Monday 5th: Farewells and a New Destination

It was our last morning here, and I was sad. Tara came to the door with my tea, and she was quite tearful. She said she would miss us and gave me a heart she had made from clay. She said I was like family to her, and I was so touched. We had a long hug, my own eyes tearing up. Tara begged us to stay for lunch, so we had tomato soup. I dropped several spoonfuls of olive oil and garlic on my bread and as a result, smelled like an Italian labourer.

We packed the car, before the sad farewells. Tara pressed a note into my hand, and we waved a tearful goodbye and set off down the windy mountain road. I opened Tara’s note, and inside she had written, ‘You are a lovely person’. I wiped away a tear and marvelled at how quickly one can make friends that feel like you have known them forever. This is, of course, one of the joys of travel.

I looked out for our cabin at Mkudze Bay. Parsons Cottage had a lovely green garden with hardy kikuyu grass and a couple of frangipani trees. I was fumbling around the big kitchen when Rollon (pronounced Roll-on) appeared in the doorway and announced that he was to be our cook. He comes with the cottage, apparently. I asked him for a pot of strong Malawian tea, which he brought to us on the wide verandah. Later, Col and I went for a walk to investigate the area and its possible opportunities for birding. There were quite a few red-headed weaver nests strung across the telephone wires and we heard the call of the trumpeter hornbill. Later, Rollon brought us rice and tinned pilchards for supper.

## Tuesday 6th December: Rock Art and a Tough Steak

Rollon brought us tea at 4:30 before heading to the Chigoni rock hike. The plateau where the rock art is located is about 80 km south-east of Lilongwe, near the town of Dedza. After leaving the guard in charge, we began the hike. It was gradual at first, and the air was still cool. It soon became harder to climb, and despite trying to speak normally, I sounded like an overheated steam train. John was amazingly fit despite his advancing years. We found the ‘Deus’ rock, which ‘points’ the way to the rock paintings above a short, steep, rocky ridge. Eventually we arrived at the huge, overhanging boulder behind which were some rock paintings drawn by the pygmy Batwa tribe some 4,000 years ago. The paintings were orangey-red straight lines drawn horizontally and vertically and apparently represent fertility, water, and food. We gazed at them for a while, each of us in our own world, imagining very different times. The climb down went a lot faster and soon we were reunited with the car and the guard. Back at the cottage, I discovered Rollon had bought some fish for us, chambo and kampango, which he set to pan-frying while I made a plate of toast. The three of us ate at the large table. Rollon had also made some grilled mango, which accompanied the fish beautifully. The lads were going to set some butterfly traps in the forest, and I was just going to chill with my book.

## Wednesday 7th December: Mvuu Lodge

That morning, a crimson sky greeted us as we sat with our mugs of tea outside, admiring the view. It was hotter than the day before already. Col collected some butterflies in his net. There were so many, they practically flew straight into it. Rollon cooked us the last of the chambo, which we had with toast, mango, and strong tea. Col fussed over the cold box, which would keep the butterflies intact before setting off for Mulanje. Following some palaver over the park fees John said we had been upgraded from the tented camp to a lodge.

We’d packed all our clothes into one bag because we had to travel by boat after a little walk. A beaming park official greeted us and took us to his small, covered boat. We sailed up the Shire River, stopping briefly to watch a small herd of elephant munching grass on the river edges. A baby elephant peered at us from behind a small bush. At the Mvuu side of the river, a couple of waiters assisted us with our luggage up to the lounge area that overlooked the river before handing out cold cloths to wipe our sweaty faces and a glass of cold orange juice. Both were very welcome. We met the host, and John introduced us. He seemed to know everyone in Malawi. An indemnity form was duly signed absolving the park from liability should we find ourselves attacked by a lion or swallowed by a hippo.

Our room was a delightful luxury tent overlooking the river with an outside bath and shower. At lunch we met Samuel, our guide. He promised us he would take us to find Pel’s fishing owl and Böhm’s bee-eater, among other things, so Col was very happy and set about cleaning his camera lenses in preparation. Later we were seated on the lodge’s open Land Rover, joined by a trainee guide, Etienne from Switzerland. The grass almost looked as though it was newly mown, as it was short and a bright green colour — this was thanks to the grazers; kudu, roan antelope, sable antelope, and warthogs. As it became darker, the driver switched on the infrared light, and lit up a sweet little family of warthog ‘piggies’ and, a bit later, some springbok. I was bitten on the arm by a tsetse fly, and it swelled up. A midge flew into Col’s eye, so as a wounded pair, we bid goodnight and headed for our tented room.

## Owls and a River Trip

A lion roared nearby around 4 a.m. and after meeting the others for a quick cup of tea, we set off for a morning game drive. Later joining John, Etienne and an armed guide for a walk through the bushveld. We returned to the boat, with Samuel pointing out birds as we walked. At the other side of the river, we found John’s car and drove to Lilongwe and looked out for a petrol station, but no one seemed to have petrol which was worrying. Despite the lack of fuel, we arrived at John’s place by 4:30 p.m. and Fumiyo was there to welcome us back with tea and mince pies. Fumiyo gave us a basket of goodies to take with us to our next place — a cottage high up on the Zomba Plateau. I hugged her goodbye, and we set off in John’s car. The ‘cottage’ was really an old-style house with a wraparound, red, cement-polished verandah. There were five bedrooms and a large kitchen. We chose our room and greeted a hovering caretaker. He told us he was Dayton and that he would cook for us, so I unpacked quickly and handed over a cardboard box of various foodstuffs. We bid John a fond farewell and sat on the verandah where Col set some butterflies. Dayton brought us a meal of soup and corn on the cob which was like eating baby’s teeth. The village was too far to walk to for extra airtime so I borrowed Dayton’s cellphone for a couple of hundred kwachas. I realised I’d been calling him Titon but having received the money and a huge tip he seemed happy to answer to anything. I sent Tim an SMS and asked him to let me know when he was safely home then joined Col for a walk into the forest. As we walked down the plateau, it became warmer and more humid. A narrow path led into the gully behind the dam wall where Col set his traps. I watched locals walk by with several trees’ worth of kindling and branches on their heads — I doubt there’ll be a forest left when we return to Malawi. We had a last walk through the forest that morning, looking for birds to photograph. The taxi arrived to take us to the airport so we bid a flurry of hasty farewells to John and Fumiyo.
