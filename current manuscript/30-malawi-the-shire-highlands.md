# Chapter 27: Malawi: The Shire Highlands

The next year, our travel itinerary took a tropical turn as we embarked on a trip to Malawi. We arrived in Lilongwe, the capital city, at 4:30 p.m. and discovered that Col’s local contact, John, had kindly sent his driver to fetch us from the terminal. After an hour and a half of driving through the changing landscape, we finally arrived at the Zomba Plateau, a magnificent mountain reach rising out of the Shire Highlands in southern Malawi. The driver turned into a long, steep driveway leading towards John and Fumiyo’s property (she is his Japanese wife). A huge, rambling stone house sat at the summit as if waiting for our arrival, uniquely built within a garden that was completely wild and untamed. John greeted us warmly—an affable, white-haired man of some sixty-five years or so. His slender wife, Fumiyo, smiled gently in greeting. John proudly informed us that this was officially the oldest standing house in Malawi, having been built back in 1879. He led us outside onto a large stone balcony where the forest grew wild and a stream meandered peacefully at the bottom of the garden. Although it was intensely humid, a welcome breeze wafted over our sweaty faces at regular intervals. Fumiyo showed us to our room, then offered us cups of strong Malawian tea and a slice of homemade tart. Afterwards, we followed John down the dusty road while he pointed out various local birds and butterflies. He is an avid butterfly collector, so he and Col immediately chatted away about the various swallowtail (Papilionidae) and nymphalid varieties that are entirely endemic to Malawi, while I focused on photographing our surroundings. The afternoon air was filled with Latin taxonomic names and birdsong in equal measure. John mentioned casually that he used to live in Kenya and had travelled many times to Uganda during his youth. I was absolutely amazed to hear that he had personally known Temple Perkins, the famous author of The Kingdom of the Elephant, and Dennis Burkitt —both of whom had been close friends of my own parents when I lived in Uganda as a young child! What an incredibly small world. I woke the next morning at 4:30 a.m. and heard John and Fumiyo quietly departing for their morning walk. They had warned us the night before that they always wake exceptionally early while the mountain air is still cool. I turned over under the sheets and slept fitfully until around 6:30 a.m., after which Col and I took a short walk into the forest, which was redolent with early morning birdsong. 

Arrival at Namizimu 

Our next destination was Namizimu. The property, located in Mangochi, lies on the eastern side of the mountain. It was a two-hour drive from the plateau, so we pulled over at a station for petrol. Luckily, the pumps had fuel—very often the tanks run completely dry around these parts. After crossing the Shire River— which flows directly into Mozambique and represents the largest river system in Malawi—John stopped the car to buy some fresh mangoes from a roadside stall. There were five identical stalls lined up, all selling the exact same produce, with rows upon rows of mangoes displayed in various stages of ripeness, boasting vibrant colours ranging from green, yellow, and orange to deep purple. We arrived at Namizimu just after midday, after driving the final ten kilometres up a long, winding dirt road through the dense forest canopy. A petite, dark, curly-haired Iranian girl greeted our vehicle with a warm smile and introduced herself as Tara. She was incredibly bubbly and welcoming, and I took an instant liking to her. She introduced us to her partner, Dave—a tall, thin man sporting a long, dark ponytail that was turning grey in parts. Tara bounced away into the kitchen and returned carrying glasses of freshly squeezed lemon water and cold melon slices, which were delightfully cool on the palate. As we sipped our drinks, I turned to look down the escarpment towards Lake Malawi, which appeared as a glittering silver line far below our vantage point. After a quick wash, we met the other guests at a large wooden table situated in the middle of a huge verandah, and lunched on toast served with garlic-flavoured hummus, fresh tomatoes, avocado, and cucumber. The company was highly pleasant, and we were soon chatting away like old friends. I brewed a cup of tea from the small tray outside the kitchen and sat down to chat with Tara. She told me she had met Dave ten years previously, and they had decided to run the historic estate—which had originally belonged to his parents—as a bed and breakfast. 

John's Tales and the Curtain Cremation 

John regularly regaled the verandah with his humorous tales of African adventure. I decided he looked a bit like the late Ian Fleming as he spoke. He told us a wild story about an old University of Cambridge friend who had dabbled in illegal financial activities using fraudulent cheques. The man was imprisoned, faked a broken arm to get transferred, and successfully escaped from the hospital. He remained on the run for years as an international fugitive, but was eventually caught by the authorities when he foolishly attempted to buy a house using a million dollars in cold cash. I jokingly told John it sounded like a script for a Hollywood movie, and he smiled, informing me it had actually been turned into a film called Julia. Another extraordinary story involved the previous eccentric owner of the house we were currently staying in—a man named Derek. When Derek died, he bequeathed the entire forest area to John. According to John, Derek’s mother thoroughly disliked her son but had come to stay with him in the house anyway, eventually passing away right on the property. Her final wish was to be cremated. Derek was informed there was only one proper crematorium available in Lilongwe, so he casually rolled his deceased mother up inside an old window curtain, bundled her into the back of his car, and set off across the country looking for the facility. En route, he decided to take a break to visit some old friends, who invited him inside for lunch. An hour into the meal, he casually mentioned to the table that his mother was sitting outside in the car. Startled, his hosts insisted he bring her inside out of the heat immediately. Derek explained that this might not be wise, as she was deceased. He politely left his friends and continued his search for the crematorium. By the time evening approached, he had driven another 100 kilometres but still could not locate the facility, so he stopped at another friend's house to ask for advice. The local houseman informed him that the owners were away for the weekend. Not wanting to drive all the way back up the mountain pass with his mother still decomposing in the back seat, Derek decided to leave her body wrapped in the curtain inside his friends' garage, leaving a note explaining the situation and asking if they would do him a huge favour and contact the local Indian gentleman who handled the cremations. It is not known exactly what the couple's reactions were when they returned from their pleasant weekend away to discover their friend's mother wrapped up in a curtain in their garage, but the story goes that they were by no means well pleased! We laughed uproariously at the tale, thoroughly enjoying John's quiet, measured way of popping out one incredible anecdote after another. Later that afternoon, I followed Col and John down the dirt road for a few hours. Their local tracker was carrying the truly important safari gear—namely, our lunch. I eventually returned to the main house glowing with sweat and heat, and Tara handed me a jug of lemon water, which I gulped down gratefully. I sat at the big wooden table and chatted with her and Lucy, her sister-in-law. Lucy called me over to her bedroom to inspect a massive butterfly that had settled inside the shower stall. ‘That’s an owl butterfly,’ I announced confidently to the room, whilst secretly vowing to confirm the taxonomy with Col the moment he returned from the forest. 

The Brothel Booking and Clay Pots 

The next morning, I sipped my brew gratefully and gazed out over the mountains, which were beautifully streaked by the sunrise. The latest guests joined our table for breakfast, and the conversation was lively. John retold the mother-in-law cremation story, which promptly prompted Lucy to share her own mother-in-law tale. Not to be outdone, Col and I shocked the table by telling them of the time I had inadvertently booked my own mother-in-law into a house of ill repute! I had answered a classified newspaper advertisement and booked a room for Anne-Sofie and Sven-Erik for their visit. However, when I went to inspect the property a day before their arrival, I discovered its true, hourly-rate nature and that most of their clientele paid strictly by the hour. It was far too late to locate alternative accommodation, so for two full nights, they slept peacefully in a room decorated with a thick, pink, shaggy-pile carpet and mirrors fixed across the entire ceiling. The bathroom was fully carpeted from floor to ceiling, and featured two separate showers sitting in opposite corners of the room. After breakfast, Col and John left for a long hike up to the plateau, while I went with Tara and the nurses to the local lapa area. Tara explained that this was where community activities like dancing, pottery, and sewing lessons took place. It was a large, round clay-floored structure topped with a thick thatch roof and entirely open sides, held up by four sturdy wooden beams. The dirt floor had been hardened over generations of use to a light, smooth colour. Three elderly local women were waiting for our arrival, sitting cross-ankled on the floor surrounded by an array of earthenware clay pots. One woman possessed short grey hair and a single remaining tooth. She beamed at our group happily, gestured for us to sit down on the floor, and proceeded to demonstrate how to thoroughly pound the raw clay to remove any hidden impurities. She then showed us how to expertly shape the pots, working from the inside out and smoothing the clay dextrously with worn, arthritic fingers using nothing but a piece of split bamboo and a smooth river stone. It was absolutely fascinating to watch. Later on, Col sat at the table and set the two Acraea aliciae butterflies he had caught earlier, watched closely by an interested audience of guests. To preserve butterfly specimens in the field, entomologists use specialized entomological envelopes. The wings are folded carefully over the back, and the insect is placed inside a triangular parchment or paper pocket. This is then stored inside an airtight plastic container alongside mothballs or silica gel packets to prevent mould and damage from pests. On our return to the verandah, Tara excitedly announced that her sister would be arriving shortly from Cape Town. We settled down at the table with a cup of tea when suddenly Tara let out an excited squeal as a taxi pulled into the driveway. A virtual replica of Tara emerged from the vehicle. She was a little taller than her elder sister, but otherwise, they were remarkably similar in appearance. Wafa—for that was her name—informed me that she currently lived in the Cape Town suburb of Observatory. We enjoyed a lively supper that evening with lots of chatter and stories while eating roast duck and vegetables. The next day, Col and John decided to go for a walk despite the falling rain, departing into the forest alongside their bearer and chief butterfly spotter. Col looked exactly like an intrepid explorer from a bygone era, dressed in his wide-brimmed hat, many-pocketed khaki safari jacket, and wielding his long butterfly net pole. 

Farewells at Mkudze Bay 

Monday the 5th was our final morning at Namizimu, and I felt genuinely sad to leave. Tara came to my bedroom door carrying my morning tea, and she was quite tearful. She told me she would miss our company terribly and pressed a small heart she had personally hand-fashioned from clay into my palm. She told me I felt like true family to her, and I was deeply touched by her kindness, my own eyes tearing up as well. We enjoyed a long, tight hug. Tara begged us to stay a few hours longer for lunch, so we sat down for a bowl of homemade tomato soup. I accidentally dropped several spoonfuls of olive oil and raw garlic onto my bread and as a direct result, spent the rest of the day smelling exactly like an Italian labourer. We packed the car before the sad farewells commenced. Tara pressed a folded note into my hand and we waved a tearful goodbye as the vehicle set off down the winding mountain pass. I opened Tara's note once we reached the asphalt, and discovered she had written, ‘You are a lovely person’. I wiped away a stray tear, marvelling at how quickly one can make deep friends on holiday who feel as though you have known them for a lifetime. This is, of course, one of the true joys of international travel. We looked out for our holiday cabin at Mkudze Bay. Parsons Cottage featured a lovely, green garden planted with hardy Kikuyu grass and a couple of blooming frangipani trees. I was fumbling around the large kitchen trying to organize our things when a local man named Rollon (pronounced Roll-on) suddenly appeared in the doorway and announced that he was to be our private cook for the stay. He came included with the cottage rental, apparently. I asked him to brew a pot of strong Malawian tea, which he promptly served to us on the wide verandah. Later on, Col and I went for a walk to investigate the area and its potential opportunities for wilderness birding. There were quite a few red-headed weaver (Anaplectes rubriceps) nests strung across the overhead telephone wires, and we clearly heard the distinct call of a trumpeter hornbill (Bycanistes bucinator) echoing through the canopy. Later that evening, Rollon brought us a simple supper of rice and tinned pilchards. 

Chigoni Rock Art and a River Safari 

On Tuesday the 6th of December, Rollon brought us our morning tea at 4:30 a.m. before we headed off towards the Chigoni rock hike. The high plateau where the ancient rock art is located is situated about 80 kilometres south-east of Lilongwe, near the historic town of Dedza. After leaving our vehicle in the care of a local guard, we commenced the uphill hike. The incline was gradual at first, and the early morning mountain air was still beautifully cool. However, the track soon became significantly harder to climb, and despite my best attempts to speak normally to the lads, I found myself sounding exactly like an overheated steam train. John remained amazingly fit and agile despite his advancing years. We successfully located the famous ‘Deus’ rock, which distinctively points the geographical way to the rock paintings situated above a short, steep, rocky ridge. Eventually, we arrived at the face of a huge, overhanging boulder, behind which sat some magnificent rock paintings executed by the pygmy Batwa tribe some 4,000 years ago. The ancient rock art of the Dedza region, a UNESCO World Heritage site, features schematic paintings attributed to the hunter-gatherer Batwa populations. The orangey-red lines, drawn in both horizontal and vertical geometric patterns, are archeologically understood to represent traditional rituals associated with fertility, rainmaking, and ancestral food abundance. We gazed at the ancient markings for a while, each of us lost in our own thoughts, imagining very different historical times. The climb back down the ridge went significantly faster, and soon we were reunited with the car and our guard. Back at the cottage, I discovered Rollon had purchased some fresh local fish for our dinner—specifically chambo tilapia and kampango catfish. He set to pan-frying the catch while I toasted a plate of bread. The three of us ate together at the large dining table. Rollon had also prepared some delicious grilled mango, which accompanied the fish beautifully. Afterwards, the lads went out to set some fruit butterfly traps in the forest, and I decided to just chill on the verandah with my book. On Wednesday the 7th of December, a magnificent crimson sky greeted our eyes as we sat with our mugs of tea outside, admiring the wilderness view. It was noticeably hotter than yesterday already. Col collected several butterflies in his net along the driveway; there were so many specimens around the garden they practically flew straight into his mesh. Rollon cooked us the last of the chambo fish, which we enjoyed with toast, fresh mango, and more strong tea. Col fussed over the insulation of his cold box, which was critical to keep the butterflies intact before our departure for Mulanje. Following some lengthy administrative palaver over the official park fees, John returned to the car to announce we had been upgraded from the tented camp to a luxury lodge. We packed all of our clothing into one single shared bag because we had to travel onwards by boat after a short walk to the riverbank. A beaming park official greeted our family and escorted us to his small, covered boat. We sailed up the Shire River, stopping briefly to watch a small herd of wild elephants munching grass on the river verges. A tiny baby elephant peered at our boat from behind a small bush. At the Mvuu side of the river, a couple of polite waiters assisted us with our luggage up to the lounge area, which directly overlooked the water, before handing out cold damp cloths to wipe our sweaty faces along with a glass of cold orange juice. Both amenities were incredibly welcome. We met the resident host, and John introduced us; he genuinely seems to know every single person living in Malawi. We were handed an indemnity form, which we duly signed, absolving the park authorities from all legal liability should we find ourselves unexpectedly attacked by a wild lion or swallowed whole by a hippo. Our accommodation room was a delightful, luxury safari tent overlooking the river, complete with an outside bath and a shower stall. At lunch, we were introduced to Samuel, our specialist tracker guide. He promised Col he would personally take our family to locate a Pel's fishing owl (Scotopelia peli) and a Böhm's bee-eater (Merops boehmi), among other regional specials, so Col was thoroughly happy and immediately set about cleaning his camera lenses in preparation. Later that afternoon, we were seated on the lodge's open Land Rover vehicle, joined by a trainee guide named Etienne who hailed from Switzerland. The open park grass almost looked as though it had been newly mown by a giant lawnmower, as it was incredibly short and a bright green colour—this manicured look was entirely thanks to the local grazers: kudu, roan antelope, sable antelope, and numerous warthogs. As it became darker in the bush, the driver switched on his specialized infrared spotlight beam, lighting up a sweet little family of warthog ‘piggies’ and a bit later, some springbok. During the drive, I was unfortunately bitten on the arm by a nasty tsetse fly (Glossina), and the skin instantly swelled up into a lump. A stray midge flew straight into Col’s eye as well, so as a thoroughly wounded pair, we bid the group goodnight and headed straight back to our tented room. 

Zomba Plateau Forest Walks 

The next morning, a wild lion roared loudly nearby around 4 a.m., and after meeting the other guests for a quick cup of morning tea, we set off for our early morning game drive. Later on, we joined John, Etienne, and an armed park ranger for a walking safari through the bushveld. We eventually returned to the boat, with Samuel expertly pointing out rare birds as we walked through the grass. At the far side of the river, we found John’s car and commenced the drive towards Lilongwe. We kept a sharp lookout for a petrol station along the highway, but absolutely no one seemed to possess any fuel, which was highly worrying given the distances. Despite the lack of local fuel, we safely arrived back at John's place by 4:30 p.m., and Fumiyo was there to welcome us back with hot tea and homemade mince pies. Fumiyo generously gifted us a wicker basket of food goodies to take with us to our next accommodation—a cottage situated high up on the Zomba Plateau. I hugged her goodbye, and we set off in John's car. The plateau ‘cottage’ turned out to be a massive, old-style colonial house featuring a beautiful, wraparound, red-cement polished verandah. The structure contained five bedrooms and a massive kitchen. We chose our bedroom and greeted the hovering caretaker, who informed us his name was Dayton and that he would be cooking for our family during our stay. I unpacked our bags quickly and handed over a cardboard box containing our various foodstuffs. We bid John a fond farewell and sat out on the wide verandah where Col immediately set some butterflies. Dayton brought us a hot meal of soup and corn on the cob, which featured kernels so hard it felt like eating baby’s teeth! The local village was far too distant to walk to for extra mobile airtime, so I successfully borrowed Dayton’s personal cellphone in exchange for a couple of hundred Kwachas. I suddenly realized I had inadvertently been calling him ‘Titon’ all afternoon, but having received the cash and a huge tip, he seemed perfectly happy to answer to absolutely any name. I sent Tim an SMS message asking him to let me know the exact moment he arrived safely back home, then joined Col for a long walk into the surrounding forest. As we walked down the plateau tracks, the mountain air became noticeably warmer and more humid. A narrow dirt path led down into a steep gully situated directly behind the reservoir dam wall, where Col expertly set his butterfly traps. As we walked, I watched several locals walk past carrying what looked like several full trees' worth of kindling and chopped branches balanced on their heads—truth be told, I seriously doubt there will be any forest left when we next return to Malawi. We enjoyed a final walk through the forest canopy the next morning, looking for unique birds to photograph, before our taxi arrived to transport us to the airport terminal, and we bid a flurry of hasty farewells to John and Fumiyo.
