# Chapter 1: UGANDA — 1960-1970 DISTAFF IMPERIALIST

It was a typically dreary November day in Woolwich, South London. Sporadic rain, sometimes heavy, drifted down the high street and onto the brave stallholders still selling vegetables to housewives hurrying home to a warm fire and a 'cuppa.' Passing cars splashed puddles of water onto pedestrians, and the streetlights gleamed through it all. As my sister-in-law and I tangled our brollies and looked for a specific shop address, I reflected on the many years that had passed since I had endured a real British winter downpour. Not that it didn’t rain in Africa—my word, no! Red mud roads and black cotton soil, dry riverbeds that became insurmountable, roaring torrents of water in just a few moments—we had it all. Then the sun would come out again, and everything would steam dry. Fat chance of that here, though, I thought, bumping into Min’s back as she suddenly stopped before a lighted doorway, saying, 'Here we are!' Here we were indeed. 'JOHN CARTWRIGHT M.P. SURGERY,' announced the sign above the brass knocker. We pushed the door open, shook and folded our umbrellas, and entered. After many years—twenty in all—through many adventures by flood and field, I had turned my back on Africa, reluctantly divorced from my husband and what had become a personal nightmare. The Africa we had sailed out to in the late 1950s had become a place of totally unrecognisable political fury. After almost eleven years of contract medical work in Uganda, we found ourselves at Entebbe airport with just two suitcases following a harrowing and dangerous drive from an upcountry town 375 miles from the capital, Kampala. To say that we were politically naïve would be an understatement. Certainly, no one in their right mind would have decided that we should then go on to South Africa purely because we had heard that the game hunting was 'good there, man!' Honestly… and being told that they needed surgeons was also a total untruth. So I often heard in the preceding years that I was a colonialist, an imperialist, etc. When we first arrived in Cape Town and hired a Volkswagen Beetle, we came out of the hotel one morning to find it spray-painted in white paint: 'GO HOME BRITISH PIGS!' My spouse was from Dublin and merely remarked that they had got that wrong anyway, and I added 'British pigs' to the list. It didn’t encourage any future enthusiasm, and I more or less hovered between outright dislike and poorly hidden tolerance from that time forward. I came to hear and see almost tangible racial hatred on all sides, resulting in my departure five weary years later, grasping the hand of a very bewildered ten-year-old daughter and leaving the older children to complete their education. I went back to what I thought I knew best: my own country. Discussing what I was to do about my future and realising that it was of utmost importance to find accommodation—i.e., no address, no job—Min and I came up with the splendid idea of seeking expert advice. Where better, we thought, than your friendly M.P.? He would surely know what was available and what was not. We were both from Lancashire and used to knowing helpful people in high places. We didn’t forget that this was London, the South, not the North, but hey, we were all English. The room into which we were ushered was basically furnished with good carpets and two leather armchairs drawn up before a large desk. The gas fire behind the desk threw out cheerful warmth. The general impression altogether invited confidence. The man standing before the fire advanced towards us, hand extended, and introduced himself. 'What can I do for you, ladies?' Min looked at me and nodded, so I gave a small account of my situation and waited hopefully for a comment. Sticking his hands into his trouser pockets and bending to peer over the net window curtains, the gentleman rocked backwards and forwards on his heels. Then, turning towards me, he addressed some point to my left: 'You people make me sick!' came the unexpected comment. 'You desert your country for the good life, live off the fat of the land and then come back when things get tough, expecting everyone to fall over themselves to help you out.' Astonished by his vehement reply, I reminded him of the government’s recruiting through the Crown Agents. 'Never heard of them,' he said. Turning to Min, who was sitting open-mouthed, he added, 'I understand you live locally, my dear; now you, I can help.' And again to me, 'I suggest you go back to where you came from.' There didn’t seem much point in replying that I thought I had done just that, or indeed in mentioning that my dad had served in the R.A.F. during the last war in Sicily, Italy, North Africa, and Burma, or that both grandfathers were in the war to end all wars. We left him without farewell. Min was pale and wide-eyed. 'What was all that about?' she asked once we were back in the street. 'I think I have just met apartheid in reverse,' I said. Frankly, it has left me more than a little confused. I think I must be an imperialistic colonial British pig no matter where I am, but especially in Britain! Let me start at the beginning, and you can make up your own mind… Most people would say that beginning any journey to a strange destination is quite an undertaking. To go with two small children under three years of age, to leave home and family and all that is familiar, is emotionally stressful. Travelling with a husband who is eager to experience practising medicine and surgery in very challenging conditions is exciting. However, to do all of this on Friday the thirteenth requires a lack of superstition and great faith in the unknown. We did all of this. 

BRITAIN TO EAST AFRICA Friday 13th November 1959 

MAD DOGS AND IRISHMEN

 A raw November day in 1959 started early. Having bid a tearful goodbye to loved ones and boarded the Boat Train at London's Waterloo station; we pulled into the Pool of London docks. There were bands playing, and groups of people singing hymns to departing missionaries. I took keen note that they did not sing to ‘those in peril on the sea’. The docks were full of ships of all shapes and sizes and standing alone was the KENYA CASTLE passenger liner of the Union Castle line. Gangplanks were down along the side of the ship and harbour crew were loading all manner of crates and goods, hopefully our few household belongings amongst them. Grasping the children’s hands, the suitcases and necessary documents we approached the PASSENGERS only gangplank. A white-coated cabin steward came forward to assist with it all, and our feet took us up into the ship and away from England. Following the steward up stairs and along passages I felt sure that once found, I would never leave the cabin again. Some days and several sea miles later, I thought back to this time to marvel at how quickly we adapted. Naturally to this day I have never admitted that I owed all this direction-finding to my husband and small son, John. Janette and I were constantly lost and found. This I think has more or less described our condition over the past fifty years in all parts of Africa. We tend to locate the nearest LADIES and work from there. Off one small passage containing just four cabins we came to ours happily both opposite each other and with portholes! The cabins each contained two bunk beds one above the other. There were built in wardrobes, and a chest of drawers firmly fixed with brass fittings to the floor. As the portholes were deep set and also fastened with brass locks, it all looked very safe and cozy. Especially so when the rain drifted in from the sea and spattered against the glass. The children’s cabin included strong string nets, which were to be pulled down at night, and in rough weather. My mother, brother and I stayed with our relatives in the Isle of Man during the Second World War, and I returned there to begin nursing training. Consequently I experienced rough seas crossings to the island on the I.O.M. Steamer. This put flight to any fears I may have had about seasickness. Ivan completed a trip to Australia and back as a ships surgeon on the P&O liner’s MOOLTAN. I was soon to discover that he was perfectly capable of walking round the deck at a ninety-degree angle. On the top deck, the ships siren was blasting and a loud speaker calling, ‘All ashore that's going ashore!’. We hurried to the ports side to be covered with streamers thrown up to the passengers standing at the ships rail, which was tossed back again, to shore. Slowly and majestically the ship moved into the Thames estuary. We watched as the land began to slide away into the grey winter afternoon. Below decks again, we found our way to one of the sittings where tea and welcome sandwiches awaited us. Walking around this second floor deck we discovered all manner of places set aside for reading, with writing desks and paper with the Union Castle logo. Here, on a large table were all the days’ newspapers, and magazines. Over in an alcove was a library filled with beautifully bound books, dictionaries, and encyclopaedias. An area decorated with small card tables was already peopled with the avid bridge players, who only subsequently surfaced at our various ports of call. Even at meal times there were fierce remarks about ‘old so and so’ who should have ‘played his hearts seeing that Marion was dummy’. They were behind me when we went ashore at Gibraltar, Genoa, Port Said and Aden. Sitting in exotic surroundings, eating, drinking, and playing bridge! Children were to be catered for separately and special children’s menus were available. We peeped into yet another large room and there were small tables and chairs, toys and everything provided to keep small people happy. My delight knew no bounds when two ‘nursery nurses’ greeted me. My two sat down immediately and made friends with two or three others. They waved me away; I could go now and actually relax for the first time in weeks. I heard numerous accounts of rough seas around the Bay of Biscay. As we put the children into their bunks for the night and pulled down the safety nets, we wondered if it would live up to it's reputation. I slept well, soothed by the gentle rocking and sound of the waves. The next morning the cabin steward brought tea for us and went to the children's cabin with orange juice. An exclamation of surprise alerted me and I walked across quickly. The steward pointed to the chest of drawers. Sleeping peacefully, his head on a pillow, Johnny was lying on top .The safety net was undone. Despite all efforts to keep him in his bunk John spent the entire voyage at night on top of that chest of drawers. He would nap in the afternoon on the bunk bed, but each morning found him curled up on top, and every type of sailor’s knot used to fasten the net was undone. Perhaps this was another way of counting our progress in knots! The sea was still relatively calm and we soon found our sea legs. There were many ways to keep occupied. Deck games and clay pigeon shooting, table tennis and deck quoits for the sporty types, who were often to be seen walking swiftly around the deck morning and evening. Below in the sitting room, a daily draw revealed who guessed correctly the distance traveled on the previous day. We read, walked and generally relaxed. I began a diary to be kept to show the children in later years. Biscay came and went with little to remark about it. On day five we rounded Spain and turned towards the Mediterranean Sea, and our first port of call at Gibraltar. The weather was warmer. Gone were the winter woolies, and out came the cotton dresses and shirts. Incidentally the winter clothes were not needed again until our first home leave two years later. The coast of North Africa was visible through a heat mist.... at long last I felt some reality to what had been until now just a myriad of plans and preparation. Nine months ago Ivan applied to the Crown Agents in London. He obtained a Primary Fellowship in surgery, from The Royal Collage of Surgeons. He was hoping that he could extend his experience in a more remote part of the world and under more challenging, often isolated conditions.The response to the application came some weeks later. Folded in with the forms for personal details etc, sat a huge map of the world. The Foreign Office agents, who acted on behalf of the Colonial Office, marked in red, the countries where medical officers were needed. This was the first time I realised how many countries were still in the colonies at that time. Ivan always enjoyed hunting in Ireland and read much on the first traveler’s ‘to Africa’ - so he chose East Africa. Extensive history about the place existed to all interested parties. Livingstone left for central Africa in his search for the source of the Nile. Speke, Grant and Burton followed and found the source, and the Baganda people. The Church Missionary Society sent out medical missionaries in the late nineteenth century. They established a hospital in Kampala, and many clinics up country. The Catholic Church was not far behind. In the future I was to come across mission hospitals, schools and workshops in the far reaches of Uganda. They were fully autonomous, and served many people over vast areas and many different tribes. As an Irish catholic family we came to know the warm welcome and dedicated work of these priests and nuns. Their tireless work and education for the local villagers bore fruit, which still has effect to this day. As the boat neared Gibraltar, numerous small craft came out to take us ashore. At the small landing stage we looked up to see craggy hills, at the foot of which was the town itself. There were gaily-painted horse drawn carts pulled up to the side of the harbour. All safely inside, we trotted off to the town centre. I remember the total effect of brilliant sun on Spanish style houses and shops. So began a series of photographs, which have filled many albums and boxes since that day. All around us were open-fronted shops selling leather goods, sandals, handbags and pouffes. Everyone I saw was buying something. I would see all of these purchases in our neighbour’s houses wherever we went in East Africa. Following everyone else's lead, we bought sandals, bags and pouffes. The leatherwork and colouring was amazing, but the sewing left a lot to be desired. Also when we arrived at our first posting, it was difficult to find a material suitable for stuffing inside the pouffe. Consequently one saw various lopsided, and leaning small seats, often with the innards protruding outwards. Did I mention the leather camels? These were also intended for seating. Needless to say with the innards problem mentioned above, I never saw anyone astride the camel's back... not even children or inebriated gentlemen. However, anyone doing the Suez trip bought some or all of the stuff for sale. Apart from the bank staff, Standard and Barclay’s, no one could expect much in the way of furniture other than ‘from P.W.D. ( Public Works department) furniture officers for the use of…’ Boarding again with all our goodies, we saw a list of events arranged for our entertainment on the notice board on our way to lunch. The most immediate was for the following afternoon, when we would once again be at sea. It was to be a children’s fancy-dress parade, with prizes for the best costumes. We bought a frilly two-piece bathing suit for Jan, and I thought that with some fruit from the restaurant, a bathing cap, and a small basket, she could go as ‘Miss Carmen Miranda’, as she put it. John was too young and active to stay still for any event. The following day, there was much activity and bustle as mothers put together various costumes. With the help of our cabin steward, we acquired large bunches of bananas and grapes. With these attached to her bathing hat and basket, a long skirt made of crepe paper, and a pair of pink plastic high-heeled slippers, Jan really looked the part. (Carmen Miranda was a Portuguese-born singer and actress of the forties and early fifties whose alluring and flamboyant image made her internationally famous. She was known for her signature fruit-hat outfit that she wore in her American films.) There must have been at least twenty children of all ages in fancy dress, and I think Jan was probably the youngest. The ‘nursery nurses’ took the children behind the curtain, and we took our seats in the audience. There were pirates and sailors, fairies and elves— all the work of clever mothers. They filed onto the stage and walked around before the Purser and his staff, who were judging the event. After some time, our smiles wore a little thin. Where was Miss Carmen Miranda? At last, the curtains opened and out she came, costume intact. All of it, that is, except the hat with the fruit, which she had eaten during the long wait. This was explained to the amusement of all present; there were, however, some very becoming stalks and a couple of banana skins left. The public address system soon informed us of an approaching weather system... Merchant Navy speak for stormy weather. ‘Not in the Med,’ we said, ‘surely not on this lovely sunny day.’ Yet, the stewards were busy tightly closing all the portholes, battening down the hatches and to my amazement, putting up sides on the tables in the dining room. We looked in on the nursery, and there were the children, gently rocking in their chairs and playing happily as usual. As the pitching of the ship increased, the dining room emptied rapidly. Our table, which originally sat six people, now only had four. The ship then started to toss the other way, and ‘number four’ left us, leaving just three: Ivan, myself, and a very hearty gentleman from South Africa, a Mr Taylor. We managed to get through some of our lunch by the expedient of the steward placing the plates of food at the top of the table whilst we dashed to the bottom to catch them. Each tilt of the ship brought the sea crashing over the decks. We collected the children and went to our cabins. They drifted off to sleep, and we tried to do the same. Only Ivan could stand by suppertime, so he brought our food to the cabin on a tray. Clinging to whatever we could grasp, we ate and then prepared for bed. Looking in on us later, the steward said we were in for a bad storm. Visibility was reduced to a few hundred yards and we were bobbing around like a cork. He’d seen worse, he said... but not often. All through that night, we rocked and rolled. No matter how often one pushed oneself to the top of the bunk, one's feet would slide back down to touch the bulkhead at the bottom. I wondered if we would feel safer in a hammock. Ivan and the children slept well, but I was too scared and tried to remember ( should it become necessary) that our boat stations and cabins were beneath the entertainment lounge. Some time during the night, the grand piano broke loose and careered over the deck, clanging and banging out a strange jumble of chords. This was, I knew for certain, the ship's swan song—a symphony to shipwreck. I prayed for morning, calm, and my mother. The following day found us all well but unable to stand upright for any length of time. Holding onto the guide rails, we optimistically made our way to the dining room. There was, again, a mere fraction of the usual diners. At the head of our table sat a beaming Mr Taylor. As I took my seat, the ship gave a spectacular roll and I held onto the table. Just then, the steward appeared with the breakfast menu. Rubbing his hands together enthusiastically, Mr Taylor exclaimed, ‘Oh good oh… bangers!’ I thought carefully about bangers for a moment and decided that I would be better off lying flat... on my bunk, and left to do just that. Ivan and Mr Taylor were left to enjoy a full breakfast, followed by a brisk, ninety-degree-angled walk round the deck. The following day bloomed bright and calm. The ship settled to an even pace and several wan but determined passengers braved the deck circuit. Rug-wrapped, with faces held to the sun, we steamed on towards our next port of call: Genoa. The children seemed no worse for the adventure, and we looked forward to getting onto ‘terra firma’ early the next day. The sun shining through our porthole wakened us. We ate breakfast (well attended this time) and joined the queue to board the tender, which came alongside. Arriving at the busy harbour, we separated and went our various ways towards the town. The roadside cafes were beginning to fill, and we walked past stalls full of vegetables and fruit, the like of which one only saw seasonally in England: huge red tomatoes, green peppers, spinach, and strings of onions and garlic, with stallholders calling out to us to buy. All were speaking in ‘Beautifula Eenglisha’ and Italian. I filled my Gibraltar bag with oranges and plums, and we went to sit on a sunny white wall to eat them. We drank coffee, which tasted like no other instant stuff, and bought huge ice creams of many flavours. Our bodies responded to the warmth as we walked, bringing a feeling of well-being that had been completely denied us during the strenuous months of preparation for the journey. Dancing occurred in the ballroom after dinner, along with much strolling round the deck, holding hands and gazing at the moon. The skies were heavy with stars. The more energetic and less romantic of us played endless games of table tennis, the ship now as steady as a rock. We now slept with the portholes wide open, and there was scarcely a breeze. At last, the only means of air conditioning, the punkahs, were turned this way and that to catch whatever cooling draught of air there might be. The name, I understand came along with many other English words from India. Here, the fans beating—or being pulled by means of a rope—were called punkahs. One huge drawback was that the funnels of the liner often blew large pieces of soot into the system. Thus, one could awake from sleep looking like one of the Black and White Minstrels*, and the pillowcases and sheets suffered equally. Great excitement ensued as we prepared to enter the Suez Canal. Passengers lined the rails as we passed the ruins of the statue of Lesseps, which was bombed during the ‘argument’ between Britain and the Middle East in the early fifties. *The Black and White Minstrel Show was a British television song-and-dance show in the late 1950s and 1960s that predominantly featured white performers wearing blackface makeup. It is considered highly offensive and non-PC today. The canal had only been reopened for shipping a few years prior. We entered a totally new world of high-piled dunes, with width enough for only two ships to pass going either way, and caught our first glimpse of Port Said. Numerous small boats were making for the ship, with many shouts from each one urging us to buy whatever they could load into their hands. Trinkets, carvings, and clothing in astonishingly lurid colours and designs were offered. One fellow, more persistent than the others, began climbing up a rope ladder near the passenger exit. Once on board, he did a couple of dervish leaps and announced that he was a magician and would perform for the children in the afternoon. We were to stay in Port Said for two days, as many passengers wanted to visit the pyramids. Hastily assembling children and pushchairs, we joined the others going ashore. I turned to look back at the ship as we sped off in the usual craft sent to take us ashore. From the lower deck crew quarters, there emerged someone who, at first glance, wore the same bouncy appearance of a pantomime dame. Dressed to the nines, gloved and hatted, was our cabin steward. His high heels clicked as he minced onto the harbour road. He paused only to open his sunshade before tottering off towards the waiting taxis. He was obviously very well known, judging by the ribald remarks and ‘offers’ of assistance that followed him. Ivan looked amused, while the children commented on his fancy dress and left it at that. The town was everything I’d read of: the charm, the colour, and the bustling, noisy merchants. We went into the local markets—the souq—and saw on all sides stalls selling everything to attract the eye and the pocket. We purchased one or two small items. Obviously, the passenger liners were considered fair game. Moving away from the rigidity of payment in Western society, I was now expected to bargain. This proved to be as difficult as breaking plates at a Greek wedding. Not knowing the value of anything was an obvious drawback, and of course, the merchant took full advantage of this. As the morning passed and the heat of the sun increased, we found a shaded sidewalk cafe, bought surprisingly cold lagers and vividly coloured cool drinks, and watched the many different tribes and peoples passing to and fro before us. Tired and satiated with sights, sounds, and colour, we made our way back to the ship for a siesta. At 4 p.m., the Gilly man, or magician, arrived. The assembled children were treated to displays of silk scarves, a bunch of flowers growing out of a turban sported by the Gilly, and numerous bouquets of paper flowers. A child was called forward and found to have pound notes and coins behind her ears and in her hair—which had no doubt been free of such things when Mummy dressed her after her siesta. The children's faces showed the appropriate wonder, and with a final jump in the air, the Gilly passed around his turban for more of the same to be magically revealed (to less interest, of course, at the next show). Despite the heat that persisted until sundown, the children fell asleep at once. Probably, they dreamed of the Gillyman and his magic. With child-minders in place, we dressed and sallied forth to taste the nightlife. Jackson, once again rid of his alter ego, was most helpful in suggesting places to eat. Choosing a restaurant not too far from the ship, we were shown to our table. It was round and could be turned to accommodate the many small and tasty bowls of lamb, fish, salads, and fruit. In the very centre was a large round of unleavened bread. The idea was to tear off a chunk and dip it into the many tasty sauces and other dishes. A rather tinny musical background squeaked through a barely recognisable ‘medley’. I identified ‘Some Enchanted Evening’ and ‘Hernando’s Hideaway’. A roll of drums, the curtains at the back of the room parted, and out sashayed a flimsily clad lass. She was shapely though heftily built, which undoubtedly helped with the wiggles. The tempo increased from the musical trio, and her belly rippled and swayed. As she danced, she moved from table to table, stopping where there were gentlemen who popped money into whatever crevice and ‘allowed access’ throughout the beads and baubles. As she came over to us, I could only feel admiration for such expert muscle control. From the look on his face, I guessed that my husband was applying a little more interest than a general knowledge of anatomy would allow. However, what struck me most forcibly was the look of utter boredom displayed on her rather fleshy face. Her eyes were quite blank and seemingly uninterested in her surroundings. I have often seen the same expression on the faces of others doing a boring and repetitive job. Travelling through the Suez Canal, one is aware of a total, blanketing silence. The mountainous piles of sand muffle all noise. Occasionally there is a ‘ploff-ploff’ sound and out of nowhere, camels and riders appear, swaying along the canal bank. A jingle of a harness, a hand raised in greeting, and then all is still again as our ship glides on. The good Mr Taylor—our table companion and ‘banger’ lover— was loud in his praise of our next stop at the Port of Aden. ‘You can buy absolutely anything there,’ he said. ’Especially clothes, shoes, and canteens of cutlery.’ Docking there the next day was rather like parking a large car. We seemed to just pull up to the jetty. The Captain put on the handbrake, and there we were—parked, so to speak. Here was something totally strange. There were market stalls right up to the jetty, two, three, four deep. I had never seen silver, gold, and brass for sale in such profusion. There were ornaments and exquisite jewellery—rings, bracelets, and necklaces set with semi-precious stones. Walking up the main street, we passed shops with young men outside who called to passers-by to come inside and buy shirts, trousers, and dresses. Despite the shade provided by the stall fronts, the heat was almost too much. An announcement from the Captain this morning warned passengers going ashore that the temperature was 45 degrees Celsius, and climbing! We were advised to wear hats and to go back to the ship at noon, when everything ashore would close until sundown. Passing into the cool interior of the nearest shop, we were immediately invited to sit, and cups of cardamom coffee were brought to us. Ivan was interested in having two pairs of khaki trousers made. Out came the measuring tape, and in a trice, sizes were written down with a request to return the next morning to collect them. An enquiry about leather sandals produced the same abracadabra results; they would also be ready the next day. The intensity of the sun as we made our way back can barely be described. All available shaded deck space was taken up by people dressed in as little as possible, reluctant to go down to the sweltering cabins. Somehow, we fell into a restless doze and made our way towards cups of tea and cool drinks in the late afternoon. As darkness came with very little warning—only ten minutes of twilight between day and night—the souk stalls dazzled and shone with coloured lights strung the length of the main street. There appeared to be much more lively trading now than in the morning. Those of us who were inclined to do so bought more and more, with the thought, no doubt, that we were heading for ‘darkest Africa’ and the possibility of few department stores. The trousers and shoes were ready for collection the next morning as promised. I cannot vouch for the sandals, but I do know that the trousers lasted through many years of rigorous bush safaris. As we headed once more into the Red Sea, and until we got away from the mind-blowing heat, each day passed in attempts to do as little as possible, wearing as little as was acceptable and drinking copious fluids. Most meals were served on deck; Mr Taylor’s all-fried breakfasts became a thing of the past. Somewhat cooler days were forecast as we sailed into East African coastal waters. Conversations tended to deal with what lay ahead, and the first-timers sought the company and advice of old hands. As we neared Zanzibar, the smells on the breeze were those of spices, even as far out to sea as we were. Paludrine anti-malarial tablets, taken for the first of many future years, brought to us the first sense of possible danger on our journey. The old, intrepid travellers relied on cinchona bark, and the quinine derived from it. There are many stories of how this wonder cure came about. The version which I heard concerns a warrior suffering from a fever who was left by his comrades next to a river to die. They laid him beneath an overhanging tree and left him with a gourd filled with water. To their amazement, he caught up with them some time later, fully recovered. He said that as he drank the water from the gourd, the leaves from the tree above fell into the water. He chewed the leaves and began to feel stronger as the fever left him. Future travellers identified this tree and advertised its medicinal properties. My father, along with most other troops posted to the Far East and tropics during the Second World War, was given Mepacrine to take. I can only say that when he returned to England from service in Burma, his face was very thin and his colour yellow. He suffered yearly from bouts of malaria, which only stopped after ten years or so. We were to see much suffering and untimely deaths from severe forms of malaria in Uganda. For now, as we approached Mombasa—our place of disembarkation—the first slight shadow was cast. Adventure lay ahead, but there existed dangers too. Now was the time to pack our suitcases and prepare to leave what had been our home for the past three weeks. This, too, was a total farewell to England. The last few days and evenings were spent in discussion with our shipboard family, coming to terms with the reality of living in Africa. Approaching Mombasa, the perfume of spices intensified, and the land came closer and closer. On the last day, all was ready; the purser was paid for monies owing, and a tip was given to the cabin steward. The harbour emerged out of the heat mist and passengers jostled together, clutching bags and children. The Uganda Medical Department had arranged train tickets for a four-berth carriage leaving Mombasa in the early evening. Amidst much unloading of boxes and trunks from the hold, we headed towards the customs sheds. The sun beat down on the corrugated roof, and I wondered if I would ever get accustomed to such savage heat. Sitting on a suitcase as Ivan went off to identify our boxes, I experienced the efficiency of the Indian and Goan officers employed in the East African Harbours Service. Everyone was polite and helpful. In a last clinging to those who were familiar, we lunched at an open-air restaurant with friends who were to go on to Zambia. A taxi brought us to the railway station, where there was much hustle and bustle. The coal- and wood-fired engine steamed gently, and a porter took us down the length of the train to a carriage marked with our surname. What a delight awaited us: four berths in a spotlessly clean carriage. A wooden table beneath the open window proved to cleverly cover a sink and brass taps. A power point for an electric razor lay above the small shelf, and there was plenty of legroom. This was to be our home for the next two days and nights. My biggest problem was going to be the control of one small and very active boy, who was already testing the leather straps holding open the window. Jan, as always, anxiously took hold of his hand and small as she was, began what has been a lifetime of keeping her brother from harm. The sound of music played on a small xylophone came closer down the corridor, and a dining steward poked his head round the door to introduce himself and announce that tea was being served in the dining car. Departure time came and with much steaming, chugging, and whistle-blowing, we pulled out of the station. I can never express enough the feelings of that single departure. Well, you are now on your own, I thought. There was so much that was totally new, even in our immediate environment. Looking out of the window as the train gathered speed, I saw the strange landscape and almost at once, the gaily-dressed African people going about their daily work: huge rocks and thick vegetation, tall trees with fleshy leaves, mud huts, and palms. For a moment of sheer panic, I wanted to shout out for it all to stop. It was too much; I wished to turn back to the ship and all that was familiar. 

Britain Rules OK! 

As I watched, the gecko’s tail lifted; he suddenly unfurled a long length of tongue and neatly rolled up a passing fly. This was my first ‘wild animal’. Well, at least one didn’t see geckos in England. We had arrived in Uganda some three weeks before. Having enjoyed a brief stay at the Imperial Hotel in Kampala, the capital, we were ‘posted’ up-country to the north-west: to Fort Portal, at the foot of the Ruwenzori Mountains, or the Mountains of the Moon. The town was so named by one of the first men to explore East Africa, Sir Gerald Portal. The main housing provided was centred around a fort with a deep moat, now quite dry, which protected the Golf Club—the nineteenth hole being the centre for meetings and entertainment. The street names were mostly those of other such intrepid travellers of the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. Thus, our house was at the top of Speke Lane, on a hill overlooking the twenty or so houses of various government employees. We were all recruited through the auspices of the Crown Agents in London on behalf of Her British Majesty’s Government. On arrival, we were briefed by the ADC (Assistant District Commissioner) and instructed in the aims of our respective jobs. This, in the main, was to prepare the Ugandan people for self-government, and to leave everything ‘nice and tidy when we left’. We were to learn Swahili, a lingua franca, and keep order, guided by the PC (Provincial Commissioner) and his assistant, the ADC. Our homes, in the main, were very simply constructed: open-plan sitting and dining rooms, two bedrooms, a kitchen, and the usual offices. Basic furniture was supplied from a central source known as the PWD (Public Works Department). We learned over the ensuing years not to accumulate much in the way of personal belongings. One was literally moved at a moment's notice, and the red murram roads (a type of orange-red soil often used to make roads in Africa) were not kind to delicate furniture. So here I was, sitting on the rattan base of ‘couch, sitting room, one, Govt. Order 131/BA’… padded with a folded blanket until the appropriate cushions and covers were ready for collection from the local shop, or duka. As these were entirely owned and run by Asian shopkeepers, many of the names and references were Eastern. The cushions, when they finally appeared, lasted through eleven years of moves up, down, and across Uganda. I cannot say they were aids to relaxation, but as everyone else bought exactly the same, one got used to them. Similarly, my kitchen was stocked with pots and pans, and the room-divider-cum-sideboard with crockery and cutlery. We awaited the arrival of our packing cases, which had come with us on our sea journey to Mombasa. Eventually, I made curtains from lengths of the strong linen material called merikani, and bought simple grass-woven mats. At the bottom of the garden was a two-roomed brick building with an outside toilet and shower. This, I was told, was the staff quarters. It was the contemplation of how to fill these rooms that had led me to sit and watch the gecko. The house was wired for electricity, but there was no supply to Fort Portal as yet. The kitchen contained a wood-burning stove, and outside the kitchen door was a recess in the wall, also requiring blocks of wood to be lit beneath a cistern to heat the bath water. The PWD provided an enormous iron with a hinged compartment for charcoal. To use the iron, one took charcoal from an outside fire, packed it into the space under the iron, and then swung the heavy implement back and forth to catch the breeze and make the charcoal glow. It was a special gift to be able to dash away with this smoothing iron and not get smuts all over the laundry. We bought Petromax lamps, pressure-pumped lamps, and a paraffin stove. I thought with regret of the mixer steam iron and table lamps currently on their way to us in the packing cases. Anyone who could help with all of this would certainly be very welcome. Thus, with a more positive approach, I answered a gentle tap at the back door. It alerted me to someone’s presence, and on answering, I had my first lesson in the employment hierarchy. She was trim, neat, and spoke a little English. Handing me an envelope, she said, ‘Jambo, Memsahib. I am coming for the ayah job. This is my barua.’ Inside the envelope were a number of letters of recommendation from former employers, all praising the excellence of Regina’s work and giving reasons for her leaving, which were always due to a ‘posting to another place’. It further told me of Regina’s capabilities with children, laundry, and ironing. I was wondering how to handle it all and politely refuse the help, when my closest neighbour called over the hedge to tell me that I would need not only an ayah, but also an mpishi (cook) and a shamba (garden) boy, not to mention a ‘houseboy’ for general cleaning. ‘But why so many?’ I asked. ‘Because you are giving them not only work and a wage, but prestige,’ she answered. Staff soon found a way to our door through word of mouth. My next applicant was Johannes, a tall and very dignified-looking cook who was no longer young. Proffering an impressive sheaf of papers, he said he had previously worked for a former PC, a titled gentleman who had since retired. Johannes’s baruas (letters) were written in high praise of his variety and standard of work. Since most of the local staff had been schooled at the Catholic missions, they mostly chose saints' names for English reference. So, it was with little surprise that I went on to hire Joassi (whom Petrus followed later) to work in the garden. My husband with his anatomical turn of mind, referred to them always as ‘Yerarssi and Yeranus’, which embarrassed me and confused our guests. One learned over the years to discern the true meaning behind these barua references. Thus, “Lucas tells me he has worked for me for the past year” was interpreted as: ‘this fellow is going to be absent so often that he will only be visible to collect his pay.’ Or, “Mattias has great spiritual moments” meant: lock the drinks cupboard. The rolling foot hills were planted to tea and coffee and the climate at an altitude of 5,000 ft. plus black volcanic soil made it perfect for gardening. One need only to plant the vegetables of choice and stand back whilst these perfect conditions took over. It was said that, if left in the ground, ones own thumb would take root. Fruit trees also gave of their best and so we grew plantains which were the staple diet of the local people, bananas both large and small, sweet pink mangos and pineapples, passion fruit and peaches. The main street in town, one of three had several small shops down either side. Many sold pots and pans and small packets of this and that. There were three large shops. A Mr. Tubby Jeraj owned a material and clothing shop. Inside one purchased the materials for the P.W.D. cushions which were made up outside by an African gentleman peddling away on a Singer treadle sewing machine. This same fellow was able to make up any item of clothing chosen from a very dog-eared pattern book. He would study the picture for a while, take up his large scissors and cut away at the material. The resulting garment was amazingly well made and the ‘memsahibs’ fairly fashionably dressed. Ivan wore a khaki hunting jacket made which was his pride and joy with a large pocket at the back to hold binoculars and several small pockets for ammunition. The two other shops were owned and efficiently run by members of the Bhimji family. The first Bhimjis literally walked up from the coast in Kenya in the early days following the First World War. This is when the East and Central areas of Africa were being divided up and land was offered to returning ex army personnel for tea and coffee planting. It was also a time when the first young officers went out to administrate on behalf of the British Government. Despite making up less than 1% of the population, the Ugandan Indian walk into Bhimji’s grocery shop was to be taken back in time. Glass fronted shelves held bottled, packed and tinned goods as many and varied as any large store in England. A mahogany counter stood on two sides, which held small and large scales and the bacon and meat-slicing machine. The huge refrigerators were paraffin fuelled, as there was as yet no electricity in Toro. At the back of the shop and up a flight of stairs was the glass walled office. Here sat Mr, Bhimji senior. He, his brothers and sons were always impeccably dressed; white shirt and tie, and always pleasant and helpful. The family also owned the shop across the road Western Province Hardware, where one could buy make – up, perfume handbags and soft furnishings. After the First World War many army personnel returned from the absolute mayhem of Flanders to a country financially beggared. They looked for opportunities to settle in commonwealth countries and the government offered various schemes. East Africa was a place of much adventure, named the Pearl of Africa by Winston Churchill. Financial assistance was given to those wishing to take advantage of the opportunities to settle, grow tea coffee and sisal. I was privileged to meet many of these old timers, now in their seventies, and to hear first hand of the early days. Most arrived in Mombasa and traveled as far as the East African Railways progressed. The journey was completed in a series of daily hikes with porters carrying luggage in predetermined size and weight on their heads. Through his interest in hunting, my husband was brought into contact with many of these brave fellows and their wives. Our main source of information came from a retired A.D.C. who lived worked and hunted in Western Province for forty years. He escorted the Duke and Duchess of York when they visited and before they became King and Queen of England in 1937. He told us that she was a much better shot than her husband. Captain Edward Temple-Perkins came out to the country in the early 1920’s. He was involved in administration of the enormous district of Toro and a friend to the Omukhama (king). His knowledge of game movement and tracking was unparalleled; his special interest however was in elephants. His book, ’THE KINGDOM OF THE ELEPHANT’ was much sought after by new arrivals interested in big game hunting. The others were tea and coffee planters also ex army, and they would meet most Saturdays to collect their mail at the small shop/café/bar in town. The place eventually became known as the GLUE POT since most people appeared stuck to their seats. As one drink became two or three, the talk became more boisterous. During one especially noisy session it was suggested that it would be interesting to see if one could stick a penny stamp on the backside of an elephant. Amid much laughter the party broke up. To the amazement of all, the following week one of the infamous Lemon brothers brought out a rather blurred photograph taken, he alleged with his box brownie camera. It appeared that there was indeed a stamp on the backside of an elephant standing obligingly side on. People were busy handing over the payment of their bet when ‘Bunduki’ Gunn announced that it was invalid. Everyone looked at him in surprise, for the evidence was there for all to see; ‘It isn’t franked’ he said! 

And that’s all she wrote! My dear mum died in 2014. I wish she had written more. Dad was the black sheep of the Crowley family; he sought adventure, and his true passion was wildlife. His happy place was in the bush, far away from civilisation. He had a tremendous sense of humour—dry and witty—and he could have friends heaving with laughter just by relating some incident that had occurred. He also possessed a wonderful artistic side.I’m not that fond of his having hunted wild animals but he assured me once they were either dangerous ( to locals) or wounded in some way. Mum was quieter and more fearful of the unknown. Life in rural Uganda was challenging for her, but she was incredibly brave when it was most needed. Her favourite place was behind the pages of a book—ideally one that was far more adventurous than she was. Mum shared Dad’s sense of the ridiculous, and she loved putting her personal mark on whatever new home we were renting; she would paint flowers around the plain PWD light shades or sew vibrant blossoms onto plain brown cushions. She was also an excellent nurse: calm, reassuring, and practical. I inherited Dad’s sense of humour, his love of wildlife, a creative streak, and an interest in gadgets that promised to do all sorts of exciting new things. From Mum, her English sense of what is right: social correctness, a deep love of reading, a fear of drowning (neither of us could swim), an inner strength when it’s most required, and a strong, unwavering sense of duty. 

UGANDA PEOPLE 

There were many interesting characters in Uganda during our time there; the country seemed to encourage them. Take Jack Lemon, one of the Lemon brothers mentioned earlier, (the other being ‘Squash’ Lemon) who lived in a very small tin-roofed house that sat astride a small stream. In fact, the stream ran straight through the dining room, and one had to be sure-footed when walking around. Jack always insisted that he was well-read, claiming to have read countless books and owned a vast library of them. One day, after a gin-fuelled discussion while sitting astride the stream in his house, dad asked him where his library was. ‘ I’ve lent them all out Ivan’. he huffed. Later, he had cause to open an old wooden chest that stood in the lounge. Dad peered over his shoulder and saw a single, well-thumbed book lying on top. It was titled On the Cultivation of Asparagus. Much laughter erupted at the aforementioned ‘Glue Pot’ the following night when Dad told everyone that the only book Jack owned was on cultivating asparagus. Our chief cook and bottle-washer, Joassi, was a good cook —when he was sober. When he wasn’t (which unfortunately, was frequently), we would find him in the dustbin; having climbed in remorsefully, he would always place the lid over his head. He wasn’t averse to replacing whisky with water in the decanter either, as Dad found out on more than one occasion. At this time, Dad was the medical officer and general surgeon in Gulu, the marketing centre of the main agricultural region of northern Uganda, where cotton, tea, coffee, maize, sorghum, and tobacco were grown in the surrounding area. The home telephone rang one Saturday afternoon. Dad was on call, so he answered reluctantly and shortly afterwards, grabbed his white coat, yelling out that there was an emergency operation to attend to and he was off. Operations generally took a few hours, so we were somewhat surprised to hear the car tyres screeching to a halt only an hour later. Dad came rushing in looking for Mum. ‘Glenys! Glen, where are you?’ he called, rushing through to the lounge where Mum had her nose in a book. ‘I need you at the hospital,’ said Dad, a little out of breath. Mum looked up, surprised. ‘Whatever for?’ she asked, putting her book aside with a furrowed brow. ‘I need you to assist me quickly with a patient,’ said Dad. ‘I’ve left him anaesthetised on the table, but we’re in the middle of the op,’ he continued. Mum got up reluctantly; she was a trained nurse but had not practised for many years. ‘Okay, I’m coming, but what on earth happened?’ she asked, reaching for her bag. ‘I’d just removed a panga—a large curved knife—from this man’s head,’ explained Dad, rushing back towards the door. ‘My assistant surgeon was looking a little pale, and he suddenly bolted- absconded, but we’re not finished. I’ve left the matron watching the poor man’s oxygen levels, but I need you to take over while I finish suturing.’ Dad later said Mum was brilliant. She donned a gown and mask, scrubbed up, and took over from the matron, who had been keeping the man anaesthetised using an open-mask method—dripping a liquid anaesthetic agent onto a gauze-covered mask placed over the patient's nose and mouth. This method was simple and didn't require complex machinery, but it was incredibly difficult to control the dosage, leading to a constant risk of overdose or insufficient anaesthesia. Dad said that whenever he looked up, he was shocked to see Mum’s pale face and blue eyes instead of his erstwhile assistant's dark brown eyes and skin. They managed to complete the operation successfully, and the patient was wheeled into the intensive care unit with a neat row of sutures in place of the panga in his skull. At home, Mum was shaken but quite pleased with herself. Dad poured her a beer and they clinked glasses. ‘To the best anaesthetist in Uganda!’ he said, smiling proudly at her. The family owned a small black-and-white television on which we were allowed to watch Lost in Space, Bewitched, Mister Ed (the talking horse), and The Man from U.N.C.L.E. in the evenings for two hours before bed. Often, the broadcast service would get disrupted. When this happened, we knew it was a simple matter of pulling up a master switch. However, the switch wasn’t located at home. Occasionally, Mum would phone the broadcasting company, and once or twice she or Dad had to personally walk down to the local television station, slip past the snoring night manager, and push the switch back to the ‘ON’ position to restore the reception. Our first house in Fort Portal was overrun with cockroaches. They littered the entire outside kitchen and could be found in the cupboards and the oven in their millions. In those days, we used Flit to kill insects. This was a liquid insecticide dispensed via a long cylindrical tin canister equipped with a manual pump mechanism. Batches of Flit contained DDT, which we used liberally with little to no effect on the cockroaches, though it may well account for a few later health issues in humans. It was the bane of my poor mother’s life, trying to avoid stepping on the multitude of roaches while attempting to cook. She eventually left the kitchen to our mpishi and only went in on his off days. As the house was not electrified, we relied entirely on gas or kuni (wood) stoves. Every evening, the doors and windows were firmly closed, and the Flit pump was used to spray each room to keep the mosquitoes at bay. Situated at the base of the Ruwenzori Mountains, Fort Portal was a vibrant township surrounded by lush, rolling hills, rocky crater lakes, and sprawling tea plantations. On a cloudless day, the snow-capped summits of the mountains were breathtakingly visible. I remember it as being intensely tropical and verdant. 

Uganda Safari

Dad took eight-year-old John on safari with him. It was to be a bonding experience. One day, Dad, his askaris, and John were out looking for spoor. John was lagging a bit behind. Dad turned around just as John passed under a huge, overhanging tree and noticed a pair of glowing copper eyes in the thick branches. He froze; he had instantly identified a leopard and hissed a warning to John to keep walking slowly, cautioning him not to turn around or look up. Meanwhile, Dad raised his rifle, keeping it trained on the beautiful but dangerous animal until John had safely reached them. Dad continued to walk backwards, his aim steady, for another ten metres. Thankfully for all concerned, the leopard remained lying watchful but still. Dad reckoned it had recently made a kill and was both tired and full—a lucky turn of events for my brother. The following day, they were tracking a wounded topi antelope when they came across a small village deep in the bush. As they stood contemplating their next move, it was suddenly made for them when two semi-naked villagers arrived and thrust their spears on either side of them. The Baganda people, one of the largest ethnic groups in the region, are known to use spears as part of their traditional weaponry, essential for hunting, livestock herding, and warfare. In this instance, they were making a very clear statement. Since neither side spoke the other's language, a series of hand signals ‘suggested’ they follow the two men. Dad and John were led to a large, round mud hut. Its sheer size indicated that the owner held a high status in the tribe, and indeed, it turned out to be the Chief’s hut. They stooped to enter the open doorway and stood blinking as their eyes slowly adjusted to the darkness. It was incredibly dark and smoky, and it was only when Dad bumped into something solid that he realised the entire entrance was filled with Ankole cattle. These are the magnificent, long-horned cattle of the area, and the most prized possession of the tribe. John and Dad carefully picked their way over the warm, wide bodies, taking care to avoid the pointy ends of their massive horns. Chickens clucked and squawked, pecking at the dry, packed earthen floor as smoke stung their eyes. Eventually, they came to a tall, dark-skinned man wearing a leopard skin and an elaborate headpiece that included a few bicycle spokes, some Coca-Cola bottle tops, and the thick, striped tail of some erstwhile animal. He motioned for Dad and John to sit in front of the open fire, which they did with alacrity before their tender skin could meet the cold arrowheads of the spears. Thus began the most bizarre conversation. It turned out the Chief could count up to six in English but otherwise knew no words, so he spoke entirely in numbers. With many hand gestures and counting, Dad came to understand that he was deemed in debt to the Chief for approaching his wildlife (the topi), and needed to provide something valuable before they would be allowed to leave. ‘Two, four, one!’ the Chief repeated meaningfully, shaking his beaded wooden stick. Dad quickly whispered to John, who was carrying a small backpack on his narrow shoulders. ‘Did you bring the whisky bottle?’ John nodded. Dad turned back to the Chief and said, ‘I have whisky!’ Naturally, the Chief had no idea what he was saying, so John reached behind, withdrew a three-quarters-full bottle of Johnnie Walker—which was alternately used for treating wounds and as a nightcap at camp—and nervously presented it. The Chief clasped the bottle in one hand and twisted the cap off with the other. He took a huge sniff, and beamed. ‘Four, two, five, six!’ he smiled, instructing one of his tribesmen to fetch a cow horn. He poured a generous measure of whisky into the horn and took a sip. He alternated between coughing and smiling, so Dad un-tensed his shoulders and gave him a thumbs-up sign. However, they were only permitted to leave after both Dad and John had shared some of the spirit. The atmosphere changed instantly from suspicion to extravagant bonhomie. After another half an hour or so of ‘number conversation’, Dad got up, followed by John. They bowed, flung a few arbitrary numbers back at the Chief and slowly backed out of the dark, smoky interior into the warm, fresh air. ‘I knew that whisky would come in handy,’ Dad said. John merely nodded and replied, ‘Let’s get out of here before he changes his mind.’ 

The Bank Manager 

We were staying at a hotel in Masaka, west of Lake Victoria, for a few days before moving again to Fort Portal in the south-west. Mum and Dad announced their intention to drive up there ahead of us for a few days to look for houses to rent. They left us in the care of our housemaid, Miriam, who also stayed at the hotel but in the servants’ quarters. On the first day after they left, we spent most of our time at the swimming pool, breaking only for meals. I had my nose firmly in a book, since I didn't swim. The following day, I struck up a conversation with what I considered, at the tender age of eight, to be an old man. In fact, he was only in his early fifties. He chatted to me about the book I was reading and then asked where our parents were, mentioning that he had noticed we were alone the day before. I told him they had gone to Fort Portal to look for a house. He smiled and asked if John, my sister Jacqui, and I would like to go to his house for lunch. I was getting bored of reading and thought it was an excellent idea. I rounded up my siblings to ask their thoughts. Jacqui, always the sensible one, said she would stay at the hotel, but John was keen. This posed a bit of a dilemma, since we had been strictly told not to leave Miriam’s sight for any reason. I figured Miriam could stay with Jacqui, and John and I could accompany him to his home, which he said was nearby. I must interject at this point that there is absolutely no way this would happen today, but at that time no one considered safety issues the way we do today. From my childhood perspective, I felt he was genuinely kind, so John and I followed him to his Mercedes parked in the hotel lot and climbed into the back. John was fascinated by this luxurious mode of transport and asked typical boyish questions about its speed and performance. Only a short while later, Mr X pulled up outside a huge, automated gate. It slowly swung open and we drove in. John and I were immediately in awe of the vast grounds, the rolling green lawns, and the mansion of a house covered in vibrant pink bougainvillea and surrounded by trees. A large, oval swimming pool sat centre stage, sparkling in the midday sunlight. We hopped out and followed Mr X into the house. He asked what we would like to eat and rattled off a selection of tasty options. The kitchen was huge and seemed to have every modern convenience available—well, for the times, anyway. Mr X fetched a large blanket, brought it outside, and spread it on the beautiful lawn. John and I happily ate a large plate of sandwiches while Mr X sat in a deckchair, chatting with us or watching as we played. I remember rolling down the huge lawn and running back up to do it again, laughing as children do, while Mr X looked on with an indulgent smile, sipping his beer. We sat outside for an hour or so, drinking Fanta and eating cake, before following Mr X back into the house to explore the rest of it. We were curious to see what else he had. He showed us various rooms. When we came to what must have been his study, John spotted a square, metal, box-like item on his desk and asked what it was. Mr X explained it was a tape recorder and showed us how to use it. John was fascinated and said, ‘Ooh, I wish we had one!’ Mr X smiled and said, ‘You can have this one!’ As the eldest by thirteen months, I interjected and told him it was very kind of him, but we weren't allowed to accept gifts from people we didn't know. ‘But you know me now!’ exclaimed Mr X with a smile. ‘Please, take it! I have no need for it now.’ Well, you can’t say we didn't try! He showed us a few more rooms and I exclaimed at one strange rug that seemed to be made from animal skins. ‘It’s rock hyrax skins,’ said Mr X. ‘They’ve been sewn together. You can have it.’ I protested again, but he insisted, so I gathered the rug up in my arms, thinking that Mum would love it. There was a beautiful silver tray in the lounge as we passed by, and I told Mr X that Mum loved silver and owned many silver items. The next minute, Mr X had picked up the tray and thrust it into my arms alongside the rug. We carried our newly acquired gifts outside again and had another drink of Fanta. After another hour or so of play and chatter, watched over by Mr X, I turned to him and said I thought it was time to go back to the hotel, because Mum and Dad would be returning soon and would worry if we weren't there. Mr X got to his feet—a little unsteadily, it must be said—and motioned us back inside to choose what else we wanted. We spent the next ten minutes delighting over various items, which were soon added to a basket. We chose pewter beer mugs, a toaster (the old type where the sides fall away to extract the toast), and John, figuring Dad would like a small telescope, threw it into the basket. There were also some silver spoons, a cake tin, and a green bath towel. These items were loaded into the car with us in the back seat, and Mr X drove us back to the hotel, where a worried Miriam was waiting with Jacqui. We hopped out of the car with all our spoils and said thank you and goodbye to Mr X, who smiled and said he had thoroughly enjoyed our company. He climbed into his flashy car and drove off slowly. Jacqui and John played with the tape recorder and made up stories requiring lots of sound effects, while we carried the rest of the things into our hotel room. The next day, around noon, Mum and Dad arrived back from Fort Portal. We greeted them excitedly and told them that we had met a man who had taken us to his big house and given us lots of presents. Mum’s eyebrows shot straight into her hairline. ‘Which man?’ she asked with a frown, and I told her all about Mr X, the lunch, and the mountain of gifts. Both parents walked into the hotel room and stared at the pile of things on the floor, unable to believe their eyes. ‘You both know that you must never go anywhere with a stranger,’ admonished Dad, and Mum told him that they would have to find out who he was and take everything back immediately. The following day, we were all sitting outside near the pool when I spotted Mr X. I ran over to him and dragged him back to meet my parents. ‘This is the man we were telling you about,’ I said, pushing him forward. Mum and Dad got up from their deckchairs and introduced themselves. It turned out Mr X was the local bank manager. It later transpired that he had recently lost his wife and was very lonely. He actually invited all of us to his home again and despite many protests from the folks, we all ended up going. Mum told us to bring back all the items we had been given. John was thoroughly unhappy about giving back the tape recorder, but Mr X insisted he keep it. The folks relented but remained firm that everything else had to go back. I often imagine this story could have had a rather different ending, instead of being about a kind, lonely man battling with the grief of his wife's death and the ensuing loneliness. Thank goodness it turned out well. We kept that tape recorder for many years. John and Jacqui made up what would today be considered a type of podcast involving two men, ‘Jim and Jed’, and their many adventures, which were embellished using an array of sound effects including cutlery, crumpled paper, a small kitchen bell, and whatever else they could find. It provided hours of entertainment, both for Jacqui and John and for the rest of us listening to their stories. 

The Dinner Party

Dad was the Chief Medical Officer in Kampala, and as such, he was in charge of welcoming visiting medical dignitaries to Uganda, among various other functions. This meant that Mum had to entertain guests for dinner on occasion. One such occasion arose when two professors—one from the UK and the other from the USA—were visiting, and Dad invited them for supper. Mum had also invited another couple who were medical doctors, alongside a friend who was the life and soul of any party and an excellent raconteur. Mum carefully planned the menu: a starter of fresh pawpaw from the garden with mango slices and lemon, followed by a roast chicken dish, rice, and vegetables. She thought the coup de grâce would be the dessert: a baked Alaska. To end the meal, coffee would be served alongside a selection of bread and cheese. Primo, the cook, had been well versed in the menu and had practised the dishes beforehand. The day arrived and the house was a flurry of activity. Primo had cleaned and polished, and Mary had kept us children out of the way (I was six and John was five years old at this stage). Dad was in charge of the drinks, so he popped into Bhimji’s for beers and whisky. He knew there was a bottle of gin and some tonic water at home, so he figured that was a good enough selection for his international guests. We children were fed early, bathed, and tucked into bed by 7 p.m., shortly before the guests arrived. Mum was devastated to find that the ‘life and soul’ friend was suffering from laryngitis and had lost his voice almost entirely. The visiting professors and their wives arrived soon afterwards, along with their friends, and Dad was tasked with seeing to their drink requests. He took the orders and eventually came to the rather snooty wife of one of the professors. ‘And what can I get you to drink, Sandra?’ he asked. ‘We have most things.’ ‘I only drink sherry,’ she replied with a sniff. ‘Sherry?’ Dad repeated, hoping he had misheard. She nodded. ‘Certainly, one sherry coming up,’ Dad smiled, although he knew full well we didn’t have any, and backed away, John Cleese-style, into the kitchen where Mum was trying to get the starters ready. She was thoroughly stressed. ‘All the bloody pawpaws are barely ripe!’ she muttered. ‘They’re only just soft enough to serve!’ Dad told her he was popping out quickly to Bhimji’s for some sherry. ‘Why don’t you just say we don’t have any?’ asked Mum reasonably. She had to finish off in the kitchen and was hoping Dad would entertain everyone in the meantime— especially as their star guest couldn’t speak. ‘I already told her we have some,’ said Dad, ‘and she said she doesn’t drink anything else.’ He promised Mum he would only be five minutes. Mum told Primo to keep cutting the pawpaw, plate them with the mango, and bring them out to the table. She then hurried back into the lounge where the guests were standing around looking vaguely uncomfortable. ‘Please, sit down,’ Mum gushed, running around to ask what everyone wanted to drink. ‘I think Ivan is getting the drinks,’ said one of the wives. ‘Where is he?’ ‘He’s in the shed,’ Mum improvised quickly, although they must all have heard the noisy car engine start up and shoot off ten minutes earlier. ‘Why is he in the shed?’ asked their friend, Chris, somewhat reasonably. ‘He, um... he’s fetching the sherry.’ Mum was desperate but could see the collective puzzlement on their faces. ‘We like to keep our sherry out there to keep it cool,’ she finished defiantly, before marching off to find the beer glasses. She returned with the beers to find an awkward silence, so she started to chatter inanely about anything that came to mind—which mysteriously included the random fact that crickets all have unique chirps. Everyone gazed at her with puzzled expressions. ‘I heard recently that crickets chirp by rubbing their wings together,’ she continued, placing the frothing beer mugs on the small corner tables. She heard a chuckle and noticed her friend trying not to laugh. Her friend raised an eyebrow at Mum, who just frowned quickly back. ‘Let me go and see what’s keeping Ivan so long in the shed,’ she trilled desperately, running back into the kitchen where Primo was finishing plating the slices of fruit and lemon. ‘Where on earth is Ivan?’ she muttered under her breath. She scooped up several plates and together with Primo, set them down in the dining room just as she heard the familiar sound of the Ford screeching to a halt outside. Dad appeared a few minutes later with a bottle of sherry and brought a small glass over to Sandra. ‘Ah, heard you were in the shed,’ said Chris with a twinkle in his eye. ‘The shed?’ said Dad, puzzled. Mum appeared and laughed gaily. ‘You know, where we keep the sherry, silly,’ she said quickly. ‘Please, come through to the dining room table,’ she continued, before Dad could say another word. Everyone trooped into the next room where Mum had spent ages putting out glasses and fresh flowers from the garden. ‘Please start,’ she said, before hurrying back into the kitchen to tell Primo to boil the Brussels sprouts and baste the roast chicken. She returned to find the table mostly silent as everyone grappled with the barely ripened fruit. One of the professors dug his spoon firmly into the resisting pawpaw and the entire wedge shot off his plate, landing squarely on the floor. Mum apologised profusely and rushed out to find another piece that was a little softer. Fortunately, Dad was holding court with one of his stories, and she relaxed a little. He poured more beer and whisky for everyone and the conversation began to flow a little easier. Mum had acquired a little brass bell and had instructed Primo to enter the room whenever he heard it. She picked it up now and shook it thoroughly. It was a few minutes before Primo registered the meaning but he suddenly appeared in the doorway. ‘Memsahib is calling me?’ ‘Yes, Primo. Please clear away the plates and bring the chicken through the hatch,’ she requested. ‘The hatch?’ repeated Primo. ‘Yes,’ said Mum, pointing to the serving hatch that opened between the kitchen and the dining room. ‘Bring the chicken through there.’ He stared at her for a moment then shrugged. He knew Europeans had strange ways of doing things so who was he to argue? Conversation continued until a strange scratching sound arose. Everyone turned to see the small wooden hatch opening. A scuffling sound followed. All eyes fixed on the opening and the guests stared in absolute fascination as a leg suddenly appeared in the small space, followed closely by an arm—at the end of which was a large platter of roast chicken. A muffled voice came through the opening, to the effect that Memsahib needed to come quickly because he was stuck. Mum could not believe her eyes. She rushed over to Primo, who was firmly wedged between the kitchen and the serving hatch. He was standing on one leg on the kitchen side, with the other now firmly ensconced in the small opening alongside his right hand which was still clutching the roast chicken. ‘What on earth are you doing?’ she whispered, trying to grab the teetering dish before it dropped. ‘Memsahib, you tell me, Primo, to serve the kitchen through the hatch!’ He always got his kitchen and his chickens mixed up. Mum exclaimed in exasperation and tried to free him while the interested audience stared in fascination. ‘Is this how they do things in Africa?’ asked the professor from the UK, barely containing his laughter. 
‘ Ah it’s not his fault,’ replied mum,’ He’s still learning the mysterious ways of the wazungu.’ Order and the roast chicken restored, Mum brought in the rice, Brussels sprouts, and carrots. She had lost her appetite completely at this point and was just wishing for the night to end. Mercifully, the conversation turned to cricket scores. A few minutes later, Mum noticed the salt shaker was empty and rang the little bell again. Primo entered, and Mum asked him to fill the salt cellar. He took the empty container into the kitchen. About ten minutes later, Mum realised he still hadn’t returned and excused herself to find out why it was taking so long. She entered the kitchen and found Primo carefully forcing salt through the tiny holes on the top of the cellar, a few granules at a time. Mum took the container from him and showed him the opening at the bottom. ‘This is where you fill it,’ she explained gently. It wasn’t his fault; he kept a large bag of salt in his quarters and just sprinkled it directly onto his own food. He didn’t understand the wazungu's need to use complex receptacles any more than he understood why food had to be served through a hole in the wall when there was a perfectly suitable door right next to it. The dishes were cleared and the talk turned to the political state of the country, so Mum took the opportunity to return to the kitchen and put the finishing touches to the dessert. She had baked the cake base, rounded a ball of ice cream, whipped up the meringue into stiff peaks and covered the ice cream with a thick, protective layer of meringue. She asked Primo to put the pudding into the oven for ten minutes and returned to the guests, who were in deep discussion about some new medical procedure. Mum took a healthy swig of her beer and glanced at her watch. Eleven minutes had passed so the dessert should be ready. Before she could even vacate her chair there was a sudden explosion and a loud exclamation in Swahili from the kitchen. She rushed in to find the oven door open and the entire baked Alaska splattered all over the interior. Primo had used her Aunt Hannah’s antique crystal glass bowl and it had exploded under the intense heat. She and Primo spent the next ten minutes frantically scooping a mixture of broken crystal, cake, meringue, and fast-melting ice cream through a sieve to ensure there was no broken glass, before transferring it into dessert bowls where she added some whipped cream to mask the unidentifiable mass. She and Primo re-entered the dining room with trays of dessert. Mum’s cheeks were flushed, her hair mussed and there was a large smudge of soot on her face. ‘A small issue with a broken dish,’ she explained brightly. ‘Tuck in, everyone!’ She then spent the rest of the course watching everyone’s mouths intently for signs of bleeding. By now, she was exhausted and praying for the evening to end. There had been some disagreement between the professors and they were arguing loudly, fuelled by excess booze as Dad had continued to refill their glasses in a desperate attempt to distract them from the mini-disasters occurring throughout the evening. Eventually, everyone left the dining room for the lounge. A little later, Mum asked who would like coffee and took the order back to the kitchen. Earlier in the day, she had bought a large, crusty loaf of bread from the bakery and placed it on a tray next to a huge round of Edam cheese, still encased in its red wax coating. The coffee was poured and taken through to everyone and Mum announced that bread and cheese were available in the dining room for anyone who would like some. She added with a chuckle that she doubted anything else could possibly go wrong. The professor from the US and Chris got up and headed towards the dining room. They returned a moment later and the Chris was laughing so hard that tears were streaming down his cheeks. ‘What is it?’ asked Dad, while Mum looked on fearfully. ‘Um,’ he gasped between fits of laughter, ‘there’s no bread or cheese!’ He laughed again while Mum looked perplexed. ‘But I put it out there half an hour ago,’ she said. Everyone was mystified and trooped back into the dining room. On the table lay what appeared to be a large, crusty loaf and an equally large red circle of cheese. But when Mum lifted the bread, the entire centre had been hollowed out; only the outer crust remained. She picked up the red wax cheese casing—it too was completely hollow! Everyone was laughing now. ‘I think I know who the culprits are,’ said Mum laughing weakly. They followed her to one of the bedrooms where she switched on the light. Each single bed had a tent made of blankets, illuminated by torchlight. At the sound of laughter and light, two small heads emerged from the blankets. Earlier in the evening, John and I had crept into the dining room, scooped out the inside of the bread and cheese and were now busy making pellets of the fresh dough, which we were eating with large chunks of Edam. Later, Dad received a telephone call from the professor from the UK. He said he had never enjoyed a dinner party more, nor laughed as much— it was quite a while before Mum hosted another party though. 

The Plane Drama 

As school children, we flew on the ‘Lollipop Special’ to and from Uganda and Ireland where we attended boarding school. John was at the Royal School in Armagh, Jacqui and I were in Londonderry at first. We later transferred during ‘The Troubles’ to the Cross and Passion Convent in Ballycastle, so we had some experience of both the Protestant and Catholic systems. The year was 1969 and we were travelling home for the Christmas holidays. We were aboard a small BEA Trident jet heading for London where we had a connecting flight to Nairobi. Our Irish friends, the Rogers family from another school, were also on board though seated further down the cabin. John, Jacqui and I sat right at the back. We took off from Aldergrove Airport in Belfast and were only about fifteen minutes into the flight when I heard the air hostesses talking in the galley area behind me. One whispered to the other that there was a serious problem. Her companion asked what it was and I strained my ears to hear; if a problem existed whilst we were ensconced in an enclosed capsule some 36,000 feet above earth, I had a right to know about it. ‘There’s a fire in one of the engines,’ the first attendant whispered and I sat up in shock. ‘We must keep the passengers calm,’ her companion continued in an urgent whisper. ‘John!’ I hissed. ‘The aeroplane is on fire!’ I was (and by all accounts, still am) prone to a degree of embellishment.

‘Oh, brilliant!’ said John. ‘I’ve never been in a plane crash before!’ He was in the middle of chopping up a bar of chocolate using the small plastic tray-table on the seat in front. I glared at him while Jacqui looked significantly paler than usual. ‘Are we really going to crash?’ she asked with fitting alarm. ‘I don’t know,’ I whispered back, ‘but the air hostesses said they need to keep us all calm.’ The seatbelt sign was still illuminated when the pilot’s voice came over the PA system. He announced that we were experiencing a few ‘technical difficulties’ and were turning back to Aldergrove Airport. He said all the usual reassuring things like, ‘Nothing to be concerned about,’ but the plane was now flying so fast that everything inside the cabin rattled. The noise was deafening and I worried that the aircraft would disintegrate in mid-air before any fire had a chance to do damage. Some people were whimpering loudly, fingers white-knuckled over the armrests. Five minutes later the PA crackled to life again. This time without any attempt at placation we were told to assume the brace position. I did so immediately, but glanced quickly at my siblings. Jacqui was bracing, but John was just gazing out of the window. I reached out, smacked him on the head and hissed at him to brace. The plane seemed to pick up even more speed, the roar of the engines deafening. Suddenly, a massive bump declared that we had landed—not on the runway, but in a muddy cow field close to the airport. Despite nothing being officially said about a fire, I knew that had to be the reason we had landed so abruptly, that and the faint but unmistakable smell of smoke drifting through the cabin. The pilot switched on the PA again and ordered us to remove our high heels and evacuate immediately via the emergency chutes, leaving all hand luggage behind. Jacqui, however, was having none of it. ‘I can’t find my gloves!’ she panicked, scrabbling under her seat. ‘What will Miss Christie say if I go back to school without my gloves?’ ‘I think we just have to go!’ I said, grabbing my drawing book and looking around for John. He was ahead of me and turned back with a grin. ‘I’m going down backwards!’ I felt my responsibilities as the eldest sibling slipping away due to sheer insubordination. Luckily, one of the air hostesses who was rushing people off the plane towards the exits, ran up to us and told us to hurry. John went down headfirst as promised. Jacqui folded her arms in front of her as demonstrated and slid down and I followed suit, landing in the soft grass at the bottom. I stood up and noticed a fleet of ambulances (ten of them, I heard later) and two fire engines. The firemen had unravelled their hoses and were aiming them at the fuselage. Passengers were running away from the aircraft as fast as they could towards a waiting bus. A strong smell of smoke hung in the air, but I didn't see any open flames—mind you, I wasn’t really looking. Everything seemed to happen in slow motion. Once on board the waiting bus, some passengers were coughing, others had suffered nylon rope burns from the chute, but thankfully no one was badly injured. Eventually everyone was accounted for and the bus drove quickly towards the terminal with forty passengers, three pilots and four cabin crew. The passengers stood in the airport lounge in a state of shock. Not long afterwards, a man in an airport uniform told us to head to the airport restaurant where we would be provided with a ‘hot supper and a brandy’. Our school friends joined us and chattered excitedly about the emergency evacuation. ‘I wonder what actually happened?’ asked Steve. I told him I’d overheard them saying the engine caught fire. As we followed the others towards the restaurant, a reporter asked if he could take photographs. The reporter fiddled with his camera and asked what had happened. I recounted what I had heard on board, adding that we were supposed to be in Uganda by 7 a.m. tomorrow morning and if we flew out later, it would shorten our holiday time. John chimed in, ‘It’s not fair!’ as though the airline had deliberately designed the mechanical failure to spite him. We were photographed looking suitably tragic after the reporter told us to stop smiling. Naturally, we were all misquoted when the front page of the newspaper came out the next morning under the headline: Plane Drama at Belfast Airport. To add insult to injury, John was named ‘Helen’ in the caption, which annoyed him intensely. After a steak supper affected passengers were put up for the night at the airport hotel. I was worried about Mum and Dad waiting for us in Uganda but the cabin crew reassured us they would be informed. The next day, the airline flew us first class to London to catch our connection. The online meal was served with real silver cutlery which I had to stop John from pocketing. ‘It’s a Christmas present for Mum,’ he protested. ‘She always needs more knives and things.’ I mentioned that while it might be a nice present for her, to the airline, it was simply theft, so he reluctantly replaced the cutlery. We arrived at Heathrow to a freezing cold London. Our grandmother, whom we called Nanny, was there smoking two cigarettes at once in her anxiety. She was always a calming, familiar presence during our many trips to and from boarding school and Uganda. She wrapped each of us in a perfumed hug and muttered something about having another cup of tea. We noticed several newspapers on a stand with our faces on the front page and went over to have a look. Nanny bought two copies—one to give to the folks back home. Later in the New Year, John told me that the headmaster at the Royal School Armagh had read the news. Seeing the photograph, he’d looked up with a frown and asked, 'Where is Crowley’s tie?’ 

Queen Elizabeth National Park

It was a family outing. We were on our way to the Queen Elizabeth National Park in the western region of Uganda. After packing up the old Ford, Dad shouted, ‘Right, men, into your boxes!’ which was the signal for us to scramble into our seats. Nanny (Mum had stayed at home) sat in the front while Dad drove and the three of us siblings squashed into the back. The drive took a few hours and we squabbled the entire way over who got the window seat. After paying the entrance fee, game viewing commenced immediately and soon a herd of elephants were spotted followed by Uganda kob, waterbuck, topi and warthogs. At midday we stopped off for lunch at the park’s restaurant. After a meal of waterbuck steak and vegetables we set off for another game drive. This time we saw a troop of vervet monkeys and a waterbuck with its distinctive ‘wet paint’ circle of white around its bottom. It was around 5 p.m. when Dad let out an expletive. ‘Blast!’ he announced. ‘We’ve got a puncture.’ ‘Bale out, men,’ he instructed stepping out to take a look. A large white thorn from a polyacantha tree stuck straight out of the tyre. A quick look in the boot confirmed Dad’s worst suspicions. ‘We don’t have a spare!’ he announced to the assembled group who were staying as close to the vehicle as possible so as not to be eaten by a strolling leopard. The puncture had left a huge hole. Dad removed the wheel and took the inner tube out. He gazed at it for a minute then turned to look at me. ‘Anyone have a needle?’ he asked hopefully. He knew I was the family first-aider—not just because I always travelled with a bandage and plasters, but because I also tended to pack a sewing kit, drawing materials, a drawing pad, and a few bananas into my bag, no matter how long or short the journey. I fumbled around and triumphantly emerged with a darning needle. ‘What are you going to do?’ I asked, handing him the needle with a length of white thread already attached (I was nothing if not efficient). Everyone else wanted to know too. We were in a remote area of the park and might not be discovered for hours. Nanny had nervously lit her third cigarette and kept a sharp eye out for wild animals which, given our circumstances, was a very real possibility. ‘Help me collect some dry grass, men,’ said Dad. ‘then you’ll see what I have in mind.’ John and I ran around the car collecting mounds of dry, yellow grass, dumping it at Dad's feet. He knelt down and started to stuff the inner tube with it. 'Need more!’ he shouted, and Jacqui carried a fresh pile over in a toy bucket. ‘Are you sure this is going to work, Ivan?’ asked Nanny, inhaling deeply on her umpteenth cigarette and clicking her false teeth. ‘No idea,’ came the reassuring reply, ‘but there’s only one way to find out!’ Dad slid the grass-stuffed inner tube back inside the outer rubber tyre, fitted it onto the hub and tightened the nuts. ‘I’m hoping it’ll get us to the main camp, which is about half an hour away, I think,’ he said, slowly lowering the jack. After climbing carefully back into the car Dad started the motor and we juddered off down the dirt road. He drove incredibly slowly and asked John to lean out of the window to check the wheel every now and again. It actually held until we were about a kilometre away from the campsite. It’d been slowly losing its stuffing and was now completely flat. However we were close enough to the campsite to hope another vehicle would soon pass and sat waiting by the side of the road for about twenty minutes while Dad stood close to his open door. Finally, he spotted a van approaching, flagged it down and explained our predicament. A short Asian man emerged from the van, placing his hands on his rotund hips as he assessed the situation. He nodded and told Dad that while his van was packed high with medical supplies, he and his brother, Rajesh, were heading back to Fort Portal where we lived. He said he could give us a lift home. Dad beamed and turned to us. ‘Okay, men. Mater B, you can sit inside with Mr Patel.’ ‘Mater B’ was the nickname Dad had given Nanny, derived from her surname; Bellis. She lit up another cigarette and asked what we all considered to be a fair and valid question: ‘Thank you Ivan, but what about the rest of you?’ We all turned to Dad, waiting to hear his solution. Dad simply pointed to the roof of the van which had a short metal covering. 'We can all sit on the roof rack,' he said, as though it were the most obvious solution— which I suppose it was, given that the alternative was being stuck in the park overnight. One by one we followed Rajesh and dad onto the roof. He sat cross-legged at the back. Dad joined him, and then the three of us kids settled in. I ended up right at the front clinging tightly to the low metal frame as Mr Patel set off. It suddenly became pitch dark, as is typical at the equator and we all clung onto the sides for dear life as Mr Patel found the tarmac road outside the park and accelerated. As we sat swaying with every movement of the van, I heard the sound of singing. I turned around and saw that Rajesh had started praying in a loud, sonorous voice. His eyes were closed, sitting cross-legged with his hands raised in supplication towards the velvet night sky. I glanced down at the front window below and saw the familiar glow of another cigarette being lit. We made it home by 11 p.m. and Dad gave Mr Patel a handful of shillings for petrol. Mum was waiting for us outside on the verandah where she had been anxiously sitting for hours. ‘You won’t believe what happened!’ John said excitedly. By this time, Mum had been married to Dad long enough to believe absolutely anything. 

Tax Issues 

Our time in Uganda was fast coming to an end; life under Idi Amin’s military dictatorship had become untenable. The Asian community—the successful business owners and entrepreneurs of the country—received ninety days' notice to leave. Soldiers were everywhere and impromptu military checkpoints popped up constantly. Mum was interrogated one morning after shopping and the contents of her boot (trunk) trampled on or stolen by Amin’s gun-wielding henchmen. You could feel the tension everywhere. In preparation for our final exodus, my parents had sent all but the most necessary possessions to the UK in several large shipping crates. Since most of the remaining furniture was provided by the PWD, we still had our beds, tables and chairs. Talk at the club turned exclusively to the madness of Idi Amin. Most of the expat community—comprising British, Irish, Italian, Polish, and of course, the Asians—frantically made plans. ‘You have to go to the tax offices in Kampala,’ warned one. ‘And show them all your tax certificates as proof that you’re up to date before you can even think of leaving,’ added another. ‘Did you hear he said he’ll rename the country "Idi", after himself?’ said a third. ‘Until someone pointed out that its inhabitants would then be known as "Idiots",’ guffawed another. The laughter was uneasy. The day approached when everything was supposedly in order. My parents took off for Kampala from Masaka, a 136 kilometre drive that took nearly three hours. We children were left in the capable hands of our loyal staff. Mum later told us the story. They arrived with an hour or so to spare before the tax office closed and entered the bare room, clutching various bits of paper. Dad told the official over the long, chipped Formica counter that he and his family would be leaving the country by the end of the week, thrusting his file of tax documents at him. The official picked up a chewed pencil (pens were always in short supply and highly coveted) and laboriously read through the file. He closed it with a snap and asked, ‘Where are the tax documents for 1968?’ Dad’s brow creased. ‘Isn’t it there? I definitely paid!’ The official shook his head. ‘I can’t let you leave Uganda until I have that document,’ he said firmly. Mum interrupted. ‘Can’t you look in your files?’ she asked hopefully. He reluctantly admitted that he could and started rifling through a few of the hundreds of files in the open cupboard behind him. He worked agonisingly slowly. Mum and Dad sighed in exasperation. An hour later he leaned over the counter, glanced at the wall clock and said he couldn't locate our file. It was almost closing time. ‘So what can we do?’ asked Dad. ‘I have definitely paid all my taxes.’ The official leaned in closer and murmured that he could produce a stamped document to the effect that we were paid up, but it would cost thousands of Uganda shillings. Mum’s bladder had been filling up over the two hours since their arrival and her anxiety was making it worse. ‘Please, may I use your bathroom?’ she begged, almost in tears at the thought of having to pay such an enormous fine—or bribe, as it might more accurately be called. He pointed behind him with his pencil and said the room on the right had a toilet. Mum hurried into the bathroom and found the small room stacked from floor to ceiling with folders. Sitting on the toilet, she idly flicked through a few of them. She could scarcely believe her luck; here were all the missing tax files;  the name of each resident written in bold on the side. She began laboriously flipping through them, one by one, moving from those beside the basin to the far corner. After twenty minutes of frantic searching, she came to a file with the name Ivan Crowley and family written in black pen. With trembling fingers she opened it and searched through the pages until she found what she was looking for: the official tax receipt for 1968. She ran back into the office where Dad had been sitting dejectedly, awaiting her return. ‘What took you so long?’ he asked. ‘I was about to call a search party.’ He stopped when he saw what Mum was holding. And that, folks, is how we were allowed to leave Uganda. 

Selling the Car

Most of the possessions we were leaving behind had been sold, except for the family car. Dad put up a notice on the hospital noticeboard: For Sale! 1962 Model Ford Escort in good running condition. He may have been a little economical with the truth there; he’d bought the car third-hand and it had given trouble one way or another ever since. Later that afternoon, Dad received a telephone call from an Asian gentleman and they arranged to meet at the Speke Hotel the following day around midday so he could take the car for a test drive. My parents had made some wonderful friends over the years. They arranged a final get-together at the hotel for drinks and snacks that same day. The afternoon arrived with clear blue skies and a pleasant temperature of around 26°C. Everyone gathered on the wide, red-painted verandah of the hotel and soon there were children running up, down and around the pool while their parents quietly (or not so quietly) got sozzled. Sometime around midday, Dad was approached by an Asian gentleman dressed neatly in a sharp shirt and tie. ‘Dr Crowley?’ he asked. Dad nodded and not wanting to waste another minute, stood up and led the man outside, 
‘Let’s do business at the car.’ At the vehicle, the man turned to Dad. ‘Doctor, can you look at this,’ he asked, thrusting a document under Dad’s nose. ‘No, no, no, my good man. Paperwork afterwards. Let’s take a test drive first,’ Dad replied. The man followed Dad haltingly as Dad urged him towards the driver's door. ‘You want me to drive, Doctor?’ he asked with a puzzled expression. ‘Of course!’ said Dad. ‘How else are you going to know what my car can do?’ The man shrugged, gave a little sideways nod and tried the door handle, but it wouldn’t budge. Dad called out from the other side, ‘Ah, that handle sticks a bit. You have to climb in from the passenger seat.’ The man dutifully edged around the car and shuffled across into the driver’s seat. ‘Okay, start the engine,’ instructed Dad. ‘Use plenty of throttle.’ The car roared to life, juddering violently along as the man tried to get used to the clutch. ‘Drive up the hill,’ Dad instructed. ‘Put lots of juice on the accelerator!’ The man’s top lip was beaded with sweat as he managed to coax the juddering car up a steep incline. Suddenly, there was a violent lurch and the car swerved. Dad looked out of his window as the car came to an abrupt halt. ‘Damn! I think we’ve got a puncture,’ he said, throwing open his door. ‘Look, old fellow, would you mind walking down to the petrol station at the bottom of the road and ask them for a new tyre? In the meantime, I’ll get the car jacked up.’ The man tried to object, pulling a piece of paper from his pocket to show Dad once more. ‘Dr Crowley, I want you to—’ Dad waved it away. ‘Later, my dear fellow. Let’s get this car back on the road so you can see what she can do.’ The man reluctantly removed his jacket and tie and marched down the hill, while Dad found the jack and started to raise the chassis. He hoped these mishaps hadn’t scared the man off and that he’d still want to buy the car, vowing to bring the asking price down a little if the fellow seemed uncertain. Twenty minutes later, the man puffed his way back up the hill carrying a retread tyre. He arrived glowing with sweat, dirt lining his eyes giving him a distinctly owlish appearance. He and Dad silently manoeuvred the old tyre off and replaced it with the new one. By the end of it, they were both more than a little dirty and shiny with perspiration. Dad stood up and asked, ‘Do you want to drive again?’ The man shook his head vehemently, so Dad slid into the driver’s seat from the passenger side while the man climbed in after him. Because of the difference in their height, the man twisted around and tried to adjust the front seat mechanism. The catch gave way and he went flying backwards straight into the back seat. ‘Ah, I meant to tell you old chap, but you were too quick,’ beamed Dad. ‘That’s the ejector seat. Well, you may as well stay there and I’ll drive us back to the hotel.’ The man just nodded miserably from the back seat and tried to smooth his ruffled hair. They came to a juddering stop in the hotel car park and Dad turned to the man, who was now sitting pretty much on the back seat. ‘What do you think of the car?’ he asked turning to the oil-smudged fellow who was clutching the leather strap and looking rather frightened. ‘The car?’ He asked, looking puzzled. 
‘Yes, do you like it?’ Dad asked. ‘It’s a very nice car, Doctor,’ he replied and Dad smiled happily, sure he would buy it now despite its little issues. He assisted him in escaping from the back seat and turned to him. ‘So, will you be buying the car?’ 
‘Take the car? No, I don’t want your car. I’m not here for that,’ he said with determination. ‘I don’t understand, aren’t you Mr Singh who phoned me yesterday?’ asked Dad, his brow furrowing in puzzlement. ‘No, Doctor, I have been trying to tell you,’ he said, thrusting the document at Dad again. ‘You treated my mother, Mrs Patel, in the intensive care unit last week—I just need your signature for her medical insurance.’ Dad took the crumpled form and read it slowly. ‘So you didn’t come to buy the car?’ he asked again hopefully. Mr Patel shook his head firmly. Dad sighed, signed the document and handed it back. ‘I was told that you would be here by your secretary,’ said Mr Patel, backing away. ‘Goodbye, Doctor.’ He almost ran back into the hotel. Dad started to laugh and he was still laughing when he rejoined the merry party sitting on the verandah. He immediately noticed another Asian gentleman sitting quietly in their midst, holding a beer and wearing a perplexed expression. ‘You must be Mr Singh, come to see the car,’ said Dad, and the man nodded a little uncertainly. ‘Well, here are the keys,’ Dad said, handing the bunch over to him. ‘Look for the blue Ford Escort outside. You can test drive it yourself and come and tell me if you want it.’ The man, looking surprised, walked slowly towards the car park. ‘It has one brand-new tyre!’ Dad shouted after him, before sitting back down with his friends and taking a huge slurp of beer. 

During the ten years we lived in Uganda, we moved twelve times and attended ten different schools; my siblings and I were getting used to always being the new kids. This was mainly due to Dad’s contract which was renewed every two years, but also subject to being moved wherever an urgent need arose for his medical services. My poor Mum had to pack up an entire household each time. A week after Dad finally sold the car, we paid off our wonderful staff who had travelled with us over the years: Primo, our chief cook and ‘houseboy’ and Mary, our beloved maid and Primo’s wife, who was always known to us as Mama ya Primo. They all had tears in their eyes. Mum managed to find them alternative work with those who were staying on in the country. They were like family to us and we cried and hugged them tightly. The plan now was to return to the UK for a short while before flying to Ireland where Dad would undertake a locum position. In the meantime, we were waiting for an official letter—the ‘buff envelope,’ as Dad referred to it—that would allow us to travel to South Africa to start a completely new life. 

My time in Uganda certainly shaped me during my formative years. I learned to adapt to new situations; always being the new girl in class meant I needed to fit in and catch up quickly and to find humour in situations that may have seemed far from humorous at the time. I learned to speak Swahili and to find similarities in different cultures whilst accepting their differences. I managed to make at least one good friend and over the years they included an American, a Ugandan, an Indian, and an African-American girl.
