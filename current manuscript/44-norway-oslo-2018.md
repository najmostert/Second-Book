# Chapter 45: Norway: Oslo 2018

Tim was beginning his life as a digital nomad, and we were farewelled at the airport by a large group, including Col and his partner, Carrie. I babbled inanely to fill the silences, my eyes swivelling between everyone like a demented robot. Eventually, the boarding call came, and we squeezed onto a packed flight. I spent the journey eating like a T-Rex because I was so tightly sandwiched, and at 3 a.m. we were served a truly revolting meal of baked beans, sausage, and powdered eggs. After a brief, sweltering stopover in Dubai—where we visited the Burj Khalifa and the aquarium—we arrived in Oslo to see mountains covered in melting snow. We took a fast, clean train to Lillestrøm, then hiked to a tall grey building in Enerhaugen. Our host, Øyvind, introduced us to his young son, who was mentally challenged and partially blind. It was a poignant, if slightly overwhelming, welcome. Oslo was fresh and sunny. We explored the ruins of the 12th-century St. Clement’s Church, which helped archaeologists determine that Oslo was founded about 1,000 years ago. The architecture in the Bjørvika district was fascinating—buildings with orange, diagonal, and top-heavy window designs. I also visited the National Art Museum, where I finally saw Munch’s The Scream. It was smaller than I expected, but no less haunting. We finished the day with a meal at Rorbua, a nautical-themed restaurant. Tim tried Minke whale meat—which he found ‘gamey’—while I stuck to salmon. I also tried the ‘Veiled Lofoten Girls’ dessert, a bizarre but tasty concoction of stewed rhubarb, toasted breadcrumbs, and whipped cream. We spent our final days visiting the Viking Ship Museum, the outdoor Norwegian Museum of Cultural History (with its impressive 1500s stave church), and the Holmenkollen ski jump, where we played in the melting snow and pelted each other with snowballs. 

Sweden: Stockholm 

We arrived in Stockholm and headed to Sturegatan to stay with Anne-Sofie. The city was beautiful, with cartoon-like clouds hanging in a bright blue sky. We visited a paper-art exhibition called Paper Stories and then met Anne-Sofie at the marketplace, which was overflowing with vibrant, plump strawberries, yellow bananas, and red apples. A highlight was the Stadshuset (City Hall), completed in 1923. It features stunning mosaics, stained glass, and a spectacular marble staircase. Anne-Sofie, who had attended a Nobel Prize dinner there, shared stories of the lavish events and the esteemed guests she had met. We wandered the cobbled streets of Gamla Stan (the Old Town) just in time to see the changing of the guard, featuring soldiers in light blue and gold jackets atop beautifully turned-out white horses. For lunch, I tried blood pudding with lingonberries and apple—surprisingly delicious! We finished our visit at Kungsträdgården, where the cherry blossoms were in full bloom, though the massive crowds made photography a challenge. Before we left, we squeezed through one of the narrowest streets I have ever seen: a flight of stone steps barely a person’s width wide, tucked between two towering buildings. Anne-Sofie and I walked into town, where the cherry blossoms were in full bloom, their glorious pink petals unfurled by the warmth of the sun. We were just in time to see the parade in honour of King Carl XVI Gustaf’s 73rd birthday. We arrived a bit late for the start, but we managed to see the Royal family on the balcony: the King, Queen Silvia, and their daughter, Crown Princess Victoria. I craned my neck and asked who the lady in blue was, and a woman in front of me turned around, her eyes shining with excitement and pride, to tell me, ‘That’s our Princess, Victoria!’. Later that day, Annie arrived at 6 p.m., and we drove all the way out to Slotsallen in Solna to watch the ‘big bonfire’ for Valborgsmässoafton (Walpurgis Night). This tradition, held every year on April 30, marks the arrival of spring in Sweden. People gather around bonfires—traditionally believed to ward off evil spirits—to salute the new season with joyful singing. It was very cold once the sun had set, so we spent much of the time in the car to stay warm. Around 8:45 p.m., the local Scouts lit the bonfire. We eventually extricated ourselves from the warmth of the vehicle and wandered over to the fire. Though only one side of the pyre really caught, it was a lovely, warming sight, standing together in the dark and watching the dancing golden flames light up the night sky.
