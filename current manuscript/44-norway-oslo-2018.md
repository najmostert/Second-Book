# Chapter 45: Norway: Oslo 2018

Tim was to start his life as a digital nomad and wouldn’t be in South Africa for some time. An assortment of people were at the airport to see us off, including Col and his missus, Tim and girlfriend, Jo and I. The atmosphere was slightly awkward so I did that thing where I babble inanely to try and fill up the silences, making an effort to include everyone so my eyes swivelled across from Tim, to Jo and back again like some demented robot. I burbled on, took lots of group photos of unsmiling people and wished the call for boarding would be announced soon. At last, the tannoy asked those travelling on flight 567 to Norway to board now and we scrambled up, paid and hugged everyone goodbye.
Tim and I boarded half an hour later than scheduled and it was a very full plane. I was seated between Tim at the window and an old woman who looked annoyed at having to stand up to allow us to our seats. I envisioned what the rest of the flight would be like once I needed the loo a few times and it was not a pleasant thought. I was so sandwiched between them that I couldn’t put my arms on any of the armrests and consequently ate my meal with the tiny arms of a T-Rex.
The usual struggle to sleep ensued and I was trying to pace my need for the loo to avoid annoying my aisle companion. At 3 a.m. we were served a revolting meal of baked beans, sausage, and powdered eggs. I couldn’t imagine it was breakfast time anywhere in the world and nibbled on a cracker and watched a film until we landed.

## Dubai

The heat hit us full force as we walked into the airport in Dubai. As our connecting flight was only late the following day, we’d stay in Dubai for the night. In the airport toilet cubicle, I peeled off so many layers I was nearly naked in an attempt to get cooler. It was too early for check-in at the hotel so we set off in the intense, damp heat to explore with our heavy backpacks; laptops in situ. Unfortunately we’d just missed the bus, so had to walk to the train station into town. It was 42°C and a huge mistake to venture outside.
Tall buildings glowed in the heat. After walking through the relative coolth of the metro station to the Dubai Mall we came to the world’s highest building, the Burj Khalifa. It is 828 metres tall (2,217 feet). The structure looks like two giant cylinders joined near the top by a walkway. It shimmered silver in the bright morning light. Tim looked up a restaurant with good reviews at Fountain Square and we had a seafood meal – sushi and prawns with caviar! First time eating this symbol of wealth for me and probably the last.
Suddenly, the huge fountain started up a magnificent choreographed display to the sound of Eastern music. The fountain shoots water as high as 152 metres (500 feet). At night, lights add to the amazing spectacle. Dubai, as a place of records, proudly states that this fountain is also the highest-performing fountain — their superlatives outdo Texas in claiming to be the world's the highest, tallest, and biggest. The spray had the added advantage of cooling us down. After lunch we walked past infinity pools, magnificent lush gardens and every manner of shop. All the big names are there; Balenciaga, Gucci, Chanel, Dior and Louis Vuitton among others. Another water display claimed our attention, before heading back to the hotel. En route we spotted an aquarium, or ‘underwater zoo’ as there are over 140 species of aquatic animal there. As it was situated in the mall we decided to go in. It was good and aside from the usual suspects, we saw Asian otters, sharks, rays, penguins and huge tunnels of water overhead and on all sides, giving one the impression of just strolling through water. A huge, man-made steamy ‘jungle’ on the next floor housed colourful macaws that flew around us, big hairy rodents (capybaras), long-necked tortoises, a giant croc from Australia and many more. It was only mildly cooler walking back along the road to the hotel. After a quick shower, we visited the nearby ‘Mr Toad’s’ Bar and Grill for supper. They were having a ‘South Africa Day’ so all manner of braai (BBQ) meat was on offer, including wors, pap and bredies. After a very meaty/carby supper I trotted back to our room, thoroughly exhausted.

## Oslo

As we came in to land, I could see thick caps of snow over the mountains. The train into Oslo was fast, clean and fairly empty; a great experience. I gazed out of the window, watching semi-melting snow as we whizzed by until we got to Lillestrøm.
After a long walk, we came to a tall grey building in Enerhaugen. The host’s boyfriend met us after we huffed and puffed up four flights of stairs (there was no lift). He spoke fairly good English and introduced himself as Øyvind (sounded a bit like ‘Awfin’). As he was showing us around I heard a sudden wailing. He broke off and disappeared for a minute, returning with a thin, Mowgli-type boy of about five years old in his arms. He told us his son was mentally challenged and partially blind, which information I was just digesting when he  thrust him into my arms still wailing in a high-pitched, frantic way. I tried to calm him and Tim stroked his arm, but the wailing continued (probably because two strangers were stroking him frantically in an attempt to calm him down). I wasn’t too upset when he relieved me of the poor child. We entered our room which was accessed via the kitchen and the bathroom was a few doors down. After  choosing our respective bunks (there were three of them) we unpacked some essentials and I looked around. A wooden desk and chair sat near the doorway with plug outlets for charging. We had a shower each and set off to explore Oslo central.
Outside was fresh and sunny. We arrived at Clement’s Church and according to the ‘visitnorway’ website, the ruins of this medieval church were built in the 12th century. The church was torn down after about 400 years, and the ruins excavated in 1920–21 which helped archaeologists determine that Oslo was founded around 1,000 years ago; four times as old as the United States.
A strange, modern-art-style collection of buildings surrounds Bjørvika: top-heavy structures, a large wooden square with smaller squares at the top, orange windows set into a grey building, diagonal windows, and a building made almost entirely of glass.
I found this architecture interesting and took a few photos. I came across a small supermarket and emerged twenty minutes later with a giant black coconut yoghurt and a sausage and with this odd combination in our cold paws, we walked through Eidsvolls plass; a square and a park located west of the Parliament of Oslo building and the huge Grand Hotel with its distinctive green-painted turrets, past Oslo Cathedral with its clock tower and Grünerløkka Vest — over the Akerselva river.
After a good sleep I made us tea, unsuccessfully avoiding our host as he was in the kitchen as well, so a bit of small talk ensued whilst I willed the kettle to hurry up and boil. I was a little afraid he would disappear and return with little Mowgli — more for his sake as being thrust into a stranger’s arms didn’t seem one he enjoyed much yesterday. I met our Filipina hostess, a tiny, dark-haired woman who spoke English well but with overtones of an American accent that all foreigners seem to obtain when learning English. All her ‘f’s’ became ‘p’s’, so it was,
‘I hope everyone is preindly por you.’
Her Norwegian partner gave us some tips on what to see and where to go for some good food. As my dad would have said, we fruitled around a bit (i.e. engaged in small insignificant activities), before walking into the cold wind outside. I didn’t mind as I always feel more energised in cold weather and it had been awfully hot in Dubai. We walked about three kilometres into town through a small park (Tøyenparken) and past the Neo-Romanesque Grønland Church with its four spires and tall, monumental tower. Some weirdly placed model ‘scuba divers’ hung from an electricity line in town, giving macabre overtones to the small shopping centre we entered for coffee.
Irreverent seagulls perched on former King Karl Johan’s head, screeching loudly. We entered the National Art museum and found the famous ‘Scream’ by Munch, as well as paintings by Degas, Rodin’s statue ‘The Thinker’, (which is smaller than I thought), Renoir, Picasso and many more.
We drank another coffee at Cafe Sjakk Matt. They have a beautiful stained-glass window on one side of the room. Intricate designs within the glass are projected onto surfaces, casting dappled, mosaic-like shapes that ‘dance’ as the sun moved across the warm café interior.
At the harbour square at Rådhusplassen, a few bands were practising soft classical music, lending to the peaceful atmosphere as we observed several ships sail by — some were old wooden sailing boats with masts. Jamie Oliver’s restaurant stands close to the ocean front and we were in two minds whether to stop for supper but the sensible one decided it would be too expensive, so we walked over to Frogner to see the Modern Art gallery. I didn’t enjoy it as much as the museum. It had a lot of modern crap in it — all gender-bashing stuff which I’m not about as it’s giving pretentious vibes. A few Damien Hirst works made up for it — mostly of cows cut in half, but still. We'd seen his best work in Venice a few years ago.
Tim and I wanted to try local Norwegian food and found a place called Rorbua, which advertised authentic food. A wood and nautical theme persisted throughout and a lovely warm fire was lit. A pretty, blonde Norwegian girl served us wearing traditional dress; called a bunad — a costume based on 19th-century folk attire, characterised by a pretty blue and yellow embroidered wool design. I ordered salmon, while Tim asked for (Minke) whale meat and veg (Nordnorsk Fjøl). I wasn't exstatic about the whale, but Tim wanted to try something authentically Norwegian. They also served reindeer, moose and catfish. Dessert offered delicacies such as ‘Veiled Lofoten girls on Rhubarb!’The waitress explained that it’s served in transparent glasses, alternating between stewed, sweetened rhubarb, crunchy toasted breadcrumbs (often fried in butter with sugar, cinnamon) and whipped cream — the mind boggles!
I was watching an old Swedish couple at the next table as I munched on my ‘rhubarb girls’. They chatted in a desultory manner while sipping on several õl (beer) while munching on fried ‘fisk’ (fish). They ordered ice cream for dessert. The waitress accidentally dropped an ice cream ball, which Tim deftly caught on a plate mid tumble and returned it to them.
‘Tusen takk!’ smiled the old man.
Our meals were tasty and although I was glad I didn’t have the whale; I confess to having a bite of Tim’s. It has a purply-pink coloured flesh and was very ‘gamey’, definitely not worth killing in my opinion.
Afterwards we walked to the Nobel Peace Centre. It was nearly closing time, so had to whizz around. There’s a lot of war paraphernalia and laureates from South Africa such as Botha and Mandela.
I bought a large, brown knitted bag in the museum shop and filled in a form that asked what the most valuable thing I own is, to which I wrote, ‘my health and my son’ — except of course whilst I don't own him, he's still very valuable to me though so I figured it could stay there.
From there, we took our first Norwegian bus to the Opera House; an interesting, sloping-roofed building situated near the sea. The opera house opened in 2008 and was built as an extension of the idea that nature is free to enjoy. Whereas normally you are likely to be arrested if you walk on rooftops, this is encouraged here. We dutifully climbed onto the large sloping roof where I photographed birds, ships and our reflections at a 90° angle. After climbing up to our apartment, I saw the kitchen was free so made  tea, hardbrød, and caviar. My small toe had been bleeding for a few days. The hostess came in while I was inspecting it and said,
‘I hab a band-age por you’. We watched her feed her son three bowls of ice cream with some alarm.
‘He lub ice cream,’ she beamed.
Next morning I skittered past the family as they were having breakfast en route to the bathroom. We decided to join them so as not to seem rude and chatted to Øyvind about overpopulation, ships,the reason for Norway's wealth, good food and coffee although not necessarily in that order. He stayed home to look after his son while Rosella worked. An arrangement that seemed to work well for them.
We took a train to Rådhuset, stopping at the Vikingskipshuset first to look at three Viking boats they'd dragged out of the mud, all beautifully preserved. These three intact Viking ships are the Oseberg, Tune, and Gokstad. They were apparently used for burying the dead, after which the boats were sent off and sunk. Included with the bodies were shields, helmets and other items of importance. These ships are three of the best-preserved Viking ships in the world and considered crucial symbols of the Viking Age.
We were hungry after this and stopped at a quaint little wooden cafe-cum-restaurant called Hjemme Hos just down the street from the museum.
It was a balmy 20°C and a pleasant walk to the folk museum (Norwegian Museum of Cultural History), which focused on the 1500s until the present time. The exhibits featured folk costumes, folk art, church art, and Sami culture. In 1903, the museum acquired a farmhouse known as a barfrøstue, as well as a summer cabin and a stave church from Gol. A stave church I learned, is a medieval wooden Christian church building characterised by its unique post and lintel construction where load-bearing posts (or staves) are a key feature. These churches are found primarily in Scandinavia, especially Norway and are notable for their intricate timber framing. The wood of this particular stave church was darkened from hundreds of years of weathering.
It was an extensive walk around this huge outdoor museum, and I marvelled at the various architectural designs of Viking times. Grass-covered huts stood near old wooden school buildings, carved wooden doors and houses were built on wooden structures that helped prevent mice and rats from attacking their supply of grain and dried meat. In between the houses, which were lifted exactly as was from their places of origin, were signs of spring: yellow daffodils, bluebells, and snowdrops. Melting snow lay in patches near the stables containing pigs, chickens and cows.
 The garden was full of people enjoying the sunny day with their families and friends. I bought Tim a spicy sausage and coffee for me and sat chatting for a while before  taking the metro to a nearby ski lift. Earlier we'd passed the American Embassy and flags were flying at half-mast. I later heard Barbara Bush had died that morning. A marathon of sorts was taking place so most of the road was closed to traffic and runners and spectators lined the streets. A very long outdoor market with stalls sold everything from clothes and food to bric-a-brac. It seemed the entire population of Oslo had turned out to participate in some way.
While sitting around the kitchen table having tea, Øyvind returned from shopping. He insisted on making us breakfast which saved us some ‘penge’, so we thanked him and chatted as he cooked up a delicious meal of bacon, eggs and toast on his convection stove. After brekky we set off for Jernbanetorget via the metro. There was quite a bit of snow on the ground as I gazed from the train window before leaving the warmth of the train into the cold, crisp air of Holmenkollen Park. This is an old ski slope and in the melting snow we came across discarded gloves and two wine glasses.
Tim and I tried out the ski simulator which was fun.  Later we found a hill that still had a lot of snow clinging to its slopes and felt compelled, as one does, to make snowballs and pelt each other with them. I fashioned a woman out of snow then having done my bit in support of equality, walked to a ski lift chair and took a seat. I imagined how it would feel to have it moving when the air and ground were thick with snow and shivered.
A couple of guys were abseiling from the top of the ski slope. After watching them for some minutes, we wandered over to one of the huge wooden restaurants for lunch. Their prices were intergalactic so we just had coffee and wandered off still hungry back to the train.
A more affordable lunch was had at a pavement cafe called Laundromat Cafe and I idly wondered if they washed the salad and spin dried the eggs. They had an English-speaking waiter which made ordering a little easier. I had eggs Benedict that didn’t appear to have been through any washing cycle. Afterwards we walked to a nearby tram and rode to the old part of the city. I noticed that most of the cars were quiet and clean. Most of the city drives electric vehicles and Norway comes first in many energy-saving measures. I saw recharging posts everywhere to keep up with the demand I suppose and many colourful old buildings which made for a few good photos. We heard from Øyvind that the guests who were supposed to arrive at 7 p.m. had already arrived, so we'd packed and left our luggage with him.
We returned to find two young Chinese girls sitting in the kitchen. One of the girls was upset because she’d lost her passport — a traveller’s nightmare but I got the feeling Norway would sort it out efficiently. Øyvind arrived as I was making tea, so we chatted in the kitchen a while before Tim and I gathered our luggage, thanked him for his hospitality and prepared to take our heavy cases down four flights of stairs.

## Sweden

We left the bus and headed on up to Sturegatan where Anne-Sofie lived. It was lovely to see her again and we sat in the familiar, dusty pink lounge and caught up ( dusty as in the colour- the apartment was very clean and tidy).
We met up with Dan again as he was also visiting Sweden. He suggested we attend a small exhibition called Paper Stories. The exhibits were made from paper that was cut to form items such as books to life-sized models. They had a Japanese collection on another floor. A very interesting hour was passed wandering around the long rooms before leaving Dan at his hostel, just down the road. Outside, the sun was shining and cartoon clouds sat motionless in a blue sky. 
Tim and I walked down to Centralen and collected Dan for a walk to Urban Deli for lunch. We walked up to the marketplace to join Anne-Sofie. So much colourful fruit was on offer — dark blackberries nestled against plump juicy strawberries, unblemished bright yellow bananas smiled playfully at shiny red apples.
It started to rain so took shelter in the Town Hall restaurant before walking around Stadshuset where we joined a tour guide with a large group. This made it difficult to hear anything he said so we sloped off at one point and headed for the City Hall to see what was on offer. The hall was completed in 1923 and boasts stunning stained glass features, intricate mosaic floors and a spectacular marble staircase. For the best city view, one may climb the 106-metre-tall tower (an elevator takes you about halfway up) — in this case ‘one’ decided not to.
Anne-Sofie told us that the hall was used to cater for Nobel Prize dinners (one of which she'd attended and she spoke of the lavish dinners and esteemed guests she’d met). There are high, wooden-beamed ceilings, beautiful tapestries, walls covered in paintings and a fantastic gold mosaic room.
In Gamla Stan we were just in time to see the changing of the guard, or rather the passing of the parade as it is called there; beautifully turned-out white horses with soldiers in their distinctive light blue and gold jackets marched stiffly up and down outside the palace. This was followed by lunch in the old city — I had blood pudding, lingonberries and apple, which was surprisingly delicious. The boys had sausage and mash. Next was Kungsträdgården to look at the cherry blossoms. They were so beautiful but unfortunately for us, hundreds of others thought so too which made for difficult photography. Free drinks were provided at a busy stall before walking past the palace again back to Gamla Stan. Over old pebble stone streets before passing one of the narrowest streets I have ever seen; stone steps leading down between two tall buildings with barely one person’s width between them.

Tim left Sweden to continue his travels. Anne-Sofie and I walked into town. The cherry blossoms were out in full bloom and the sun had warmed the buds sufficiently to unfurl their glorious pink petals. We watched the parade in honour of King Carl’s 73rd birthday, arriving too late for the first bit but in time to see the Royal family on the balcony; King Carl, Queen Silvia and their daughter, Princess Victoria. I was craning my neck and asked who the lady in blue was and a woman in front of me turned round, eyes shining with excitement and pride and said,

‘That’s our Princess, Victoria!’

Annie drove us all the way out to Solna, Slotsallen to watch the ‘big bonfire’, (known as Valborgsmässoafton / Walpurgis) in celebration of the beginning of spring. Valborgsmässoafton is held on April 30 every year to mark the beginning of spring in Sweden. People were gathered around bonfires traditionally believed to ward off evil spirits and to salute the new season with joyful singing.
It was very cold as the sun had already set so we sat in the car. The Scouts lit the bonfire at around 8:45 and we extricated ourselves from the warmth of the car and wandered over to the fire. Only one half of it burned but it was a lovely warming sight standing around watching the dancing golden flames light up the sky.
