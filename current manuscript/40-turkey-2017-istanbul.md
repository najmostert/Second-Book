# Chapter 37: Turkey 2017: Istanbul

It was almost my birthday; a significant birthday — so significant I decided to leave the country. I felt Cuba was far enough away. We were flying with Turkish Airlines.
Unfortunately this trip was marred with the admission of John to hospital with some unidentifiable problem that was causing him to 'black out' suddenly in his wheelchair. A frightening experience for both of us. By the time I left they had identified a respiratory infection and he was on strong antibiotics. I had hired the assiatnce of two male carers by this time, one of whom was living with us and they reassured me they would keep me informed of his progress. Nevertheless I left for the airport with a heavy heart with Tim. 
I managed to fall asleep for four hours and only my full bladder woke me. I returned to my seat smelling strongly of lemon spritzer and settled down for another snooze. I was just about to re-attach my eye mask when suddenly all the lights went on and the sound of the drinks trolley started rumbling softly down the aisle. I glanced at my watch and saw it was 4 a.m. and felt mildly irritated as I could easily have slept a further few hours. In retrospect, this would also have better prepared me for what was to come.
We arrived at Istanbul airport and marched straight to the Airport Information desk to register for the free city tour that the airline provides to travellers with a late connecting flight. At the appointed hour we were met by a  local woman wearing a blue headress who read out our names from within the small group that was to accompany us. We almost missed our names as she asked whether ‘Timot V R Mos’ and ‘Janeth’ were present. We claimed the titles anyway and walked with her and eight others towards an awaiting bus that drove us first to the Blue Mosque via JF Kennedy Road.
After leaving the bus, we followed our tour guide around streets featuring colourful wooden houses in yellows, blues, reds, and greens. We were taken to see the tall Walled Obelisk; a Roman monument located in the former Hippodrome of Constantinople built in the 10th century AD.
Our guide said we would have a free breakfast and led us to ‘Turkistan Asevi’; an authentic Turkish restaurant where the group was seated at long wooden tables for plates of boiled eggs, olives, tomatoes, cucumber and cheese with bread and water. It was a bit bland but not bad for a free meal provided by the airline.
The ‘Blue Mosque’ is actually the Sultan Ahmed Mosque. Unfortunately, Sultan Ahmed the first had not spent much time inside his mosque as he died soon after it opened which is why I don’t believe in planning too far ahead; I could get trampled by an elephant and have waited for nothing. Not that I was anywhere close to having a monument built for me; anyhow his tomb lay on the north side of the mosque, facing Sultanahmet Park.
It had a stunning interior lined with over 20,000 handmade ‘Iznik tiles’ turquoise in colour. Iznik was an Anatolian town famous for its traditional pottery.
 The upper levels of the mosque were also painted blue. There were more than fifty different tulip designs in the same blue tile. The Blue Mosque was the only mosque in Istanbul with six minarets. When it was first built, this provoked hostility because at that time, Mecca was the only temple with six minarets. Many in the Muslim community saw it as disrespectful to the Kaaba. To make amends, Sultan Ahmed I paid for a seventh minaret for the mosque in Mecca, which went some way in appeasing the people.
Afterwards we walked to Haseki Hürrem Hamami; a 16th-century Turkish bath. This building was designed in the classic Ottoman baths style, with two symmetrical separate sections for males and females. Both sections, situated in a north–south direction, are on the same axis; a novelty in Turkish bath architecture at the time.
Many shops sold a variety of touristy things as we passed the Bab-i-Hümayun, the walls of Constantinople, Fatih Topkapı Palace (which had some beautiful mosaics laid in sumptuous colours of reds and golds, many patterned tiles, and marble floors), and Kalenderhane Mosque. Afterwards the guide took us to the office to buy tickets and we entered the Topkapı Sarayı Museum. From the 1460s to the completion of Dolmabahçe Palace in 1856, it served as the administrative centre of the Ottoman Empire and was the main residence for its sultans.
Topkapı Palace Museum was one of the largest palace museums of the world with 300,000 archive documents, spectacular collections consisting of Ottoman arms and instruments, an invaluable treasure like the Spoonmaker’s Diamond; a priceless 86-carat, pear-shaped diamond and sacred relics.
The smell of spices assailed our nostrils as we approached a large marketplace where I bought soft dates.
As the call for boarding boomed over the speakers, we passed through passport control, received our gate passes and sat in the departure lounge until 1 a.m, both exhausted having had an intense day of touring after a long flight. As we shuffled up to the gate, passports and tickets in our hands, I whispered to Tim that I was really looking forward to having a good sleep on board. The flight attendent held out her hand and I gave her my passport. She frowned and asked me to stand aside. Tim handed her his passport and boarding pass but the same thing happened.  We were not sure what we were waiting for. Eventually, the line of passengers thinned out, then another couple were pulled aside and stood nervously with us. When the last passenger had walked through, she turned to us and asked where our ‘Travel Cards’ were. We all looked at each other.
‘We have British passports,’ I ventured, ‘We don’t need visas.’
She shook her head.
‘Not visa, Travel Card!’ We consulted with each other quickly but none of us had even heard of the term before, never mind being in possession of one.
‘Where can we get one here?’ I asked anxiously.
‘You have to return to your country,’ she replied unhelpfully and I stared at her in disbelief.
I pulled Tim aside and whispered that we should go and find someone more helpful and walked as fast as we could to the other side of the terminus to find a help desk. They just pointed to another area further along and we entered an office with four officials sitting on swivel chairs drinking coffee. They looked up as we entered.
‘Excuse me,’ I said, ‘Please tell us what a travel card is and where we can quickly get one. Our plane is going to leave soon and we need one now.’
They all shrugged, saying they didn't know. Tim suggested we go to the check-in counter and ask there. We had no luck there either. I was frantic by now and shouted at the official,
‘Who can help us? We were not told about this thing anywhere, but we need one now!’
At that moment the loudspeaker announced the departure of Flight XYZ to Cuba. Tim and I looked at each other and it dawned on us that this was really happening. The holiday I had paid mucho pesos for to celebrate my significant birthday was just not going to happen. I was devastated. It was now 1:45 a.m. and we were beyond tired.
‘Let’s just go and find a coffee shop to sit down and try to work this out,’ suggested Tim with his usual calm manner.
 After retrieving our luggage which had been booted off the plane (I bet the other passengers were pleased as this would naturally cause a delay), we walked around  for a coffee shop where we could get Wi-Fi to rebook a flight, as well as inform various luxury resorts that we were not going to be coming to Cuba after all.
After walking around for twenty minutes we realised nothing was open so took ourselves and our baggage outside the airport in search of a hotel. After trying a few places without success we had to admit defeat and trudged back into the airport—through X-rays again. Eventually, Tim and I sank into some seats at the Take Off Cafe. It was closed too, so no Wi-Fi until it opened at 5 a.m. We sat in shock and discussed some other possibilities. Eventually, there were some stirrings at the cafe; chairs were being pulled out and doors were opening. We immediately ordered a coffee and asked for the Wi-Fi password. Tim set to working out where we could go. ‘We’re definitely not flying home!’ he said firmly.
I sent messages via my phone to all the places in Cuba that I had booked and to the airline asking for reimbursement. I had messaged the carer and heard that John was still in ICU but was responding well to antibiotics and physio. I was still so worried about him and asked to be informed timeously should his position change.
At around 6:30 a.m. Tim looked up from his laptop and said,
‘Okay, Mother. We’re going to book into the Hotel Sultania here in Turkey tonight, then we’re leaving for Croatia tomorrow night.’
I smiled tiredly at my wonderful son and thanked him for sorting it all out. We left the airport and waited for a taxi and I told Tim I'd tried to cancel accommodation and get a refund before our driver roared into view, but so far no one was willing to do that.
Here’s a fun poem Tim wrote about being denied access to a plane to Cuba late at night in Istanbul and deciding to travel to Croatia instead:

The Midnight Plan in Istanbul

The night is dark, the hour late,
As we arrive at the airport’s gate.
But Cuba’s flight; it slips away,
Our plans dissolved, no trip today.
In Istanbul, we stand forlorn
We need a new plan to form
And then a thought sparks within the daze,
“Why not Croatia’s coastal bays?”

The Hotel Sultania was a vision of elegant restraint amid the crowded concrete jungle. They graciously stowed our luggage away until our room was ready, allowing us to explore. Craving another caffeine fix, we hit the streets in search of a local cafe for fortification.
After coffee and breakfast we walked around Istanbul again, retracing some steps from the day before but at our own pace before returning to the room for a much-needed nap; it had been well over forty-eight hours since we had experienced any kind of sleep. Feeling a little better, we wandered around the streets looking for a good place for supper and found a lovely patio spot overlooking the city, called The Olive Restaurant. As we sat there, the call to prayer rent the air with its eastern mystery, reminding me of Saudi Arabia. The waiter was friendly and presented our food with a theatrical tremble of the lid before revealing our food.
Afterwards, we walked around Fatih  (I pictured walking around a person of excess adipose tissue), looking at interesting and sometimes ancient buildings such as Hagia Sophia and the Hacı Beşir Ağa Mosque, with its characteristic white stone interlaced with flat red bricks. I purchased tickets for a sunset cruise along the Bosphorus Sea.
It was a pleasant experience, sailing smoothly over the darkening water until sunset which, although not quite spectacular, was an interesting orange-yellow. Various landmarks passed by as we traversed the smooth waves, such as the New Mosque with its twin turrets, Hagia Sophia, the square Beylerbeyi Palace, the Bloomberg Museum and the beautiful Ortaköy Mosque. It was quite chilly though and neither of us was dressed warmly. After passing under a long red bridge known as the Bosphorus Bridge, we arrived back at the dock and began walking around Sultanahmet Park and its surrounds.  Lunch was at the Sultania where we chatted about our imminent flight to Croatia. I found a wall along the path decorated with broken pottery using large pieces so a quarter of a cup with a handle stuck out of the cement in 3D. We wandered past buildings that were painted in deep pinks, aquamarines and yellows. Colour abounded bright and cheerful. After check-out, the receptionist gave each of us a beautifully embroidered purse with the legend ‘Sultania Hotel’. I used mine for many years for my drawing pens. We headed back to the airport.
